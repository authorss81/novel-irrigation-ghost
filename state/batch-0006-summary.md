## What the batch proves

1. **Volume 01's closing question is answered from the village's side and it is the Guild's side at the same time, and the answer is not a conspiracy.** A village two days west has been taking water out of a channel the Guild's own engineer certified *solid to the rod at both ends and to remain so* eight years ago. The channel is a rising main. The water comes **out of the crown joint of a silted six-inch pipe and goes over the side of it**, eleven feet uphill, and a rod at the low end cannot find that, and a good return is therefore true. **The mechanism is a shut flume sluice, a full tank, a weeping joint and a gravel bed, and the only document any of it produced in six years is a road notice nailed to a stake with nobody's name on it, in a ninety-year-old frame, referring to a gate that was shut a year before the notice was written.**
2. **The Reach's problem is not scarcity and has not been for six years.** The Sallow Head tank is six thousand gallons of room, it is at the same mark to within a quarter of an inch, and six years of tank card in two men's hands go to Vellum once a year in a cart that nobody has read. A tank that is fed, is full, and has one outlet is not a tank that is being emptied. **It is being passed through, at about 850 gallons an hour, into a drain.**
3. **A route name is a negotiated address and the batch says so and then proves it the hard way.** Tarin Rusk proposes *the Sallow* in a room over a shop with the doors open and forty-one people in it, and **a village refuses an outsider's route name, in daylight, in its own hand, and it is entered in a book**, and the refusal costs a woman of fifty-four twenty-six years of work because a book of customs with a refusal in it is a record. The word means four incompatible things and **all four are true and none of the four is anybody's fault.**
4. **The batch refuses the cheap victory twice and says why both times.** The waste pipe is found, and he puts his hand on it, and he does not open it, and he asks for it written down. And the Sere oath is read aloud on a bank, and Perdie Ammid refuses to have it read in an assembly, and he does not override her, **and the reader is told on the page that this is the third of three things he has decided to keep rather than give away, and that this is the flaw and it is expensive.**
5. **Two people who have never met measure the same water from opposite ends and get numbers four and a half times apart, and the chapter that shows the gap does not close it and refuses to write *no loss* anywhere.** 850 against 190. The difference, 660 gallons an hour, is in the ground under a lane, and a man with a rule and a string can put a bound on a difference and not a place on a gallon.
6. **The volume's thesis is spoken by a farmer, not by the protagonist.** *A name is a way of being able to sue somebody, and a village with no name lost that in 1959 and got used to it, and I am not going to be talked out of the only piece of equipment this room owns by a man who has been here four days.* And by a woman of seventy-one: *when you take a word a people have used since before anybody's grandmother and hang it on somebody else's thing, you have taken the thing that made them a people and made them a row.*
7. **The protagonist is the worst man in the shed and the volume says so for six hours.** Twenty-two feet of gate bed at Culm, thirty-four degrees, nine feet in the first hour, and a bar taken off him and a shovel handed over without a word; and a nineteen-year-old boy saying *you've stopped, because your hand went.* **He is not a sluicewright and does not become one. He is the man the crew fetches when a number has to be believed**, and the crew's one stated reason is given once, on the flags, and is never repeated.
8. **The relationship milestone for the volume is on the page and it is not a romance.** Four minutes in a room, a figure struck off a card, a woman agreeing with a record office against her own village in nine words, and the disagreement written in two hands on a page of somebody else's book with the fifth condition satisfied and nothing withdrawn. **No warmth was manufactured and none existed.**

## The arithmetic, all of it on the page and all of it derived

- **The tank: 6,000 gallons of room.** 20 ft × 15 ft = 300 sq ft. 2 ft 8 in = 800 cu ft. **A foot of water is seven and a half gallons in this basin, so 800 × 7½ = 6,000.** The sum is done out loud twice by Margit Saye and a third time by a boy of fifteen. The mark on the wall is at 2 ft 6¼ in; the water on the thirtieth was at 2 ft 6⅛ in; six years of card show a variation under a quarter of an inch.
- **The chamber: nineteen feet deep, silt nine feet two inches.** The card's *silt 2 ft 4* is the layer in the outlet channel below the sill, which cannot be scraped because the pipe runs in it. **A chamber 228 inches deep with 118 inches of silt in it has not filled, and there is no line for it anywhere, because nobody has been inside since the sixth year of the standard.**
- **The throat: 860, then 833, and the printed figure is 850.** A slot two inches wide by an inch and a half is **three square inches, which is a forty-eighth of a square foot**, and that is the working as she gives it. A lath twenty feet in **thirteen seconds** is 20 ÷ 13 = **1.538 ft/s**; ÷ 48 = **0.03204, which is a shade UNDER a thirty-first** (1/31 = 0.03226); a cubic foot is seven and a half gallons; that is **14.42 gallons a minute and 865 gallons an hour**, while a full thirty-first would be **14.52 and 871** — **the chapter prints both, so the reader can see that 860 is not the textbook figure rounded but the instrument's, and that Margit Saye says so out loud.** A lath twenty feet in **thirteen and a half seconds** on the first of next month, in the same slot, unaltered, is 1.481 ft/s, and that is **833.** The two are three per cent apart. **The printed figure is the middle, 850, and Tarin Rusk's stated reason for a dull number is that a man with two live charges will not be able to defend an extreme figure in three years.** Margit Saye makes a boy of nineteen do it a third time on the back of his own hand with a nail. **The two trials are three per cent apart and the figure the Reach prints is the middle one, and the card says in ink that it is not a figure anybody should lean on.**
- **The plank: 193 and 191.** Six gallons in 1 min 52 s = 193.9 → 193; in 1 min 53 s = 191.2 → 191. **One second is about five gallons an hour and Nesta Sarg has refused for forty years to put a figure in a book that cannot be got to the right gallon in a second.**
- **850 against 190 is four and a half times; the difference is 660 gallons an hour and it is in the ground.** **The same subtraction on Friday's one-trial-at-each-end figures, 860 against 193, is 667, and both sums are on the printed card with the reason they differ — a man who keeps the one that flatters him is a man with a reason.** 190 is Nesta Sarg's figure and the two trials are printed underneath it, so the headline comparison does not rest on an unprinted number. The drain is 2 ft 2 in wide with 1½ in of water in the middle, is unlined, is on gravel, and the water table is about a foot below the invert.
- **The taken-figure, 38 an hour.** A sixty-gallon barrel filled in an hour and thirty-five minutes, one round trip in two hours, and the cart goes on every morning but Sunday's. 60 ÷ 1.583 = 37.9. **In Nesta Sarg's own mouth in Chapter 54 it is forty-two churns of thirty gallons since the first of the month, two a morning, over twenty-one mornings in a month with three Sundays in it, and forty-four by the second of next month.** It is a habit and it comes out of a habit and it is struck off a printed card in Chapter 60 by the woman who wrote it.
- **860 an hour is 20,640 a day and 7,533,600 a year.** Checked in Chapter 59.
- **The Reach's own figure: 94,500 a season, a fifth of it 18,900, over four days 4,725 a household a day, 290 households at one fifth 5,481,000 for the season.** All in Chapter 59, all from a locked press at Culm in a hand that is a copy of a hand two centuries older.
- **The head at the Sallow Head is `7·31` metres, spoken *seven point three one*, and nobody measures it in this volume.** The split `7·31` / *seven point three one* is kept exactly as Volume 01 kept `5·89` / *five point eight nine`, **and both halves are now on the page: `7·31` is the third line of the 1902 printed slip at Brant and is the only one of the three lines the later ink did not touch, and *seven point three one* is said by Tarin Rusk in Chapter 55 and by Jory Vant in Chapter 56, who stops and says it again because he cannot say it the way it is written.**
