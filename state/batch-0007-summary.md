## What the batch proves

1. **The weep is a break and not an overflow, and there is nobody to catch.** Four miles up the Kerrow valley above the lime kiln, on the same main, a dry-stone conduit laid in 1843 is weeping out of a **cross joint in the crown, four and a half inches across**, with a second weep eighteen inches above it and two courses open above that, and a **lime rind on the lip that is the only thing standing between that joint and a face of stone the size of a doorway.** The bank on the west side of the road is soft in a drought because a bar goes into it eighteen inches and comes up wet. **A man in a field says there is nobody to catch and a man at a fence line writes it on a slate.** The volume's crisis is on the page and it is a hundred and eighty-one years old and it is structural.
2. **The road is the evidence, and it is the cheapest thing in the batch.** A man of sixty-one has kept a book at a toll bar for forty years that is not a toll book, **he will not read it out and he will answer if he is asked**, and Tarin Rusk asks him four questions, one at a time, on a Friday morning, and gets: no inspection on a Guild road in six years, eleven things up the road that were not lime, a man with a rod eight years ago, and **a man with a chain and a padlock and a card at ten minutes past two in the afternoon six years ago.** The thesis of the volume is in that object and the road man says it better than the protagonist does: *a route is a road with somebody at every end of it who has to be answerable for it, and the looking and the asking are the two things that are not a mechanic.*
3. **The amendment is in the Reach's own copy of its own schedule, in a chest at Brant, in a hand nobody in the Reach can read.** A keeper's name struck out in the sixth year and *supply district 14, North Sallow* written in its place, lawful, six years old, and **Margit Saye reads the hand and will not say a man's name in a room.** The chapter names **four documents for one reach** and says that three of them are in one village and one is in a town and one is in a shed and none of them knows the other three exist.
4. **Yara Sen comes west for four days for the fourth line of a letter that was not about evidence**, and gives the Reach a boiled-jar test that costs a pint, a jar, a ring and an hour, and **refuses to give a number, and the number is the whole of what the village wants** — and the number she will not certify is not a safety figure but a sufficiency figure: 850 an hour for ten days is 204,000 gallons, which is **a hundred and twenty gallons an acre**, and a hundred and twenty gallons an acre is not a crop.
5. **THE COUNT, on Sunday the fourteenth, at the plank and at the tank and at the flume sluice, comes out short and is published short.** Four hundred people in a yard at the shop at Brant. **The third rod has nothing in it, and the figure is not zero**, and the reason printed under the hole is a padlock, and **Perdie Ammid says so in the yard without being asked.**
6. **THE WINDOW, on Monday the fifteenth, opens lawfully and is stopped at about an hour and forty minutes by a woman standing in a long drain with the Reach's own third book in her hand**, invoking item 1 of the Reach's schedule of 1902, which forbids any other discharge into the long drain. **Eight hundred and fifty an hour for one hour and forty minutes is 1,417 gallons** and it went into a drain two miles below the plank, and **Tarin Rusk had been in the Reach ten days and had not asked for that book**, and Perdie Ammid says the sentence the volume exists for: *you did not come here for a witness. You came here for a rod.*

## The arithmetic, all of it on the page and all of it derived

- **The weep, 2 gallons twice.** 2 gal in **1 min 20 s** = **90 gallons an hour**; 2 gal in **1 min 21 s** = 88.9, written **89**. Both printed. A hat-and-watch figure struck out on the boy's own hand on the Wednesday and **not printed**, because a figure struck out on a boy's hand is not a figure. **Ninety is about a tenth of what the stone throat gives at the bottom of the hill, and the difference is not a measurement of anything.**
- **The tank on the fourteenth: two feet six and a quarter inches, twice**, against a mark cut in the wall with a chisel and no date on it, and an eighth of an inch above the lowest figure in six years, and **an eighth of an inch is the whole of six years' variation.** Margit Saye: *if it were a foot I would be writing a different word.*
- **The plank on the fourteenth: 193 and 191, again**, off six gallons in 1 min 52 s and 1 min 53 s, and the figure is 190, and **Nesta Sarg says out loud that the same two numbers to the second, three weeks on, is a count that is worth more than one that comes out different, and that a different figure would have gone for first.**
- **The flume launder.** Six inches wide, about three-quarters of an inch deep, **four and a half square inches, which is a thirty-second of a square foot**, and a cubic foot is seven and a half gallons, and at exactly a foot a second that is **eight hundred and forty-four gallons an hour.** Jory Vant times it at twenty feet in twenty seconds, which is exactly a foot a second, and Margit Saye says a stone launder does not go at exactly a foot a second and uses the figure anyway, **because a Reach that lets a fitter substitute her own figure is a Reach that has two figures, and that is where this whole business started.**
- **The window: 850 × 1 hour 40 minutes = 1,417 gallons.** Done once, out loud, on the flags outside the shed. **The sum uses 850 and not 844, and the reason is given on the page.**
- **1,417 against the fourth book's 4 gallons a household a day for 290 households = 1,160 a day** is a day and a fifth of the Reach's own household drinking, **and it went down a flume and into a long drain.**
- **Yara Sen's sum, once:** 850 an hour for ten days is 204,000 gallons, and for 1,700 acres that is **a hundred and twenty gallons an acre.**
- **Nine inches of head.** The flume's own invert at the sluice is **nine inches below the tank's mark on the wall** — found by a boy of fifteen with a hand in a dry launder and a string across it, not by a fitter with a level. Two inches on a smooth launder would be enough; nine on a stone one a hundred and eighty-one years old is what a fitter asks for. **This is why the flume can be dry while the tank is full, and it is a fact about the flume, not a surprise.**
- **The run of eighteen minutes** over two miles and four chains, a shade under eight minutes a mile, is stated out loud as a thing that will be asked about, and Wick Venn has said in advance on the Sunday night that the boy can do it in twenty and will not stop.
