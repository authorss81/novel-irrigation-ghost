# World: The Marrow Basin

## Shape of the region

The Marrow Basin is a high, bowl-shaped country around the **Sere River**. Wet air rises against the **Thorn Crown** mountains in the north and east, while the basin's center sits behind a line of low chalk ridges. In ordinary years, runoff descends from the mountains, gathers in a chain of reservoirs, and is lifted through old stone stairways to the cities and farms between them. The basin is not naturally empty. Its wealth comes from the difficult work of moving water uphill and deciding who may use it.

The principal places in the story are:

- **Cresset:** A dense city of pump houses, slate roofs, and terraced cisterns built around the dry center of the basin. Cresset's Water Office maintains the old aqueducts, but the city council has spent decades treating vanished routes as administrative errors rather than physical losses.
- **Sallow Reach:** A patchwork of farms, orchards, wind pumps, and canal villages west of Cresset. Its fields depend on mountain runoff and on informal agreements between families that predate the current kingdoms.
- **Meltwater Verge:** The low, fertile floodplain downstream of the Sere. It receives Cresset's overflow and the basin's floodwater. It is not a safe alternative to drought; moving water there can kill crops, homes, and children.
- **Vellum:** The old administrative and record city on a dry central terrace. Its courts, archive vaults, and stone record houses hold the legal version of nearly every settlement in the basin.
- **Thorn Crown:** High source country, cloud forest, and stone terraces. Its mountain communities know the weather in ways the lowland offices do not, but they have little power over the legal allocation of their runoff.
- **Pale Teeth:** A salt basin of dry lakebeds, buried aqueducts, and ruins from the former Orisene water empire. The salt hides old routes and makes accidental water-opening especially dangerous.
- **Glassstep:** A lower-Cresset neighborhood physically inhabited by people but officially absent from the municipal map. Its streets, homes, and small cisterns sit over a route that the One Map cannot openly acknowledge.
- **The Blind Reservoir:** A vast, partly natural and partly excavated chamber beneath the old Orisene works. It holds water that has been detached from its recognized routes. It is not an ocean or an unlimited reserve; it is a dangerous, unmapped pressure system with no ordinary outlet.

Travel between these places is possible by foot, horse, handcart, canal skiff, mountain mule, and maintained aqueduct. A journey can take days or weeks. Roads, bridges, pumps, and weather determine what is possible; a character cannot teleport simply because a route is magical.

## Historical foundation

### The Ninefold Flood

One hundred and eighty-one years ago, nine upstream gates were opened and closed in conflicting orders during a spring melt. The resulting flood cut through the Sere's middle basin, killed thousands, and erased several villages from the first official survey. The disaster was not caused by a monster or an inexplicable curse. It was caused by a political and engineering failure: each settlement believed a different gate order was legitimate, and the water obeyed none of them.

### The One Map Accord

After the flood, rulers, engineers, and record-keepers built a shared calibration lattice called **the One Map**. It connected major aqueducts, record stones, rain gauges, and copper address plates buried beside the physical gates. The plates translated an approved route name into a specific valve setting: a recognized inlet could open, while a conflicting name triggered a pressure-relief overflow toward a hidden sump. Its original purpose was practical: if every water order carried the same official name, no gate could claim that a disputed route was legitimate.

The builders believed that a single address would be safer than a thousand arguments. They did not account for communities that had survived by sharing water, keeping several versions of a boundary, or deliberately leaving a route unnamed. The lattice is not morally aware; clerks, engineers, and rulers decide which address is official, and the plates make that decision physical. The One Map began to remove those alternatives. The removals were initially called administrative corrections.

### The First Unlisting

Twenty-six years ago, after a political dispute over the old Glassstep terrace, the map authority erased Glassstep and seven neighboring villages from the public survey. Their residents were told to move for flood safety. The land was not physically removed, but their route names, land claims, and burial records were made mutually incompatible. Water that should have reached their homes began to collect in the Blind Reservoir instead.

The official history calls this a necessary correction. Families who remember the old streets call it the first **unlisting**. The disagreement is the moral center of the story.

## Water infrastructure

Cresset and the older basin cities use a mixture of ordinary engineering and remembered hydraulics:

- **Stone and ceramic aqueducts:** The oldest channels are lined with fired brick and lime. They are durable but vulnerable to settlement, salt, and sabotage.
- **Brass and copper survey gear:** Engineers use sight-lines, weighted tapes, pressure bells, float gauges, and hand-built water wheels. A good engineer can solve many problems without magic.
- **Sluice houses:** Each major route has a physical gate, a keeper, a maintenance log, and a local assembly that grants or refuses a service window. A route is never just a tunnel; it is a chain of people who can open, close, inspect, and question it.
- **Record stones:** Important turns and junctions are marked in stone with a route name, a date, and the names of witnesses. A record stone does not create magic. It keeps a route's address legible after rain, flood, or political change.
- **Household cisterns:** Every respectable home, school, clinic, and workshop has some stored water. During a severe drought, private cisterns become political facts. The rich can hoard; the poor can be blamed.
- **Rain glasses and wind vanes:** Forecasters read pressure, cloud behavior, and old weather sayings. Their skill is useful but not infallible. Magic can open an existing moisture corridor; it cannot order the sky to obey a timetable.
- **Calibration rain shutters:** Hinged catchment panels at high source stations, coupled to the One Map's copper address plates. They can divert a measured rain catchment toward a named outlet; a failed or unlisted plate sends overflow toward the Blind Reservoir.

The city's physical infrastructure is maintained by real work: wading into silt, replacing seals, recalibrating gates, repairing pumps, carrying tools, and arguing over money. A route can be technically open but socially closed, or socially defended but physically failing.

## Politics and economy

The basin is divided among several polities, but water crosses all of them. The five recognized water jurisdictions are the **City Republic of Cresset**, the **Sallow Reach Communes**, the **Thorn Crown terraces**, the **Meltwater Verge flood councils**, and **Vellum's Fair Copy jurisdiction**. Pale Teeth is a disputed ruin zone administered through temporary claims rather than a stable government. Cresset is a city republic in name and a water oligarchy in practice. The **Basin Assembly** can approve budgets and emergency service windows, but its members are selected unevenly and rural districts have limited representation. The **Meridian Compact** is a newer alliance of city-states, guilds, and source territories that claims to coordinate the whole basin. It controls the mountain gates and sells priority when water is scarce.

Water is not a simple coin. A community may hold a **route right**, meaning it has a recognized claim to be included when a route is named and a right to negotiate service and maintenance. A route right is not an absolute claim to unlimited water; it is a political promise that the community will be heard before the route is changed. Older villages often understand this better than the city offices.

The main economic pressure is predictable: dry years increase the value of pumps, records, guides, and political access. Contractors profit from temporary repairs. Guilds control specialized knowledge. Record offices control proof. A person who can read a vanished route is valuable, endangered, and easy to accuse of fraud.

## Peoples, practices, and ordinary life

The basin has many communities, but no single culture owns the water. City people live by bells, wages, school shifts, market queues, and public fountain hours. Sallow Reach farmers speak in field names and family obligations rather than cadastral parcels. Thorn Crown villages keep weather songs that are not merely decorative; they preserve the timing of reliable rain corridors. Meltwater Verge families build on raised stilts and mark flood years by the height of stains on their doors. Glassstep residents use household stories, songs, brass keys, and the positions of old pumps because official maps have failed them.

Religion is not organized around a single god. **Readers** are trained custodians who work with route memory, while **quiet houses** preserve memories that are too dangerous to speak openly. The Quiet Archive grew from these houses. Its members do not worship memory; they believe uncontrolled remembrance can be as destructive as forgetting.

No faction is simply good or evil. A guild can keep a channel alive while exploiting workers. A court can prevent a massacre while erasing a village. A family can hoard a cistern out of fear while still rescue a stranger. The story keeps those distinctions visible.

## Factions

### Cresset Water Office

The public engineering department responsible for pumps, gates, surveys, and emergency service. It is underfunded, politically compromised, and staffed by people who know the physical plant better than the council does. Tarin begins here. The Office is capable of great work when allowed to act locally, but its central records have been selectively purified.

### The Open Channel Guild

Contractors, sluicewrights, and mountain engineers who maintain the high gates. The Guild has real expertise and a habit of treating access to knowledge as a private asset. Its members can repair a route faster than the Water Office, but they also decide whose work is counted as skilled labor. The Guild is the first organized force that understands the magical rules and exploits them.

### Route Assemblies

Local councils of residents, farmers, fishers, schoolteachers, and caretakers who govern a named route. Their authority is uneven and often informal, but they are the institutional form the final solution will generalize. A route assembly can refuse a transfer, maintain a local cistern, and keep a public record. It cannot command weather or make a neighboring assembly accept its claims.

### The Quiet Archive

A network of vaults, copying houses, and trained readers that claims to protect communities from dangerous memory. It preserves genuine evidence and also removes counter-records that could trigger old conflicts. Its internal rule is that an unverified memory should never decide a public route. That rule is safer than rumor but has become a tool for quiet centralization.

### The Meridian Compact

A regional coalition controlling source-country gates, standards, and emergency allocation. It has prevented several wars and made some real infrastructure projects possible. Its leaders have also decided that a small number of privileged basins should receive water first so that famine deaths remain limited. The Compact is not a hidden monster. Its public leaders are accountable, and its internal debate is visible if someone knows where to look.

## The physical truth of the drought

The drought is real. Crops fail, reservoirs empty, wells become brackish, and bodies suffer from heat and dehydration. The false explanation is that the Basin's water has simply vanished. The hidden truth is that the One Map has been **unlisting** routes. When an address is removed, its copper plates close the ordinary inlet gates and open pressure-relief channels into the Blind Reservoir; the same calibration network misaligns high-altitude rain shutters, so a catchment's moisture is spilled into the same hidden sump instead of being available to local route keepers. No water is created or destroyed by the map. The surface appears dry because the physical flow has been made unreachable and misdirected.

This means every restoration has two effects: water returns to a community, and the political force that claimed control of that route must change. A physical victory can expose a theft, create a public assembly, or cause a flood downstream. The story treats water as shared infrastructure, not as a reward for the strongest magician.

## Reveal discipline

The following facts are withheld from the opening reader but are the planned foundation:

- Cresset's Ashgate Reservoir still exists below a later settlement.
- The ghost is an accumulation of unlisted routes, not a single dead person.
- The Blind Reservoir holds the missing water.
- The One Map was designed by people who remembered the Ninefold Flood.
- Boryn Vey quietly reactivated its newest function six years before the story and expanded the drought protocol afterward.
- Lira Rusk created the counter-map and did not survive to see the final restoration.
- The final answer is a distributed system, not a return of the old single map.
