# Chapter 0013 — The Shelf-Marker

Mara Quill came into the lower room on the morning of the twenty-third of the ration, did what she did every morning, which was to look at the nine slots, and found that there were eight.

She stood in the doorway with her sleeve rolled and a bundle of slips in her other hand and let her eye go along the rack.

The rack was made for eight. Folio nine had been in the ninth slot since the day the rack was built, on a *pending disposal* slip five and a half years old, and the crack across its diagonal went from the cut name to the worn grey nothing at the bottom, and it had lichen on it that had never had a chance to grow. It was where she had left it on the twenty-second and it was where it would be on the twenty-third and it was the dullest object in the building.

Folio eleven was not in the slot.

She did not touch anything. She looked at the shelf-marker — a small strip of gummed card in the slot, hand-lettered, as all the markers in the lower room were, because a printed marker cannot be reprinted and the Office had stopped printing them in the middle of the second year of the ration.

The marker said: **ASHGATE INTAKE, MAINTENANCE SERIES, FOLIO 11. WITHDRAWN. R. QUE.**

And a small strip under it in a different ink said **17/4**, which was the seventeenth of the fourth month, which was six days ago.

Mara Quill put the bundle of slips down on the bench, because her hands were doing something she had not authorised, and stood there, and then did the thing she had been trained since she was nineteen to do, which was to establish provenance before she had an opinion.

*Withdrawn.* Not *removed*. *Withdrawn* is a word with a technical meaning in a building like this one and it means the volume has left the working sequence and gone to controlled custody, which in the old usage is the strong room, which is two doors, two keys, and two names, and which nobody on this floor has opened for eleven years.

*R. Que.* Her own initials, in a hand that was not hers, in ink that was office ink.

*17/4.* Six days ago. On the seventeenth she had been in the low court of the Fair Copy at Vellum on a two-day collation of a boundary volume with two hundred and eleven leaves in it, and had walked out of the building at four on the seventeenth and gone home, and had come back on the nineteenth and worked two days, and had been in this room every working day since the nineteenth, at this bench, with the door open.

Six days. It had sat in the strong room for six days, and the man who had put it there had written her initials on the marker, which meant he had done it correctly, and the writing of her initials on the marker was the *only* part of the procedure that had touched her, and it was the part that had told her nothing at all.

---

She went up two floors and stood in the doorway of the reading supervisor's office for about four seconds before Hollis Kreft finished a sentence to a junior, and then she said, "Kreft. Folio eleven."

He put the pen down. He was fifty-four and had been in the building thirty-one years and he was not a man who enjoyed being interrupted and did not pretend to be.

"Restricted handling," he said. "Seventeenth. You were at the low court."

"You could have left the marker. You did leave the marker. The marker says *withdrawn* and it has my initials on it and I have had it in front of me every working day since the nineteenth."

"I did leave the marker. That is the marker." He turned his chair a quarter round, which was the whole of his body language. "A withdrawal is entered in the sequence register, which is on this desk, and the sequence register is not the place I go looking for a person's attention. If I have to come and find eleven people every time a contested volume is taken into custody, then a contested volume is going to sit on the open shelf for a year because nobody can find a moment to walk round a floor."

"You took a volume into the strong room on the seventeenth without telling the only person who had been working on it for a fortnight."

"I did."

"In a fortnight it has become a public document that this office holds an uncertified substituted leaf in the Ashgate series, and that the series is under restriction, and that there is therefore a substituted leaf. You have published the finding without the name on it."

Kreft looked at her for slightly longer than he usually did, and she understood, with a small cold drop, that he had got there first and had decided to let her get there at her own speed.

"Yes," he said. "I have."

---

The argument went for twenty minutes and it was, in the end, entirely one-sided, because he was right.

"This office's standing rule is that an unverified record may not decide a public route," he said. "It is rule nine of the reading rules and it has been the rule since the year I came in here, and it is the single most sensible thing this building owns. There is a leaf in a book that a conservator will not certify, and the leaf says a head of five point eight nine at a gate that the same book records as shut. If a man with a bar and no authority takes that number out of this building and puts it in a district, then a hundred and twenty-odd people are drinking off a head that nobody in this building has ever laid an instrument against. That is not a technicality. That is people being ill."

"I know that," said Mara.

"Then you know I did the rule."

"You did the rule. You did the rule and you read a catalogue out to a public board in the reading room at ten past eight this morning, and the catalogue is in a corridor anybody walks down, and it says *contains one substituted leaf, uncertified*, in the Office's own hand, and the Office has never had a press notice and does not need one."

"I did that too."

"Why."

"Because it is true, and because a secret in a restricted stack is a thing that only this building knows, and in about four months somebody in the commissioner's registry is going to be writing a line that says the Office has had a contested folio in hand since the ninth day of the month and has not reported it." He squared a stack of slips. "I would rather be the building that published a discrepancy than the building that concealed one. Concealment is what got the last three of my predecessors moved, and I am not going to be the fourth, and if I am stood in front of an assembly and asked, I would rather hold up a catalogue with the date on it than hold up nothing."

Mara Quill sat down, because she was going to be there a while.

"You have taken the only durable thing I had and published it," she said. "I want to be exact, because I am not accusing you of anything. Before the seventeenth there was a discrepancy in a requisition book in my own hand, and a page in a volume, and a number. That is a fact three people could have raised. From the seventeenth there is a *catalogue entry with a date*, in the Office's hand, asserting that a substituted leaf exists in the Ashgate series. That is now the most durable evidence in the basin, and it is evidence of nothing except that a man in Vellum thinks a page was replaced."

"That is what the page was."

"It is what the page *is* to anybody who is not me," she said, "and I am the only person in this building who can tell the difference between a substituted leaf and a badly bound one, and you have just made the difference unarguable to a hundred and fifty readers and unarguable in exactly the wrong direction."

Kreft turned the chair a quarter turn back.

"Then go and get a second one," he said. "Independently. Somewhere it is not this building and not me. Find the same hand, the same substitution, the same office, in a document this office has never touched, and carry it to the requisition book, and then you will have a pattern and a pattern is a different animal from a discrepancy."

Mara Quill did not answer, and he looked at her for a moment longer.

"That was not a stupid question," he said. "That was a test, and you will not want to tell me the answer, and I am not asking you for it, and I want that on the record as a thing I did deliberately. A thing that costs you, if you had given it to me, is not a thing I am going to take by asking twice."

---

She did not sleep much that night, and the thing she could not get past was the durability of it.

It was not nerve. She had known what she was doing on the ninth of the month when she drew a one-to-one key of two documents in her own ink in a locked lower room with a green lamp over the flame, and she had chosen on purpose, out loud, in her own head, to be the only person in the basin who could read the evidence on condition that nobody could check her. She had gone into that trade with her eyes open and she had not been frightened.

What she could not get past was that the trade had a term, and the term was the seventeenth.

A drawing in one woman's hand, in one woman's ink, in one woman's drawer, read by one woman's eye, is not a record. It is a very good reason for one woman to be believed for about a year, and then it is a rumour, and then it is a woman with a rumour. If Mara Quill were hit under the arch of a cart on the Vellum road tomorrow, the pattern would be gone, and the only trace of it would be a catalogue entry in a building that had an interest in the pattern not existing, saying that a leaf had been swapped, with no way on earth for a later person to show that it had.

The Ashgate leaf was in a locked room. Her copy of the pattern was in her own head. Those were two rooms with the same thing in them and neither of them was a room anything could be checked in.

She got out a sheet of paper at about two in the morning and wrote out what a second, independent copy would have to be, and it took twenty minutes and it came to four lines, and then she sat looking at the four lines for a long time.

> *1. The same substitution of a whole leaf, not a strike-through, on a document this office has never held.*
> *2. The same institutional correction wording, from a schedule entry, in a different archive.*
> *3. The same ink, from a dated tin, on a document the Ashgate series has never been compared with.*

*2 and 3 were the pattern. 1 was the trick that had actually been used on the Ashgate leaf, because whoever did it had removed figures rather than struck them, and a second copy of a strike-through list did not prove a second leaf-removal.*

And then she wrote the fifth line, which she had known was coming since about the eleventh of the month and had been not writing down.

> *The only survey originals of the blue steps in the basin are in the blue steps.*

---

She was born in the second house on a lane in a district that is not on any sheet she has ever certified, to a mother who had kept the pump books for a household of canal workers, and she has been eleven years in this building and has never once been able to find her own mother's lane on a page she has certified.

The fair copy was upstairs, in the open survey press, in the middle of the room where anybody could walk in and take it out, because no one has ever contested Glassstep, because Glassstep is not a place anybody has ever claimed was false. The fair copy was useless. A fair copy is a copy of a copy of a survey, drawn up in a press in Vellum by four men over a winter, and what was in it was a clean list of a district that did not exist, with the household names gone and a marginal reason in a hand with a leaning *c*.

The field book was the original. The book the surveyor had carried up the hill with, with the chain lines and the levels and the house numbers in the order he had walked them, and the redaction sheet was a loose folded folio tied into the back of it with a bootlace, and by the practice that predates this office by a century the book went to the house the surveyor had lodged in on the last night of the survey and stayed there as the district's own copy of itself.

Mara's mother had told her the name of that house when she was about eleven, in the way mothers tell children things that are not important yet: *the Roegs have the book, and mind it, and if anybody ever asks you for a paper out of this lane you say you do not know where the paper is.*

---

She began the warrant on the twenty-fourth, which was the correct route and took four days and two refusals, and which was the only reason she was ever going to be allowed to stand in that yard with a lamp.

A conservator could not go into a district and take a document away, and could not ask a household for one either. What a conservator could do was a **collation**: the quarterly verification of an office fair copy against the district original, which the Office had done every quarter since before Mara came to it, and which was how a fair copy was corrected when a district had kept something the press had got wrong.

A collation warrant was a printed sheet with five boxes and it was a real instrument and it cost the applicant nothing, and it required: the volume, the fair copy it was to be collated against, the district, the date, and the purpose.

She filled in the purpose in eleven words, because eleven words was what a purpose could hold.

*To establish whether the district original agrees with the fair copy as to household names and the schedule entry of their removal.*

Kreft read it, and signed it, and then did the thing she had braced for.

"You proposed this yourself," he said, "this morning, before the requisition tray came round, and you did not wait to be asked, and I am going to write on the sheet that you proposed it, because if I do not, then in two years somebody will read a conservator's signature on a district travel warrant and think somebody sent her."

"That is fair."

"It is also going to be the fourth note in your file this quarter," he said, "and I am going to write that one too, and it will say *district travel, unaccompanied, self-proposed*, and it will not say anything else, and I do not intend to have a conversation with you about it."

---

The application for the blue steps came down on the twenty-fourth with the morning's tray, because the warden's office sent everything it entered in the district book to the Office on a routine, and because the Office filed anything it received in a tray.

It was a single sheet with a printed border, a warden's date stamp in the middle, and six numbered boxes, and Mara Quill read it standing up in the tray room with forty other people's paperwork under her arm, and the second box stopped her dead.

*2. Roeg, Hanne, of the fourth step, the blue steps, holding the household at the fourth step.*

Mara Quill put the tray down on the shelf, which she was not supposed to do, and stood there.

She had spent fourteen days with a folio she could not get at, in a building that had published her finding without her name, in a city that had just put a household's name and a household's step on a piece of good paper and sent it to a man whose registry is a list of names with addresses on them.

The only copy of the survey in the basin that could corroborate anything at all was a book lying under a floor in the house at the top of that application, in the keeping of the woman who had put her own name in the second box on the evening of the twenty-second, four days after Mara Quill had gone down to the low court of the Fair Copy and left a volume in a strong room without being told.

Two rooms, both locked. One of them with her initials on the marker.

She read the whole thing through twice, standing at the shelf, including box five, and then she did something she had not planned.

She went and got a slip and copied out the file number and the date stamp and the warden's name, and the words of box two, and the words of box five, and she copied the words of box two and box five only, and she wrote at the foot of the copy in her own hand: *Copied 24th day of the month, from the district tray, by M. Quill, conservator. The applicant's consent to this copy is not held.*

Then she filed that slip in the requisition book, which is a public book, and which anybody in the building can read.

It was the first thing she had ever put in a public book that she could not prove, and it was the correct thing to do, and she went down the stairs to the lower room afterwards and found it had cost her more than the drawing had, and understood exactly why, which was that the drawing had been about the past and the copy was about a living woman and a living house, and one of those was a job and the other one was a choice.
