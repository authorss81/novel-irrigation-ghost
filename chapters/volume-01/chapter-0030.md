# Chapter 0030 — What A Warden Writes Down

Sluice Warden Pell came down the cattle track on the morning of the fifteenth with his coat over his arm and a satchel he had not brought the last time, and he was in the lane for four hours and did not go up to the house at all, and a hundred and forty people stood in the yard from about eleven and stayed until three, which was the largest a yard in the blue steps had ever been and which nobody had arranged.

He had come on the Tuesday because he wrote to the district book on the Tuesday, and he had told a yard full of people on the thirty-fourth day that he was going to write to the district book every week for as long as he held the post, and he had meant it in a way that turned out to be more expensive than he had calculated, because a man who says he is going to write a line every week is a man who has agreed to have something to write about.

Hanne Roeg's letter was in the satchel. It was on a sheet of good paper, the kind a person buys when they mean it, and the ink had not run, and it had a woman's name at the foot of it in a hand that had got worse toward the end.

"Ma'am," said Pell. "Before I read this out I am going to say three things, and then I am going to read it out, and then I am going to tell you what my office can do with it, which is almost nothing, and I am not going to have anybody in this yard spend four hours hoping."

"Go on, then," said Hanne Roeg.

"One. A warden's office cannot inspect a sealed works, cannot order a structural examination, and cannot enter a district. I have said the last of that in a return already and I am not going to pretend to a wider power because a woman has written a good letter.

"Two. I am going to read it out because a warden's entry that nobody has heard read is not an entry, it is a scrap of paper in a book in an office, and the first document this district ever had said there was nothing in it, and the first document this district has *written* is not going to sit in a book unread.

"Three. This is going up. Not today and not out of spite. A condition enters this office and it goes on, and there is nowhere above me except the commissioner's office, and the commissioner's office is the office that sealed those works six years ago and holds the file on the engineer whose name is in your letter. I would like everybody in this yard to hear that sentence before they decide how they feel about it, and I would like it heard in daylight."

Nobody moved. About a third of a hundred and forty people understood exactly what he had said and the rest of them looked at the third.

Then he read the letter out. It took eleven minutes. He read it the way he read everything, with the timing of a man reading an instrument, and he did not editorialise once, and at the fourth of the five paragraphs he slowed down, because the fourth was the one about a woman of seventy-one who had already refused a hole in a hill in writing and had put the refusal in a city document on purpose.

When he had finished he folded the sheet and put it inside his coat, and then he took out a form, because the letter was a request and a request is not an entry and a warden enters conditions.

---

The form was the same form as the twenty-second of the month. Five boxes, printed: **row number. keeper. tanks. gallons. last serviced.**

He filled in two of them.

> *Row number: no row. No row exists. Entered as "none held" in the box headed row number, this office having no row.*
> *Tanks: 3, on the 21st day of the month, aggregate 1,400 gal. 38 pails, aggregate 600 gal. All empty. Yard cistern, 10 ft by 12 ft to coping, 8 ft of room, 4,700 gal of room. 950 gal standing on the evening of this date. Dispensary churn, 80 gal, and a stone jar, 11 gal on the 9th day of this month and not replenished since.*

And then the remark, which is where a warden's office keeps everything it cannot put in a box, and it is long, and he read it out in a yard with a hundred and forty people in it, and it is in the district book of the lower terrace road in a fair hand, and anybody may read it.

> *Condition reported to this office on the ninth day of this month by three persons of this district, and confirmed to this office by the engineer named in the district application of the twenty-second: that there is a water chamber beneath this district, on the works at Ashgate, having standing water in it with a measured head of 5·89 metres at the main wheel, that a pumping plant on that works has been abstracting from that chamber continuously for four years against a certificate of condition certifying the chamber void at decommissioning, and that a settlement of eleven houses and thirty-four persons stands upon that chamber and appears on no schedule in this city.*
> *No time is entered against this condition, because a warden enters the time a condition begins and this warden was not present and has not seen the time it began and will not enter a time he did not witness. It began on the first day of this month. That is the date. This form has no box headed time. It is the only box I have ever wanted in it, and the reason nobody in this city knows what hour of what day a district of one hundred and twenty-six persons started to stand on top of a chamber of water is that there has never been anywhere to write it down.*
> *This office has no power to inspect a sealed works, has no power to enter a district, has no power to order an examination, and has entered no finding in this matter. The condition is entered. A condition is not a finding and a finding is not a remedy and this office has none of the three.*
> *Further: a notice of uncontrolled channel condition was posted in this district on the first day of this month by a warden of this office, with the cause box left empty on purpose. It is the only document this office holds in which the words the blue steps do not appear, and it is the reason this condition can be entered at all, because a condition entered on a document that says nothing about where it came from is a condition this office can be asked about without saying anything. The condition was reported to this office on the ninth day of this month. It is entered here on the fifteenth. Fourteen days from the notice and six from the report, and this warden would like the six on the record next to it, because the six are the office's and nobody else's.*
> *S. Pell, sluice warden, lower terrace road. Fifteenth day of this month.*

He read all of that out, and then he put the form down on the low house step next to two slates, and a hundred and forty people looked at it, and the boxes said what boxes say.

---

Nobody noticed for about four minutes and then Tam Roke noticed, from the top end, in a voice that carried the way it has carried since he was thirty-four years old.

"Pell. That box there. *Keeper.*"

"It is blank," said Pell.

"It is blank because you have not written a keeper."

"It is blank because this office has no keeper to write, and I have been down this road nineteen years and I am going to tell you what a keeper is in this office and you will not like it. A keeper is a name against which entries are made. That is all it is. It is not a hero. It is not a man who fixes things. It is a man on a form that a person with a form will come and find, and it means that when I write a line in this book in six months and something is wrong, there is one person in this row who is *in this book* and can be asked what happened."

"Then put somebody in it," said a voice from the arch.

"No," said Tam Roke, to the arch, without turning round, and it was so flat and so fast that the arch went quiet. "No, and it is not me, and it is not going to be me, and I would like it entered that I have said that in front of a hundred and forty people and not two."

---

Tarin Rusk had been standing at the wall since eleven and had not said anything, and at about twenty past two he came off the wall and put a sheet of paper on the low house step under the warden's form.

"Before anybody goes home," he said, "there is one more thing and it is mine and I have written it, and Pell is not going to write it because a warden does not write opinions, and I have no office, so it is going in the book on my own hand with my own name at the bottom of it, and there is exactly one copy and it is on this step.

> *Engineer's statement, fifteenth day of this month. T. Rusk, senior flow engineer, Water Office, under a stand-down from the tenth of this month and under two open charges neither joined to the other.*
> *1. The head at the main wheel at Ashgate is 5·89 m. I took it off a pressure shadow on the page of a volume on the first day of this month, and the plant's own board has carried 5·9 for the two years above it, and those are the same figure. Since the plant was stopped on the second I have had no way of putting a gauge on that wheel and no authority to. Nothing done by me or by anybody else has increased it and nothing ever has been able to.*
> *2. The chamber is intact. It is not an empty ruin, it has not been drained, and the water in it stands eleven feet one inch above the outside ground at the door of the works, measured by string and rule and checked twice, and it has not moved.*
> *3. The works has a pump on it. The pump has been stopped since the night of the second of this month and it was being run before that for four years, and the number it was run against is on a board in the building, and the number on the board is not the number in the certificate.*
> *4. Eleven houses and thirty-four persons stand on the roof of that chamber and have stood on it for thirty years, and there is a well in one of them that brings up about a hundred gallons an hour out of it, and that well has never been tested by anybody, and I have no instrument that would test it, and I am obliged to say in a yard that I cannot promise it will be the same water tomorrow.*
> *5. I cannot make water come to this district. There is no head I can increase, no gate I have authority to open, and the only supply this district has had since the first of this month has been the discharge of a plant on a works two miles up the mill road, and that plant is a contractor's, and it has been stopped by a person I have not met, and I do not know whether it will be started again.*
> *6. I have a record stone at the low pump with one name cut on it and one face blank, and I cannot cut the blank one, and I have not cut it in a month. I have asked four people in this lane to put a hand on a route and all four of them said no, and I wrote down every one of their reasons at the time, and I still think three of them were right, and I am not going to argue with the fourth either.*

He read it out. Then he signed it, at the foot, on the step, in front of a hundred and forty people, and it was the first document he had signed since the tenth day of the month and it was thirty-six days.

Pell took it, and read it, and put it in the satchel with the letter, and did not ask for it back, and did not offer to certify it, and said:

"It goes up with the condition. That is what the condition does. It goes on the file and it goes into the district book, and there is a copy of the book in this office and a copy in the commissioner's registry, and in about nine days there will be three copies, and the third one will be in a building in Vellum with a number on the file, and in six weeks there will be a fourth in a place I cannot reach."

"Which is the office," said Tarin Rusk.

"Which is the office," said Sluice Warden Pell, "that holds two certificates in a deal box thirty yards from a chalk board, and which will receive this, and which is the reason I have written in the remark that I have no power to inspect and no power to enter a district, because a man is not obliged to act on a condition and I would like that on the record with my name at the bottom of it."

He picked his hat up off the step.

"Right," he said. "I have got a form with two boxes full and three empty, and I have been a warden on this road for nineteen years and I have never once been in a position to have two boxes full. Two of those three empties a man could fill in this yard in a minute, and I have not filled either, and it is one reason and not two.

"*Gallons remaining* wants a number, and there is a number, and if I put nine hundred and fifty in that box then in six weeks a man in an office will read a thousand gallons in a lane of a hundred and twenty-six people, and it will be true, and it will also be a lie, because it does not have the dispensary in it, or the jar, or the seventy gallons a day going out of the door, and a box that can only hold one of those three is a box I am not going to fill. And *last serviced* is a date a keeper writes, and this office has no keeper, and a date a man puts in a box headed *keeper* is a lie with a number on the front of it.

"The third is the keeper, and I have been thinking about the keeper for two hours and I have come to the end of what a warden may put in a box and I am not going to put the end of it in a box."

He looked at the yard.

"So that is where this district is on the fifteenth day of the month. It is in a book, three times over, with a chamber under it and a well in it and thirty-four people on top of it and three pounds and sixteen shillings in a bucket and a physician's book with nine children in it and a letter asking a question that no office in this city has ever been asked, and the keeper box is empty, and it is going to be empty on Friday, and it is going to be empty on the twenty-seventh of next month when a man with a form is due to come down this track in any case, and it will be empty on the day after that, unless somebody in this yard can say *I will answer for it* and mean it, and be answerable for it in ten years."

He put his hat on.

"Nobody has to answer me now," he said. "I have wanted to say that since the twenty-first and I have not said it. You have all had six weeks of this office standing in this yard telling you that things were possible, and this office has been wrong in this yard, and I am not going to be sorry for it this afternoon in a way that buys me anything. The first document this district ever had said there was nothing in it. Four children in a physician's book are eleven days past a fortnight that the woman who wrote it was proud of.

"Today is the day I tell you that a route with a name and a record and a keeper can be held, and a route with only a man in it cannot. I have been the man in it three times. The third time was on a mill road two miles up the hill in the small hours of Sunday morning, and it cost a man the use of his left hand, and I did not send him and I am not going to stand here and pretend to you that I would have stopped him. What I am entitled to say is that if there had been a name in that box a fortnight ago there would have been a person in this district a man with a form could have come and found, and I do not know what that would have been worth, and I am not going to stand in this yard and tell you it would have saved a hand."

Somebody said, at the back, "Then the box stays empty."

"Yes," said Sluice Warden Pell. "I expect it does. And I am going to write *keeper, none held* in it in my own hand, on this day, in a book that four people in this city have copies of, and if a man comes down that track in six weeks with a form he is going to find those two words, in a fair hand, in a fair book, and he is going to know that a district of a hundred and twenty-six persons looked at a box and left it empty on purpose and not because nobody noticed."
