# Chapter 0025 — The Well That Never Needed A Pump

It took them the whole of the eighth day to get out of the chamber, and the reason it took a day was a saw.

Niko Venn went up and down the two hundred and forty-one steps eleven times with a lamp and a length of oak bar, and he cut at the bar for the best part of six hours, and the bar would not come out of its sockets because oak that has been dry in a door frame for thirty years in a sealed building has shrunk about half an inch at each end and set like iron, and the only way to move it is to cut through the head of it, and the only way to cut through the head of it is to saw across four inches of oak with a hand saw and a bent blade in a stair two hundred feet from anybody who can help you.

Tarin Rusk went up with the lamp and came down eleven times, and on the fifth time he sat down on the fourteenth step and had a quarter of an hour of something that was not a panic and was not a plan either, and it was this: that if Niko Venn put his hand on that saw the way he had put his hand on a coil of somebody else's rope on the second of the month, then the two of them were going to be in a hole for three days and the boy's lamp would run out before the boy did.

He went down and made him take a rest and gave him the good hand's grip of the saw and watched the boy's face, and Niko Venn said, "I know," and took the rest, and came back to it.

Wick Venn went up in the morning and went down in the afternoon, once, and the going up took him the better part of two hours and the coming down four, and he did it with his right hand and a wall, and he did not ask for anything, and nobody offered, and afterwards he said it had been the easiest thing he had done in nine years, which was not a joke and which nobody in that stair believed and everybody let stand.

The bar came out of its sockets at ten past four in the afternoon, and the door came in against the frame and let the afternoon of the eighth day of the month into a stone chamber eleven feet under the floor of a building in a row of houses at the top of the mill road.

---

There was a bucket on the floor, and a broom, and a window with a shutter nailed open, and no other light in the world.

The room above them was a store. It had been a filter bed room and it had been a store for a long time, and it had a cold hearth in it and a shelf of candle ends on the mantel, and it smelled of candle ends and cold ash and wet lime.

Down the row outside, a girl of about fourteen was standing in the lane with a pail in each hand and a windlass bucket beside her, waiting, because she had heard a saw.

She looked at three men coming out of a store in a building she had swept out on Monday, and at the left hand of one of them bound in clean linen, and she said:

"You'll be the pit," she said. "You're the pit they said had water in it."

"Where is she," said Niko Venn.

---

Nell Drust was thirty-nine and kept the row and had kept it for sixteen years, and what she kept was the water, and the water came up out of the ground under her own kitchen floor and had done so for as long as there had been a row.

"There is no pump," she said. "There has never been a pump. You put a pail on the well-head and it comes up, and it has come up since before my mother-in-law's mother and this is not a thing I have to be proud of, it is a thing I have to be careful with, because I have four in this row who cannot be got to the far well and the far well is dry."

"How much."

"I don't know. Nobody has ever wanted to know. My mother-in-law used to say it came up about as fast as a man could lift it out with one pail, and that is the only figure there has ever been, and it is a figure a woman said on a step forty years ago."

Tarin Rusk took a six-gallon pail and his watch and put it on the well-head, which was a slab of stone with a hole in it, set in the floor of a kitchen in a house that had a blue door, which he noted and did not comment on, because there was a row of blue doors in a lane a mile and a half away and every terrace in this city had a row of blue doors on it, and it was not the same thing, and it was going to be extremely easy to make it the same thing and he was not going to do it.

> *Well at the row, Ashgate. Six-gallon pail, well-head slab, standing 3 min 30 sec to fill.*
> *6 gal in 3.5 min = 1.71 gal/min = 103 gal/hr = 2,466 gal a day.*

He checked it twice, because he was a man who checked things twice now, and then he told her the number, and she made him say it again, and then she said the thing that he carried down the hill for the rest of his life.

"Right," said Nell Drust. "Then there is more coming up out of that hole than thirty-four of us can use in a month, and you have been telling me that as though it were a discovery. It is not a discovery. It is a kitchen."

---

The row was eleven houses and thirty-four persons and they called it Ashgate, and Ashgate on any sheet in this city was a structure with a closure date on it and a warden's seal six years old across the front door.

There was a man there of about fifty called Coll Preece who had been four years on the pump and who came out of the row at half past four and did not appear to be frightened of anybody, which Tarin Rusk had expected and had still been glad of.

"Right," said Coll Preece. "You are the pit, and I said what I said on the second and I have thought about it since, so I will say it better.

"I said *you will be the Water Office, and you will be the wrong week* because you had a lamp and a coil of rope and no warrant, and because I have been on this plant four years and I have watched a tank certified dead in a room upstairs be pumped out of every week of those four years, and if you turned out to be the Water Office you would shut me down by Friday, and I have a boy on a staging who has not been paid since the second and a girl who brings me oil twice a day. So I said it to see what a man with a lamp would do.

"And what you did was go down. And then the office shut us off on the Wednesday, and there was no letter and no reason, and the horse went out of the basin on a cart on the Thursday with two other men's horses, and the sweep has not turned since." He looked at the row. "So I told you the truth and you went down a hole and the boy has not been paid. I have had a bad week as well."

"Who shut you off."

"The office. Not a man in it. An office." He said it without heat, because he had had a week to get to the bottom of it. "I have never seen a signature on anything at this works that I could put a name to. There is a name on the certificate. I have never met him and I have never seen him and I do not believe he knows this hill exists. I have got a ledger of cans and I get paid by the can and there is a board with a head on it and the head on the board has been 5·9 since the spring before last, and I have written that number nine hundred times, and there is a piece of paper in that store upstairs that says the tank was empty when they shut it, and it is nailed up in the same building as the board, and I have never once thought about it, because I am paid by the can."

---

The store was up a step and round the corner from where the door was, and it had a shelf of day-books on it and a press of nails under the window and a deal box with a lock on it, and the lock had been opened and shut so many times that the hasp was round.

The gauge certificate was in it. Two sheets, a year apart, and Tarin Rusk stood and read both of them with a lamp and did not touch them, because you do not touch a thing in a deal box in a store that is not yours.

> *CERTIFICATE OF CONDITION, ASHGATE INTAKE, MAIN WHEEL AND CHAMBER, LEVEL FOUR. Upon decommissioning the above structure the chamber was found void, with no residual head and no standing water, and no maintenance being required. Certified for the record.
> *4th day of the dry season.*

And underneath, in the same pen, in a slightly heavier hand, the word *maintained* — the way a man writes a word when he has been told to write it and does not have the time to be pleased with it.

Tarin Rusk had spent nineteen years in a building that had been signing its work in that pen since he was fourteen years old. He knew the way it came down off the *m* and the way it left a little hook on the end of a *d* and the way it never once hurried.

It was the second sheet that he took out and read and put back and read again, because it was four years old and it certified the same thing, and it had a different date on it, and there was nothing at all in the pen of the second sheet that was not in the pen of the first.

So there were two documents in a deal box in a store at the top of the mill road: a certificate, in an engineer's hand, saying that there is nothing in the tank, and a board, thirty yards away in a building nobody could enter from the road, with a column of chalk figures going back years that say there is a head of five and nine on it.

He got out the blue book on the step of the store with the lamp on his knee and wrote four lines and no more than four, because he was a man under two charges and a third was forming in his coat like weather.

> *8th day. Store, works at Ashgate, deal box. Certificate of condition, chamber at Ashgate void, no residual head, no standing water, no maintenance required. Two sheets, one dated at decommissioning, one dated four years ago, both in the same hand, both certified void, the second with the word *maintained* beneath it in the same pen.*
> *The hand is that of the acting commissioner of this city. I have known it for nineteen years and I am not going to be asked to swear to it in a room and I am not going to swear to it in one today.*
> *In a building thirty yards away, on a board, in the hand of a man paid by the can, a column headed *head at wheel* reads 5·9 for the best part of two years, and reads 5·7 for two years before that, and no one has ever written down why the figure changed.*
> *So: the tank is not void. A plant has been pumping out of it continuously for four years against a certificate that says there is nothing in it to pump. That is abstraction out of a stored head, and a stored head that is not on a schedule is not in any allocation, and there is no allocation to infringe. I cannot do one single thing about any of the four lines above, because I have no office, no district, no crew and no money, and I have two charges, and the only man in this city who can act on it has signed both pieces of paper, and he is not standing in this lane.*

He shut the book.

Then he went and had that argument with himself out loud, standing in a yard, and lost it, and then Nell Drust put a cup of tea into his hand because it was that hour and because the row did that.

It was hot and it went down the way water goes down and it had no taste of any kind in it, and it had had no taste in it since the seventh of the month, and he drank all of it, because it was a cup of tea given by a woman who had never met him to a man who had come up out of a hole in her floor, and he thanked her, and he did not say one word about it to anybody then or afterwards.

---

They came down off the hill on the evening of the eighth, and the road out of Ashgate goes past eleven houses and a shuttered store and a building with a padlock that has not been picked since a man put it there six years ago, and at the top of the mill road, where the road turns and you can see the city in the valley, Niko Venn stopped and turned round and looked back up at the row, and did not say anything, and they walked on.

Tarin Rusk came down off the watershed in the dark with a lamp he no longer needed, a bad shoulder, a book with nine lines in it, and a fact.

He had a fact about a chamber under a street with eleven feet one inch of water in it, and a street of houses on top of it, and a well that came up at a hundred gallons an hour into a kitchen, and a certificate in a deal box thirty yards from a chalk board, and eleven houses and thirty-four persons who had been living on the top of it for thirty years without being told, and he had not one thing he could do with any of it, and he was going to have to go down a cattle track at dawn and say all of it out loud in a yard to a hundred and twenty people.

The worst of it, and he had worked this out somewhere in the second mile and tested it three times since, was not the certificate. It was that the chamber had a roof, and under the roof there were houses, and the people in them had a name, and the name was the name of the thing underneath them, and they had been saying it out loud, in the ordinary way, to anybody who came up the hill, for thirty years.

Nobody had put them there. That was the thing he could not get past, and it was not a comfort, and it was worse than a comfort.

There was no order. There had never been an order. There had been a works, and then a closure, and then houses with nobody in them, and then people, and then a well, and then thirty years, and at no point in any of it had a single person decided to do any of it to anybody.
