# Chapter 0037 — A Bundle Of Papers

The Water Office kept its discipline room over the sluice store on the terrace road, and it had a table, six chairs, a window that gave onto a wall, and a clock that was four minutes fast, and on the morning of the twenty-fourth day of the month it was the room in which Tarin Rusk was told for the first time that his own office had been keeping a bundle of papers about him since the spring.

The hearing was called a preliminary inquiry. That was on the summons in his hand, and a preliminary inquiry has one question, and this one was on a printed card propped against the mantel.

> *Whether charge one and charge two may be heard together.*

Dunn Averell was forty-eight and a solicitor in this city, and the district had retained him out of a fund for men who cannot pay, because a servant of a corporation gets a friend of the office and the only friend of the office available in twenty-one months was him. He had never met Tarin Rusk. He was not on his side.

He said so in the first two minutes, before the charge was read, because he had decided in the corridor that he was going to.

"Mr Rusk. I am not your friend and I am not your enemy and I have read the file, which is the whole of my preparation and there has been no other preparation, because this office has no money and I am not paid by the day. I am going to do a competent job and a competent job in a preliminary inquiry consists of finding out what the card is actually asking and then arguing the answer that helps my client most, and on this card they are not the same question, and I am going to argue for the one that does not help you, and I would like you to know that I have thought about it and I am not being brave. I am being thorough, and being thorough is what they pay me for."

Ilse Roan was thirty-nine and had built the charge papers in April, and she put the two sheets on the table in front of her and did not read them out, because everybody in the room had them.

> *First charge, tenth day of the month before: tampering with city records. Subject: the calibration log of the Ashgate intake, in the matter of a figure recorded on the first day of the month before.*
> *Second charge, twentieth day of the month before: unauthorized opening of a sealed structure, and service of water from a closed work this city's ledger records as sealed. Subject: a gate on a works sealed six years, and a branch off the run to a district.*

"Two subjects," said Ilse Roan. "Two dates. Ten days apart. The first is about a book. The second is about a structure and a district. The registry's position is that they arise from the same course of conduct and should be heard together, and that is the whole of the question on the card."

"The registry's position is wrong," said Dunn Averell.

Nobody had expected the man who had been retained for the applicant to say that, and Ilse Roan put her pen down.

"Mr Averell, they are in the same month and the same matter and—"

They were not the same subject and they never were, and if he let them be joined he would spend the rest of the inquiry on a question he could not win, and he would do it at the applicant's expense.

Averell had the bundle in his hand. It was a brown paper file about two inches thick and he had not opened it.

"Charge one is a document case. It turns on a figure in a day-book and on a genuine pressure shadow under a removed leaf, and the shadow is older than my client by a year. Charge two is a structure case. It turns on a gate, a head, a tank and a district, and none of those four things is in dispute between anybody, including your registry, because your registry has never said otherwise. Join them and I stand up in front of this table and argue the weight of a nib cut for two days and a half, and the district is not here, and the chamber is not here, and the eleven houses are not here, and the only people in the room are you and me and a man with two bad hands."

"A course of conduct is a matter for the chair and not for counsel."

"A course of conduct is a matter for the chair," said Averell, "and I am telling the chair that the conduct alleged in the second charge is not proved, because the head is in dispute, and if I let you join them I have conceded the head to get to the nib, and I would like the chair to understand that I have done that arithmetic before this morning and it is why I am the one saying it."

He put the bundle on the table.

"Now. The other thing. And I am going to do this because I am not going to let it be done to him."

He untied it, and it was not large, and it was the sort of bundle an office makes when it is being complete: everything about a man, in order, tied twice.

"This is from the fourteenth year before last," he said. "Emergency closure, the Riverside cut, a side channel, a flood, the nineteenth of that month. He was nineteen and he had been in service fourteen months. The file has the order, the entry, the hour, the engineer's signature, the report, the inquiry, and a schedule.

"It has been in his file since the spring. It is in his file because it is his file and it is in it because the registry does not take things out, and I have read it, and I am not going to have anybody in this room discover it at eleven o'clock this morning, and I would like it read out, because it is going to be read out anyway and I would rather it was read out by the man it was written about."

He pushed it across the table to Tarin Rusk.

It took him about four minutes. The order was in a hand he had not seen since he was fourteen and it was his own. The channel was a side cut on the Riverside at a place called the Tannery Race, and the order was to close it, and the hour was in the afternoon, and the reason in the margin was in the voice of a man of nineteen writing the reason as briefly as he could because the water was up.

Below that was the report, and then the inquiry, and then a schedule of names.

Eleven names. The youngest was nine. The oldest was sixty-one and had gone up onto the roof of a store and had been brought down at nine in the evening by two men from the hospital, which is in the report and not on the schedule.

He read the schedule to the end and then sat with his hands flat on it for a moment, and then he turned it round and pushed it back across the table so that all three of them could see it, and he did not say one word about it, and it was the most disciplined thing anybody in that room had ever watched a man do.

"The finding," said Averell, "is that the closure was justified. That word is in it. *Justified.* And the finding is that it was made without authority by a man of nineteen, and both of those are in the same document, and the office has had both of those sentences for fourteen years and has not put them together in anything, and I am going to put them together now.

"It is not evidence that he cannot do his job. It is evidence of a kind. It is the evidence of a man who will close a channel without authority, on a day, with a number in his head, and take the responsibility himself, and be right about the head, and be wrong about the floor, and be right about the hospital and wrong about eleven people on a schedule.

"Which is exactly what the registry alleges in the second charge, and I am not able to keep it out, and I am not going to try, and I would like it entered that I have not tried."

"Admitted," said the chair.

"Entered that it is not excluded and not objected to," said Averell. "And I would like the chair to hear the difference, because there is a difference, and it is the only thing I have left to argue with."

"The chair has not ruled on admission and will rule on it after the card is answered." The chair had not moved once in twenty minutes. "Mr Rusk, you have been given the packet on the fourteenth. Is there anything in it that is wrong?"

"There is one thing in it that is incomplete," said Tarin Rusk, "and I have known for fourteen years that it is incomplete, and I am not going to tell you what it is, because it is not mine, and because if I tell you what is missing then I will be asked what is in it, and I will have to say a name, and I have no business saying a name in this room on an unrelated matter."

Nobody pressed him, which was not kindness. It was a chair with a card to get through.

The card was answered at eleven twenty.

The chair found that the two charges might not be joined, on any ground, and that the application was refused, and that the second charge could not be heard until a finding had been made on the physical condition of the works at Ashgate.

"There is no such finding," said the chair, "and this office has no power to make one. The works were sealed six years ago. I am told, and I have not seen them, that the condition was certified on that occasion and once again four years afterwards. I am also told that the signature on both of them is the acting commissioner's, and that his office is the office that sealed the works, and I am not going to sit in this room and pretend to you that a certificate is not a finding, because it is one, and it is in a deal box in a store that this office has never opened, and if that is wrong then the honest thing is to go and look, and this office has not looked in six years and is not going to look before a man with a form comes down a cattle track."

"Then the finding exists," said Averell.

"Then the finding is in a box and not in this room, and a finding in a box is worth exactly what the box is worth, and I will not rule on it and I will not let anybody in here rule on it in my absence."

"Suspended accordingly," said the chair. "Until such time as a finding is made or an examination is held. Mr Averell, you will want a date."

"I will want a date," said Averell, "and I am not going to be given one, and I would like it minuted that I asked."

"It will be minuted that you asked. It will be minuted that no date is entered. That is how the last one went and that is how this one will go, and it is not anybody's malice, Mr Averell, it is that this office has a sheet of paper and the matter is not on the sheet.

"And I will tell you what I would have to have in order to enter a date," said the chair. "An examination. I cannot enter a date for a hearing on a matter whose facts have not been found, and I have been in this post nine years and I have entered four and three of them were adjourned."

---

There were two things left on the table and Averell dealt with both of them in about six minutes, and the second of them is the one that mattered.

"Third charge," he said. "Unauthorized descent on a sealed works from the quarry side. Discovered by the office that reported the lantern on the ledge. It is not on your sheet, Mr Roan, and I would like it on the record that I have asked for it three times in writing and I have had it once, in a corridor, on the fourteenth, and I have not been given the sheet. It exists as a conversation. When it is a sheet I will be served with it. I mention it because in about three weeks somebody is going to come in here and say the conduct is a course of conduct, and on that day the *course of conduct* will be a descent into a sealed works with a named foreman's lamp on the ledge, and I will want the sheet, and I will not be able to argue a card in a corridor."

"Mr Averell, there is a great deal in this file and I am not going to say any of it is unfair—"

"Mr Roan, I have not been accusing you of anything since the first two minutes," said Averell. "I said the registry's position is wrong. That is not an accusation. It is a position, and it is a wrong one, and if it is a wrong one twice then I would like to write it down, and you can decide what to do with the writing."

And then he said the other thing.

"The Open Channel Guild sent an inspection team to that works on the twenty-second. Four men, three days, and they are not from this city and they do not work for this office. I would like that on the minutes, because a Guild team on a sealed works is a document arriving, and whatever it says is going to be in this matter whether anybody files it or not, and I would like this office to have written down on the twenty-fourth of this month that it knows that team was there, so that it is not said afterwards that the office was surprised."

The chair ruled that it should be minuted.

Tarin Rusk walked back up the terrace road alone in the middle of the day with the summons in his coat and the file's eleven names behind his eyes and no office to go to, and he was not sure which of the two things in his coat was the heavier, and it was not the eleven names, because he had known about the eleven for fourteen years, and it was the *suspended* — because a preliminary inquiry that cannot be held until somebody examines a chamber he has already been down is not a win, and it is not a loss, and it is a third thing, and it means that the next date in his life is going to be set by a man with a form coming down a cattle track from the city.

He got as far as the second gatehouse and then had to sit down on the step for a while, because the bench book was in his coat and he had been carrying it since the night before and had not opened it, and he opened it on a gatehouse step at half past one in the afternoon, and the first page had a date on it, a day and a month and a year, in a hand he did not know, and a line about a gate bed and a cold river and a number, and nothing else, and it was nine inches by six and it was written in from both ends, and it had been going for longer than the works at Ashgate had been sealed.
