# Chapter 0044 — Yes, And The Wrong Man In The Chair

Amos Trevail was forty-four and he was a district examiner, which is a man with a printed warrant, a form, a statutory right to enter any ground in his district, and no power whatsoever over a person, a contract, or a works that had been sealed by somebody else before he was born.

He had been in the post eleven months. In that eleven months he had examined eleven conditions, entered nine of them, and had learned two things about his own job that were not in the warrant: that a district examination is not a hearing, it is a form with a great deal of room on it, and that the only thing he could actually do about a district was make the district legible to somebody else.

He wrote out of Vellum on the second day of this month, on a sheet of his own with a printed head on it, and it went up the cattle track in a bag, and it asked three things.

> *To the Office of the Acting Commissioner, water and channels. From Amos Trevail, district examiner, lower terrace road and the works district, warrant in hand.*
> *1. I am directed to hold a district examination at the works at Ashgate on the twelfth day of this month, upon a request made at a bench in daylight on the twenty-seventh day of the last month and forwarded by the warden's book.*
> *2. I require the store at that works to be opened in my presence on that day, and the deal box in it to be produced and opened, and the papers in it to be read into the record of the examination.*
> *3. I require the acting commissioner to be notified of the day, and I require to be informed in writing whom this office intends to send to attend.*

The answer came up the track on the fifth, on the fifth sheet of paper there had ever been on this matter, and Sluice Warden Pell read it out in the yard on the terrace road at about ten o'clock in the morning with about fifty people in it, and he read it in five numbered lines because it was in five numbered lines.

> *Office of the Acting Commissioner, water and channels. Fourth day of this month. To Amos Trevail, district examiner, in reply to your communication of the second.*
> *1. The examination will be held at the works at Ashgate on the twelfth day of this month at ten of the clock. You will attend. The ground beneath the district at the lower terrace road will be examined in the same sitting at your discretion.*
> *2. The store will be opened on that day. The deal box in it will be produced and opened in your presence. This office has not had the key to that store for six years and has this day taken measures to have it.*
> *3. The acting commissioner will attend in person and will produce the records of that works, which are the records of his office, and which it is the duty of the officer responsible for them to produce.*
> *4. You are reminded that a district examination is not a hearing and has no power to take evidence about a person. Anything tendered to you about a person will be received by you, minuted by you, and not acted upon by this office, and the person will be served with a copy of it.*
> *5. This office notes that the district in question holds no row and no schedule entry, and that the matter you have been directed to examine was requested by it, and that a district examination may examine the ground.*

---

The yard took about two minutes on the third line and then went quiet in a way that yards do not.

Then Old Marden said, from about the middle, in a voice that carried without him meaning it to:

"It said yes."

"It said yes," said Sluice Warden Pell.

"It said yes and it has put the man in the chair," said Old Marden. "That is not a refusal. That is the other thing. That is a man agreeing to open a box and bringing the one who wrote what is in it to hold it while it is open."

"It is," said Pell. "And I have read it three times and there is no line in it that anybody can object to without the objection reading like a request to keep the box shut."

---

The argument about what to do about that was had in four places over two days and it was had badly, and the reason it was had badly is that everybody in the lane had spent a month learning how to read a form, and a form that agrees with you is much harder to fight than a form that refuses you.

Hanne Roeg got it first and said so, on the step, to nobody in particular, in a yard of about thirty.

"The only thing I can do with that is ask him a question in the room," she said, "and to ask him a question in the room I have to put it in the box. And I stood in this yard on Sunday the twenty-seventh day of last month and argued for that box standing empty, and I said that a hundred and forty people had looked at it, and I was right that day, and I am not going to pretend I was wrong.

"I have been arguing for an empty box since a man with a bar asked me to sign a stone. And what I now have to do is fill it, because the third line of that sheet is the only way a piece of paper in this city ever gets put in front of a man who wrote one, and I would rather have that box full by a thousand and not by one.

"So I am going to put one line in it, in my own hand, and I am going to read it out before I write it, and I am going to be exact about what it is and is not, because I have signed five things in this lane's life and every one of them has cost somebody something."

She read out the line twice and then Niko Venn wrote it on the slate and she made him read it back.

> *That the two certificates in the deal box at the store at Ashgate, both in the acting commissioner's hand, both certifying the chamber of that works void, of no residual head, of no standing water, and requiring no maintenance, and neither naming the day it was made, nor the person who made any measurement, nor the plate, the bore, or the method, be read aloud in full into the record of the examination, and be produced to the examiner, and be minuted as produced and as read.*
> *This is a matter. It is not a charge, and it does not ask that any charge be brought or withdrawn, and it does not ask for a schedule, a route, water, or a keeper, and the district asks for nothing it asked for on the twenty-seventh day of last month except this one line.*
> *H. Roeg. The fourth step. The fourth day of this month.*

Marda Ollan wrote it out in her own hand, and then wrote the two charges standing against Tarin Rusk beneath it with their dates on them, because a request that costs a man something ought to carry what it costs, and then she said the sentence she had to say.

"I argued on Sunday for an empty box," she said. "I said an empty box that a hundred and forty people looked at was worth more to us than a full one that one woman wrote. And the box has a line in it and I am not going to stand in this yard and pretend that I am not part of the reason it is not empty.

It is one line and it asks for a piece of paper to be read out loud. If anybody in this yard has a better sentence I will copy that one instead and I will not feel anything about it at all, and I am going to be in this yard until about six."

Nobody had a better sentence, and she copied it, and Marda Ollan was right about the empty box on Sunday and the full box on Thursday and neither of those things cancels the other, and that is a thing about boxes that everybody in this lane now knows and did not know a fortnight ago.

---

On the afternoon of the same day, at the public desk in Vellum, a conservator of the Municipal Memory Office published a document.

The document was four sheets and it had a provenance head on it and a registration number and a place on the public shelf where anybody could stand and read it, and it concerned a bound volume of one hundred and eighty-one years, seven hundred and twenty-four leaves, four entries a year, and one leaf at the end of it.

Mara Quill established, in eleven hundred words and without raising her voice once, that the last entry in that leaf had been removed and replaced by a leaf of later stock in sizing and in ink; that the pressure of the same pen making the same shape was carried on the leaf beneath; that at the end of the last line of the removed leaf the shadow carried a schedule reference of three digits, which she did not transcribe; that the closure schedule of the lower terrace road, entry 118, dated twenty-six years ago, records the plating of the terrace outfalls of rows seven to twelve and the removal of that row from the distribution schedule; and that the schedule is a public document in the warden's office of that road, and that any person might go and compare the two.

And then, in the fourteenth paragraph, she named the document's subject by referring to it, and the document's subject was a schedule, and the schedule was the **lower terrace road**, and the lower terrace road is the road the blue steps is on.

It went out on the Friday. Mara Quill filed a second copy at the warden's office on the terrace road by the ordinary course of the document, and Pell entered it in his book in a fair hand, and by the Saturday morning a copy was nailed to the door of the dispensary with a wet corner, and about nine people read it standing in the lane and compared the same lines in the same order.

Nobody in that lane had any way of knowing that the blue steps had just been put into a public record in the record city by a reference, and no row, and no schedule entry, and no name, by a woman who had been told four months ago by a man with a bar that he was not going to be the one who writes a place into a document by pointing at a document that covers it.

Tarin Rusk found out about it on the Saturday afternoon, in a lane, from a woman with a slate who had read it out twice on a step, and he walked up the lane to the fourth step and said four sentences to Hanne Roeg, and then he went down to the dispensary and had a conversation with Yara Sen in a doorway that lasted about two minutes and that neither of them would repeat.

Then he did the thing that condition five requires.

He wrote it out. He wrote it in the blue book, and then Marda Ollan copied it into the district book in her own hand, in the yard, in daylight, with both their names at the foot of it and the date, because they had agreed in a room in Vellum in the spring that they would disagree in public with the disagreement recorded, and neither of them had ever imagined for a second that the first time would be about a place.

> *From the engineer named in the charges standing against him, the fourth day of this month, in the yard of the blue steps, and entered in the district book in the hand of M. Ollan, as required by the fifth condition of an agreement made at a dispensary step and of which the whole of the fifth condition is that they disagree in public with the disagreement recorded.*
> *I told the woman of this lane in a road on the twenty-third day of last month that I would not be the man who writes a district out of a document by referring to a document that covers it, and she put it in her own book in her own hand, and I have not been asked to take it back and I am not asking her to.*
> *She has done it. A schedule reference is a reference and she did not transcribe it, and the district is in the record city in a public document because a road is named in a public schedule. That is exactly the thing I said. I was right about it, and I said so to a man with a satchel, and the man with the satchel was right about all three of his reasons and the third one is the only one that ever cost anybody anything.*
> *And I am going to say the other half of it, because a record of a disagreement that has only one side in it is a document about somebody instead of a document about a thing.*
> *She named a road, and the road is where a district is that has no row, and there is a chamber under a row of eleven houses two miles up a mill road, and there is a leaf in a book in Vellum that was cut out and put back with something else in its place, and those two things have been in two buildings in two cities for four months and are now in one paragraph in one document on a public shelf where anybody can stand and read it. That is not nothing. That is the only time this month that two halves of a thing have been in the same sentence.*
> *And I still say that a reference is a way of putting a place in a document without being asked, and that I would not do it to her, and that I am not going to be able to un-know that I read about a girl of about fourteen on a slate this month and thought that a description is not a name.*
> *I am not going to say any of this in a room in the city. I have no standing in a room in the city and there is not going to be one this month. So it is written down here, in a yard, in daylight, in a district book with four copies of the warden's book behind it, and a woman will copy it if she wants to, which she is entitled to do and which I would like.*

---

The copy went up the cattle track on the Sunday morning in a satchel, carried by a boy of twenty-two who was not an apprentice and had paid a penny at a chandler's on the Friday and had five pence left and no trade, and he carried it up because he had said out loud that he was not going to chase the Water Office and that was still true, and because there are two people in the blue steps who cannot read and it is not acceptable to the lane that a document lands in a building and nobody in the lane has been in the building.

He read it to Ondra at the left of the arch on the Sunday evening by the light of a lamp on a step, all two thousand words of it, twice, and Ondra sat with the blanket round his boy's legs and did not interrupt him once.

"That is a woman in Vellum writing your road down," said Ondra at the end of it.

"That is a woman in Vellum writing your road down."

"And you are cross about it."

"I am not cross about it," said Niko Venn. "I was not able to say anything in the second paragraph and that is the first time in two years. Read it again. I want to hear the part where she does it deliberately."
