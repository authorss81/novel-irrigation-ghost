# Chapter 0008 — The Replaced Leaf Has a Cousin

On the ninth day of the ration, the Municipal Memory Office of Vellum received a crate of commission-supply paper, six hundred pounds of it, and the delivery was the reason nobody noticed that Mara Quill had been in the lower room since the lamps came on.

The paper was not the point. The paper was the reason the day porters were on the ground floor with a barrow and shouting, and the reason the night porter had gone to the strong room to sleep off a cough in a locked building, which was a thing he did most nights and which nobody except Mara had ever thought worth a question.

She had the Ashgate volume on the bench in the lower room with the door shut and the shutter across the window, and she had a lamp with a green glass over the flame, and she had until the porter woke up, which gave her an hour and forty minutes.

The restricted stack held forty volumes, all of them contested, all of them held because somebody in this building had once signed something in a hurry. Folio eleven of the Ashgate maintenance series had been in it since the third, since Kreft had ruled that a volume with an uncertified leaf could not be circulated to anybody, including the person who had asked the question, and had ruled it in one line without any meeting at all.

Two volumes on either side of it were an estate boundary dispute from a flooded village and a will in which a dead woman had left her pump to her sister, and neither of those had moved since 118.

She had the ink before midnight.

Court-supply ink does not date by feel. It dates by three things: how it was cut, what was in it, and what tin it came out of. The nib cut across a court-supply nib leaves four small shoulders where the quill split, and a private ink leaves one, and a forger's ink leaves whatever the forger's patience was worth. The gum carries iron and gall and a little gum arabic, and the gum arabic came from the north coast trade, and the north coast trade ran on to a schedule, so a court-supply ink from before a certain year has a faintly different feel under the thumb when it is dried: a little more brittle at the edge of a stroke, because the gum had come in from a harbour that had closed its assay that year.

And the tin: every tin of court-supply ink the basin had ever issued had the issue stamped on the lid in the Office's own die, with the year and the batch, and a registrar who wanted to know when a document had been written could lift the colour off the page and read the answer off a lid.

That is what nobody did, and it was the reason the substitution on the Ashgate leaf had survived twenty-six years, and a year and a half on an open shelf, in a building with the door propped.

Mara Quill lifted the colour off the *maintained* with a small lens and a sheet of prepared paper, and read the batch on the lid, and then sat very still for a while with the lamp making a green light on the wall.

The batch was dated twenty-six years back.

She had been in this building eleven years. She knew, because she had signed the receipt, that the Office bought court-supply ink by the gross — a tierce, a tierce and a half — because it was cheaper by the gross and because it kept, and because the store was on the upper landing in a tin that was not locked, and because no officer in the history of the Municipal Memory Office has ever thrown out a full tin of court-supply ink. There is a shortage of nothing. There is a shortage of people, and paper, and appropriations, and there has never once been a shortage of ink.

So the batch number on the lid told her that the page that said a shut gate had no head on it had been written in ink drawn from a tin bought twenty-six years ago, and the Glassstep district survey in the survey press downstairs — the fair copy, held openly, because nobody has ever contested Glassstep, because Glassstep is not a place anybody has ever claimed was false — was written in the same tin.

She did not need to go and get it. That was the thing that frightened her. She had certified three hundred volumes in this building and she had looked at that survey perhaps six times, and she could have told you the hand on it without going down to look at it, and she was not going to be able to explain to anybody, ever, how she could.

It was the hand of her mother's brother.

Wend had been nineteen and out of the registry office for the season and at their mother's table every evening in that autumn with a court-supply pen and a docket of work he had been paid a half-crown a sheet to copy out, because their mother had a bad year and the family did not discuss the bad year. He had sat at the end and written all evening and made a game of it with a nine-year-old girl who was supposed to be practising her letters, and he had let her copy a line of the docket onto a discarded docket, and he had told her that when she was older she would be the one telling people what was true, and she had thought that was a joke about letters.

He had a way of making the downstroke of a *g* lean. Not much. Enough that if you had been taught to read it at a table, you would know it in a document a hundred and forty miles away in a house you had never been to.

The Glassstep survey redactions were in that hand. The names of the district's households had been struck through with a single stroke, in a list, and the reason given in the margin was *consequential correction, terrace row removed from distribution schedule*, and the *c* in *consequential* leaned.

On the Ashgate leaf, the *m* of *maintained* leaned.

Neither of those was the discovery. The discovery was the pattern, and the pattern was not two documents.

Mara laid the fair copy of the Glassstep survey on the bench beside the Ashgate volume and put a sheet of prepared paper over the two of them and drew a key at one-to-one scale, tracing the two hands down onto the same surface so that they lay on top of each other and could be read together.

The strike-throughs on the Glassstep survey were short horizontal strokes, entered from the left, made without lifting the nib and without pausing, in a list of one hundred and four names, and every stroke was the same length because they had been done by a person who was being paid by the sheet and had decided, correctly, that this was not a piece of work to be admired.

The redaction on the Ashgate leaf was different and worse. The pressure figures had not been struck through. They had been *taken*: the leaf had come out and been replaced whole, which takes an hour with a blade and a bone folder and which nobody in this building had done since the flood, and the page that had gone in its place said *chamber void* in the same hand as the Glassstep margin, and the word *maintained* was at the end of the replacement, and the lean was in it.

Four of the readings had been removed. Not all of them. The page that replaced it kept the dates and the keeper's marks and the gate states for eighty years of a shut gate, and it kept the reading column as well, and four of the numbers in that column were five point seven and a bit, exactly as the eighty years before them had been, and one of them — the last, the one in the year before the flood — was not.

Whoever had done it had not replaced the leaf to make the number false. Any fool could have written a false number, and Mara had a shelf of fools' work in her head and this was not it. Whoever had done it had taken a true reading off the record and left a column of dull true readings above the place where it had been, so that the only way to find the odd one out was to already suspect it. The *maintained* was the signature of a man who expected to be caught and wanted, in the end, to be answered.

It was not the work of a forger. It was the work of an office.

She drew the map for forty minutes. Two columns: left, the Ashgate leaf as it stood, ruled out, the shadow lettered in where she could read it and marked *5·89* and *gate shut* and *maintained*; right, the Glassstep redaction column, one hundred and four strokes, the margin, the *c*.

Underneath she wrote the three facts that could be written without anybody having to believe anything.

> *Same batch. Court-supply issue, twenty-six years prior. Same hand. Same office.*
> *Ashgate: readings removed and a clean leaf set in. Glassstep: names removed and a reason entered. Both entered under one distribution-schedule line, both bearing the same correction wording.*
> *The instrument is not a false number. It is a removed true one.*

And then, in a smaller hand, because she was being honest with the paper even where the paper could not defend itself: *One hand, in service across the whole period. That is either a long career or a house that is taught to its children.*

She inked it. She let it dry. She drew it herself, in ink she had brought from her own house, in a hand that took an hour and forty minutes and was nothing like a court-supply hand at all.

The rule was the one that got her. There was a hand-drawing register in the upper room, a ruled book, and every sheet copied by the draughting machine was entered in it with the copyist's name and the date and the number of the original, because the machine could not be trusted with anything a person might later deny drawing. It was not a rule anybody enforced. It was a rule that existed, which in this building was the only kind that mattered.

She took the register down and put it on the bench in front of her, and she wrote the date in it.

Then she put the pen down without writing anything else.

The rule was that a sheet copied by hand is entered with the copyist's name, the date, and the number of the original, so that no one can ever be found holding a drawing that nobody will admit to having made. She had a sheet. She was not going to enter it. If she entered it, the sheet became a document with a custodian, and a document with a custodian in a building like this one could be called for, indexed, compared, and required to justify itself in front of a supervisor who was entitled to ask why a conservator had drawn a one-to-one key of a restricted folio and a district survey at two in the morning.

If she did not enter it, the sheet was a thing that existed nowhere at all. It had no custody, no provenance of its own, and no chain. It was hers, entirely, in a drawer under a tray of brass weights, and if she ever was seen holding it she would be an archivist of eleven years' standing with an unlogged map and no way at all of showing that it was anything other than a thing she had made up in a night of not sleeping.

That was not safety. It was the opposite of safety, and she sat with the register open in front of her for a long moment and understood exactly what she had chosen: she had chosen to be the only person in the basin who could read the evidence, on condition that nobody could check her.

She shut the register and put it back on its shelf with the others, and put the Ashgate volume back in the restricted stack in its own place, and put the Glassstep fair copy back in the survey press in the open, where anyone could take it out, and hid the drawn sheet inside the back of her own bench drawer under a tray of brass weights, and blew out the lamp.

There was a courier on the stair with a crate of commission-supply paper and a bundle of Water Office circulars, and he put the bundle on the mat and went, and she sat down on the floor in the dark and went through it because it was nine pages of the sort of thing she had stopped reading years ago.

Circular twelve of the ration, district returns. Cistern rows and taps, lower terrace.

*Row 7–12, cisterns returned, twelve days. Low pump, terrace road: not running. Blue-step lane taps: not running.*

Mara Quill read that four times.

She did not know where the blue steps were. She had been born in the second house on a lane in a district that is not on any sheet she has ever certified, to a mother who had kept the pump books for a household of canal workers, and she had been eleven years in this building and she had never once, in all that time, been able to find her own mother's lane on a page she had certified.

And the answer to requisition eighteen was clipped to the back of the circular, because the Office attached its answers to the next thing that came through the door, which is how a public office saves the cost of a courier.

She read it in the dark by the light off the stair window. She could make out most of it. She was a reader of ink, and the ink was court-supply, and the hand was not her mother's brother's hand but it was an engineer's, and it was a good hand, and it said that a shut gate under head is a valve, and that the Office can confirm the figure, and that the Office cannot certify the condition of a reservoir it has not inspected in twenty-one months.

And the figure it confirmed was 5·89.

Mara sat on the floor of a dark lower room in Vellum with a piece of paper in her hand and understood that the number was now in two documents, and that the one in the Ashgate leaf was a hundred and eighty-one years old, and that the man who had written the second one had been on a sealed intake at six in the morning with a bar in his hands, and that in a day or two he would have to explain to somebody which of the two numbers had been invented.

And there was nobody else she could show it to. Not tonight. Tonight the only person in the basin who could read what she had drawn was the woman who had drawn it, and she had signed a porter's initials into a book instead of her own, and she had an uncle in the acting commissioner's registry who had written the word *maintained* in a hand that leaned, and who had been nineteen years old at her mother's table and had been paid a half-crown a sheet.

She put the circular back in the bundle. In the morning she would take it to the upper room and staple it to the requisition file, because that was what happened to circulars.

And she would find out, before the quarter turned, whether folio eleven was going into the strong room for good.
