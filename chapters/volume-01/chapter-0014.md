# Chapter 0014 — Nineteen Said No

He had made a list, and the list was the first thing he had built in a month that he believed in.

It was in the blue book on two facing pages, and he had ruled the pages himself with the straight edge, because a ruled column that wanders is a document people stop believing. Left column, the name. Middle column, in his own hand, exactly what the person would be agreeing to — not what they would be *helping*, which is a word that means nothing, but what they would be answerable for. Right column, the date, and then a line ruled under it for a mark, which he had left blank on all nineteen of them.

At the top of the left-hand page he had written the heading he meant to read out to each of them, and he had rewritten it four times.

> *A WITNESS TO THE GLASSSTEP LOW PUMP BRANCH AGREES THAT: the water in the branch comes from the Ashgate intake overflow; that on the twentieth day of the month it ran eleven hours from 16:00 to 03:00; that the head in the branch is 5·89 metres; and that the district has borne salt fever in consequence of it.*

And underneath that, in smaller writing, because he had learned it the hard way from a man at a pump in a lane:

> *And that the person signing may be asked at any time in the next ten years what the water did, and must be able to answer.*

He went up the lane on the morning of the twenty-fifth with the book in his coat and a stick in his hand, and it took him the whole day, and it went the way he had expected it would go and not the way he had wanted it to.

---

Wick was the first, and it took nine minutes and it was not about fear.

"I took that door frame down," he said. "On the first day. The man in the low house with the bed on the frame, whose name is Ondra, and whose boy has got the fever — I took his door frame off the wall and stood it in the water and he has not looked at me straight since the seventh, and he is right. That is the row's whole memory of me. That is what I am in this lane: the man who took the door frame."

"You were trying to keep the grit out of the rooms."

"I was," said Wick, "and it did not work, and I would do it again, and I would rather that be the thing in the yard than the other thing." He looked at the book in Tarin's hand and did not take it, which Tarin was grateful for. "I also work in the Reach from six to two, six days, and I will tell you why I am telling you that, because you are making a list and a list wants reasons. A witness has to be somewhere the water is, or somewhere it went. I am on the Verge road all week. Ask me what I saw on the twenty-first and I will tell you that I saw a cart."

---

Bel Crale's was the refusal that made him sit down on her step, and she gave it standing over the deal table with the lamp on, in the voice she used for reading out a dose to a child.

"You are going to write down that this dispensary is supplied from a branch off a sealed structure and that its keeper is a man with two charges against him," she said. "And the next time the district is inspected — and there will be a next time, and it will not be a warden, it will be a man from the Office with a form — that form will have a box on it that says *water supply in connection*, and I will have filled it in, and it will be the first time in forty-one pounds of this building's life that anybody has been able to say where the dispensary's water comes from."

"I would not—"

"You would, and it is not the point." She put both hands flat on the table. "I have been in this room since the third day of the fourth month. I have told the inspectors nothing that was true. I have told them I keep a bucket and a kettle. That is not a lie, it is an answer to a different question, and it is the only reason forty-one pounds has lasted until the twenty-fifth day of the month and the reason three children of yours did not die in a bowl on the seventh. Do you know how many of this row could be closed on a Tuesday because somebody wrote down where the water came from? Every one of us. That is what *illegible* is for. It is not shameful. It is a wall, and it is thirty years thick, and it is the only thing standing between this lane and a decision about it."

"Sixth of June," said Tarin.

"Say it again."

"Sixth of June. I will not put the dispensary on the list. I want you to hear me say it twice, because I am going to be back and I am not going to forget it."

"Once is enough, and I will hold you to it." She looked at him properly for the first time. "You have a list. Go up the lane and do your list. And when you have done it, come back and tell me how many you got, and I will tell you now that the answer is going to be a condition and not a number, and you already know that, because you worked it out on the seventh day of the month in a yard full of people and you told them so."

---

Old Marden asked for his name to be cut in two.

He was eighty-one and he lived in two rooms at the top end and he came down to the yard on a stick on the twenty-fifth, which was a thing he had not done in a month, and he stood by the cistern with his coat on and did not take the book when it was offered.

"Read your middle column again," he said. "Slower."

Tarin read it again.

"There," said Marden. "*Has borne salt fever in consequence of it.* Now. I was not there on the seventh. I was in this room with a blanket round me and the third child was in the row below and I was told about it by a boy. I have not been in the water. I have not had it on my hands. I have drunk my own butt's worth and boiled it, and I am eighty-one and I am the man in this lane most likely to be dead by the frost, and I would like to say so in writing."

"You can say whatever you want in writing."

"Then I want two lines instead of one." He was not being difficult; he was being exact, in the manner of a man who had kept a household's books in an unlisted lane for fifty years and had a great deal of practice at making a thing true on paper. "One: I am a witness to where this water comes from, because I have heard it in the wall every day of my life, and I was the man who asked the engineer, in front of eighty people, where the water went that came down the run before the pump, and he told me, and that is a thing that happened. Two: I am not a witness to what it does to anybody, because I did not see it and I will not be asked to say."

Tarin Rusk stood in a yard and discovered that a partial witness was a category, and that he had never once in nineteen years of gate work thought to have one.

"That is a hard thing to put on a stone," he said.

"I am not going on a stone. Stones are for the pump. I am going on your paper, and your paper is a piece of paper, and a piece of paper can have two lines on it." Marden took the book at last and read the middle column again with his finger on it, and then he wrote the two lines himself, in a hand that was worse than Tarin's, and signed the bottom of them, and gave the book back. "There. Now it is true and I have said what it is not."

---

Marda Ollan signed, and made four conditions, three of which he argued with and lost.

"One. I have kept the keys to the top end since my mother died and I count that as keeping the route, so put it there," she said. "Two. I am not a witness to the running. I am a witness to the houses. Three. If anybody comes to this lane with a form, I am the one they come to, because I put my name in a box on the twenty-second and I am not going to pretend to somebody else in a good mood that I did not." She took the book. "And four. You write the date. You write the actual date, today, in your own hand, under my name, and not the day you asked me."

"That is a strange thing to ask for."

"It is not strange, it is a record," she said, "and you are a man who has spent a month learning what happens to a document with nobody's name and no date on it. I have kept two rooms of keys for nine years and I could tell you the date of every one of them, and the reason I can is that I wrote them down, and the reason I wrote them down is that I did not trust anybody to come and ask."

---

The Tall boy signed because he had been the one who could not find the top of the lane, and he gave a reason that took the yard's breath out of itself for about four seconds.

"I was the one," he said. "On the seventh. Doctor Sen said it and I sat there and I did not say it because I was ashamed, and then somebody said it and then I did say it, and then everybody knew, and my mother made me say it in front of the row. So I am the one, and if there is a place on that paper for the person who got lost on the seventh, put me in it, and I will say that I lost the top of the lane and that the lane was the same lane and I did not know, and that is the only thing I know about any of it and it is the only thing I am good for."

Tarin wrote it and the boy's spelling of *occasionally* was wrong and he left it wrong.

---

And then there was the boy of nine, who came up off the coping with his bit of chalk and asked whether he could put his name on the end of the list, and Tarin said no.

He said it quickly, and then he said it again slowly, and he said it in front of Marda Ollan and the Tall boy and, unforgivably, in front of the boy's mother, who had come out of the door at the sound of her son's name.

"No," he said. "Here is why, and I would like it written down, and I would like you to be angry with me about it afterwards and not before. The middle column says a witness can be asked at any time in the next ten years what the water did, and must be able to answer. You are nine. In ten years you are nineteen, which is old enough to be angry, and you would have to answer a question about a thing that happened when you were nine, to men with forms, about water you were not in the room for. And I will tell you what the answer would be. It would be *I was nine and somebody asked me to*, and that is a true answer and it is worth nothing, because the whole point of a witness is that nobody can say that about him.

"And the other half, which is the half that matters. If the only second hand on this route is a nine-year-old, then the second hand is a family. The route is held by the Roeg household, and the Roeg household can be found, because a woman wrote it in a box on a form on the twenty-second, and when the route goes wrong, which it will, the first thing anybody does is come to this house and ask why the boy was on it." He shut the book. "No. Not today, and not in ten years, and not if I have to come back and ask you again."

The boy's mother said, "You have been in this lane a month and you have just told my son that he is not a person who can be asked a question."

"Yes," said Tarin.

"That is what I would have said."

"Then I am sorry, and I would not change it, and I have not said it to make you feel better."

---

He was on the step outside the dispensary at dusk on the twenty-fifth with four names in a book and a fifth, the boy, in brackets with a line through it, and the book open on his knees, and he did the sum, and the sum was not the sum he had made on the cattle track.

Twenty-two households. Two had said no to the petition outright. He had asked twelve. Nine had said no to a witness, and every one of them had said it for a reason that was better than Tarin's reason for asking, and three had said yes with conditions, which is not the same thing as yes.

He wrote it up the way he would have written up a fault, because that was the only shape he had.

> *Witnesses, glassstep low pump branch, 25th day.*
> *Ollan, M., top end, keeper of two rooms, nine years. Houses, not running.*
> *Marden, A., top end, eighty-one. Source, not consequence. Two lines, at his own insistence.*
> *[Name], age 19, dispensary lane. The seventh. Answering for having been lost, which is the only thing he can answer for and it is not nothing.*
> *Bel Crale, dispensary. Refused. Reason sound, and mine is not better.*
> *Wick, thirty-eight. Refused. Reason sound.*
> *Others not asked, having already answered.*

Then he wrote the condition, and it came out of him without his having decided to write it, which was how the true things got written.

> *The condition on all four, stated five ways and not one of them by accident, is: I will not be the second hand for a route I cannot stop.*

And underneath it, because he had been an engineer for nineteen years and could not help the last two lines:

> *That is Roke's reason. Roke gave it once, in a yard, in front of a warden, on the twentieth, and did not soften it, and it has walked round this lane by itself and arrived at every door before I did.*
> *A route with a name, a record, and a keeper can be held. A route with a man in it cannot be held, and cannot be shut, and does not stop when the man goes home.*

He read that and then read it a second time, and then he understood the last part of it, and it took him a while.

He had spent five days treating the missing second hand as a missing *person*. He had gone up and down a lane looking for somebody. And the four people who had signed had all, without ever meeting, refused the same job on the same grounds — which was that the man who stands a hand on a route has to be able to shut the route, and the man who shuts a route is the man who stands at the head of it, at the wheel, on the works, at the gate, in the dark, at four in the morning.

There was no such man in this lane. There could not be one. The lane had kept its own pump for thirty-one years without a keeper's mark on any sheet in the city, and what it had actually been doing for thirty-one years was keeping the *end* of something. The other end was up a sealed hill behind a gate that was chained, and the wheel above it was loaded with five point eight nine metres and could not be shut by anybody standing here, and every man between here and there drew a wage out of the office that had charged him twice on two different days.

He had been looking for a neighbour to hold the route for him. The route did not need a neighbour. The route needed the other end.

Niko Venn was sitting on the wall with his boots up on the coping.

"You have got a face on you," Niko said.

"I have four names and one condition and the condition is the second end of the pipe."

"Right." Niko took his boots off the coping. "Then I will tell you what you have got, and I do not say it to be clever, I say it because you have been asking the wrong people. You have not got a list of the willing. You have got a list of everybody in this lane who has something to lose in this lane, and you wrote it out yourself about four hours ago and you do not like the look of it. Every one of them has a trade and a family and a landlord or a board that can shut them, and the reason nine people said no is not that they are frightened, it is that they are *employed*. You cannot ask a man with a job to be answerable for a head of water."

"That is the truest thing anybody has said to me this month and you are twenty-two."

"I am twenty-two and I have been left in a lane for a month with nothing to do." Niko swung down off the wall. "You want me to say it and I will say it: the second end of that pipe is the filter house under the works, and there is a way in off the quarry side that is not on any survey in this city, and I have told you about it and you wrote it in the book and underlined it."

"You told me on the fourteenth."

"I told you on the fourteenth because you were the only man in the basin who could have sent anybody. Are you going to send anybody?"

"No," said Tarin.

"No. And can you pay anybody?"

"No."

"Say it out loud. I want to hear the word."

"I have no money," said Tarin Rusk, "and I have no authority, and there are four men in a city of forty thousand who could sign a descent order and all four of them are in a building that has already put two charges on me."

Niko nodded slowly, as though a thing he had expected had arrived on time.

"Right," he said. "Then I am going to go and find out what a man has to have to go down there, and you can write it in your book, and then you can sit on that step and say the word *no* again in a fortnight. Because somebody is going to say it a great deal more often than you are going to, and I would like to know the number." He got up. "I will want rope. I will want a lamp with a hood, and a second lamp, because one lamp is a man who has fallen down a hole. And I will want somebody to hold the end of the rope, and that is the one that is going to be difficult, and it is going to be difficult because a man who holds a rope for somebody is a man who is on the record for it."
