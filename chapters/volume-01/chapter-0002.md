# Chapter 0002 — The Warden's Road

The warden's station on the terrace road was a hut with a slate roof, one room, a stove, a bench, and a board nailed to the outside wall that carried more of the law of Cresset than the courthouse did. Tarin had walked past it four times a week for four years and had never once gone in to report a thing he was not asked to report.

He went in now because he wanted a row.

Pell was behind the counter, which was a length of scaffold plank on two flour barrels, and he had a sheet of oiled paper in front of him and a stub of pencil and the settled air of a man who had already been in the same mood since six in the morning.

"You are early," Pell said.

"I am early."

Pell put the pencil down. "Then you have come for something, and it is on a schedule or it is not on a schedule, and either way I am going to write it down. What have you got."

Here is the thing about a sluice warden, and Tarin had learned it the way you learn the shape of a stone step in the dark. A warden is not an engineer and not a judge. A warden decides what gets written down, and what gets written down is the only thing that has ever really happened. Pell did not want the story. Everyone brings a warden a story. Pell wanted a condition, because a condition has a head and a gate state and a direction, and a condition can be entered on a line and be true in ninety years, and a story about a district that is not on his sheet is a story.

Tarin put his bar against the leg of the bench and did not sit.

"Five point eight nine metres of head above the gate seat at the Ashgate intake, live on the stilling tube, pulsing. Inspection gate unsealed and the main wheel is loaded. The channel above the gate is taking water and running it off the hillside into the covered terrace run."

He said it in the flat voice he used for crews in the rain, and he left out one thing, and the leaving out was a decision he made while he was doing it, standing there, with the stove ticking.

Pell's pencil was already moving. "Unsealed means open."

"Means open."

"By whom."

"By a bar," Tarin said. "At eleven minutes past six this morning, which is before the check."

Pell wrote that down. He wrote slowly, because Pell wrote everything slowly, and when he had finished the line he read it back the way he always did, and the reading-back was the moment Tarin understood that he had just put his own hand in a document.

"Seventeen days ago you signed four lines in this room," Pell said. "On those lines you wrote *structure void, no measurable pressure*. I countersigned them. My mark is on them and it is four feet from your signature."

"I know what I signed."

"Then somebody is going to read those two pages side by side." Pell put the pencil down and looked at him with no enjoyment in it at all. "I am not going to pretend to you that I am sorry. Water is in a channel I cannot reach. Where does it go."

"Under the terrace. The covered run at level four. It comes out at the lower fold, in a low arch on the mill side."

Pell pulled a board-tray out from under the counter and ran a finger down the column. He was not a fast man. He got to the fourth level, and stopped, and went back, and stopped again lower, and then he took the tray out entirely and put it on the plank and opened a second tray, and by the time he had opened the third tray Tarin had understood what was happening and had gone cold from the waist up.

"Nothing," Pell said. "Nothing receives at level four past the fold. Nothing is listed on that stretch. There is no row there."

"There is a row there. I heard people at the arch."

"Then they are somewhere else and the water is somewhere else, and a channel with a head on it does not care which." Pell set the trays down. "I cannot give you a row I do not have. If I wrote a row on the board it would be a row I invented, and in six years somebody would come and ask me where I got it, and I would have to say I got it from you."

He was not being clever. That was the difficulty. Pell was being exactly as careful as he was always, in the way of a man whose whole value is that nothing he writes is ever found to be made up.

Tarin felt the shape of the next hour arrange itself and he did not want it. "What is the outfall on that stretch?"

That is the thing he should have asked first, and he had spent twenty-one months writing *no settlement beyond* on a line that meant he had never asked.

Pell reached under the plank and came up with a board, a longer one, in three sections, and he laid the middle section flat on the counter and turned it round so the printing faced Tarin. It was a schedule, not a map: a numbered list of terrace outfalls with their state, their last service date, and their keeper. Most of them read *in service* with a keeper's name and a date in a hand that had been keeping them for years. Number fourteen read differently.

Tarin read it twice.

> *14. Terrace overflow, row 7–12, lower fold. SEALED, PLATED, NO KEY ISSUED, OUT OF SERVICE. Closure entry 118 of the standing schedule. No keeper assigned. No service window. Row removed from the distribution sheet, same entry.*

"Same entry," Tarin said.

"Same entry as the row coming off the sheet. I do not maintain fourteen, engineer. Nobody maintains fourteen. There is no key, there is no keeper, and there is nobody to answer a complaint from, because the schedule that put it out of service is the same schedule that says there is nothing at the other end of it to complain." Pell put a finger on the entry number. "That is dated twenty-six years. It is the only line on this board that old."

He looked up.

"I have walked that terrace twice in six years, for the wall, not the drains. I would have told you the water had somewhere to go if I had looked. Nobody made me look."

He said it the way a man says he has been wrong: without a great deal of interest, and as though it were the only part of the conversation he was going to be permitted to have.

Tarin looked at the number and the row and the twenty-six years and understood, in the flat unhurried way he understood head loss, that he had a courtyard or a lane or a set of steps somewhere below him where a hundred and fifty feet of residual head had exactly one vessel to go into, and that the vessel was a cistern, and that when the cistern filled there would be nowhere at all.

"That's a plate," he said. "That's not a grate. That's a plate across the outfall."

"I have never seen it."

"You don't have a key."

"I have never had a reason to want a key to a thing nobody maintains." Pell's voice stayed level, and something behind it did not. "You will want to say now that this is somebody's fault, and it may well be, and I am a warden and not a magistrate, and my business is that there is a channel with a head on it and no row at the end of it. That is all I have."

He slid a printed sheet across the plank. Tarin recognised it before it stopped moving: a *notice of uncontrolled channel condition*, one page, with a printed box near the foot headed **cause**.

"Post that before the light goes," Pell said, "and I will put it on the board, and the district it names gets a warden at the arch in the morning. Leave the cause box empty and I post it anyway, and a notice with an empty cause box reads as ignorance, which is cheaper than a lie and worse than a mistake. Fill it in and it reads as a breach, and a breach at a sealed works on a day you were on the platform before six is a matter for the commissioner's office and not for me."

Tarin took the sheet.

It was good paper. That was the part he would remember. Good stock, the kind the Office bought by the ream for anything that had to survive a winter, and the box for the cause was two inches deep and printed in the same court-supply hand as everything else the city had ever written down.

"Right," Pell said. "Which row."

Which row.

He stood there with the sheet in his hand and the whole afternoon in front of him, and he thought about the arch, and the sound of a bar going into a door frame, and a name being said over and over to somebody who was not answering, and he thought about the box.

If he wrote *sealed inspection gate opened by bar at 06:11 by T. Rusk, senior flow engineer, no authority* then that box would do what boxes do. It would put his name and a number and a date on a piece of paper in a hut on a road, and the water office would open a file on it before dark, and the file would be about the Ashgate works and not about the arch, and the arch would go on doing whatever an arch does while the file was read.

And if he did not write it, then the form would go on the board with an empty box, and the district at the end of it would get a warden in the morning instead of an investigation, and he would have gone down there in the meantime and looked with his own eyes at whatever a hundred and fifty feet of head does to a place with a plate across its outfall.

He put the sheet into the inside pocket of his coat, squared the flap with two fingers, and did not write in the box.

"Level four," he said. "I'll have the row for you by the time the light goes."

Pell's eyebrows went up a fraction. "You'll walk it."

"There's nobody else to walk it."

"Mm." Pell drew the third tray back under the plank and shut it, and pushed the pencil into the tin, and did not say anything else, and Tarin understood that he had just been permitted to leave by a man who would be thinking about the empty box for a week.

The door was propped for air and the light outside was a hard flat white. On the step, where he had been sitting with a requisition rolled in his fist like a rolled cigarette, Niko Venn got up off the stone.

"There you are," Niko said. "You've been in there twenty minutes. I signed for six lengths of lead pipe at the mill and I have got to get it down to the Reach before the light goes or the Sallow cart will be up at four and I'll be stood there in the dark looking competent."

"You are standing there in the light looking idle."

"That's a good word for it." Niko rolled the requisition over and tucked it into his belt. He was twenty-two, quick with his hands and quicker with his mouth, and he had been Tarin's apprentice for eleven months, which was long enough to know that Tarin would rather carry something up a hill twice than ask for a hand. "What's Pell doing?"

"Counting things."

"He counts things." Niko came down off the step and fell into step, which Tarin had not asked him to do. "He asked me where the fold was, actually. Before you got there. He wanted to know if there was anybody living in the row past the mill culvert, and I said I didn't know, and he said that was a stupid answer, and I said it was the only one I'd got."

Tarin kept walking.

"He was right, though," Niko said. "There is somebody. Everybody down there knows there's somebody. They call it the Fold, or the blue steps, or nothing at all, depending which side of the terrace road you're standing on. The Water Office calls it nothing. I've carried pipe past that arch so many times I could do it blindfolded, and nobody's ever once asked me what's at the other end of it, and if I'd told you there was a row down there you'd have said the sheet says there isn't one, and you'd have been right, and we'd both have gone on carrying pipe."

Below them the covered run came out of the fold's north side and went under the terrace, and the terrace road went on down past the mill and turned off toward the Reach road, and the path that went down into the lower terraces was a cattle track with a gate on it and a padlock on the gate and a notice that the gate was not a water gate and was not to be used for water.

Tarin took the track at a fast walk. The light was going white and long behind the ridge and the wind came up off the dry bottom of the fold with a smell of dust and hot stone and something underneath it, faint and strange, that was not the smell of a drought and had no business being in a district with no name.

"Get your pipe down," he said over his shoulder. "And Niko."

"I'm going."

"Level four, past the mill. If you ever run a load past that arch, look at what comes out of it."

Niko stopped. He looked at his master's back, and then at the arch at the bottom of the fold where the run came out under the low stone span, and where the light was coming up off the water and the people who were standing in it.

"Right," he said, to nobody much, and went off up the road with his pipe.
