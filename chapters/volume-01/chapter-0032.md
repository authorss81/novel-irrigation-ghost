# Chapter 0032 — The Answer In Her Own Hand

Niko Venn and Tarin Rusk went up the mill road at half past four in the morning of the seventeenth with a lamp, a pail, a string, a rule, and a sheet in Tarin's coat that Hanne Roeg had put in his hand at the fourth step the day before and had not let him read on the step.

"You will read it to them," she had said. "That is the whole of what it is for. I am not writing it for a drawer and I am not writing it for the examiner and I have not written it in a good hand, because it took me until about nine and my hand had gone and I would not pretend it had not."

There was no mist at the top of the mill road and the city was in the valley with the light going on it, and the eleven houses on the works were awake, because in a row of thirty-four people where the water comes up out of a kitchen floor at a hundred gallons an hour, somebody is always up before the light.

Nell Drust was on her own step with a pail of the morning's first draw in her hand when they came round the corner, and she looked at the two of them and at the lamp and said:

"You have come about the water."

"I have come to tell you what is under your houses," said Tarin Rusk. "I have been under them. I want an hour and I want you to do the rest of your day, and I am going to say the whole of it in your own yard in daylight with the doors open, and if anybody wants to send me down the hill before I have finished they can and I will not say one word about it."

"Say it here first," said Nell Drust.

She listened to about four minutes of it in her own doorway with a pail in her hand, and then said: "Are you telling me my well is off the same water."

"Yes."

"Then get in," said Nell Drust, "and get that lamp off my flags. I have a floor in here and I am not having a lamp put on it by a man with no office."

---

They put a bench out in the row because it was warmer and because thirty-four people cannot hear a man on a doorstep, and by the time the sun was an hour up there were twenty-eight of them sitting or standing and the rest were inside, and Tarin Rusk told it in the order he had worked out on the road down, which was the order of things a person could check.

He told them about the adit and the two turns and the four hundred yards of it, and about the ledge and the kerb and the line cut in the rock at the water by a man who expected to want it again. He told them the measurement, twice, out loud, and made the girl who had brought the pails a year ago write it on a slate while he said it, because she had a good hand for figures and because he wanted it in the row and not only in a book in a city four miles away.

Then he told them what was over their heads.

"Nine feet four inches of water above the floor of the adit at its mouth, and the floor of the adit at its mouth is the floor inside the filter house door, and the ground outside that door is one foot nine inches above that floor, and so the water stands eleven feet one inch above the ground outside the door of these works. Under this row. Under the flags of these houses. Under the yards and the lane and the bottom end where you have all been dumping things for thirty years."

Nobody shouted. He had told himself in the second mile of the mill road that somebody would shout, and he had got four things ready for it, and the row did not shout, because a row of thirty-four people who have drawn their water out of a hole in a kitchen floor for thirty years is not a row that has ever had a reason to be frightened of what is under the houses. It is the only thing they have ever had.

A man at the back said: "Is the floor going to go."

"I do not know," said Tarin Rusk. "I will not tell you. I have been in it for four hours and I am a man who has never had a floor come up in his life and I am not going to guess at yours. What I can tell you is that the chamber is intact and it has not moved and there is no wet mark on the ledge and no weed and no drift, and that the roof of it is a floor of flagstones on putty with your hearths and your chimneys in it, and that a man cut a line in the rock at the water line a hundred and eighty-one years ago, which means somebody expected to want to know where it was again, and it has been exactly where he cut it ever since."

A woman said, "Whose hearths."

"Yours," said Tarin Rusk. "It is not one house. The flagging changes direction where the light reaches and the level of the floor is not the same all the way along, so it is a street and not a house. There is a cut lead stub of a floor drain in it forty feet along. And there is a stair in it, going up in eleven steps, that comes up through the roof of that chamber to a door under a store, and the store has a hearth in it and a shelf of candle ends, and a girl of about fourteen has been sweeping that store out on a Monday for a year and never once thought that was remarkable."

The girl at the well-head, who was about fourteen, said there was the yard brush, and went home.

---

Then he came to the part he had walked two miles and a half to say, and he had not led up to it, because leading up to it would have been a way of making it easier for them and he had promised himself on the mill road that he would not do that.

"There is a pumping plant on this works. It is not new and it is not strange. A beam pump and a horse gin, four years old on the contract, and it has been pumping out of that chamber for four years through a rising main off this works and a covered channel that comes down the terrace road on a foot in the hundred, and it delivers into a cistern in a lane a mile and a half away, in a district of a hundred and twenty-six people, and a person put a hand into that chamber on the seventh day of this month and it works and it is at five point eight nine metres at the main wheel and it cannot be increased by anybody.

"It was stopped on the night of the second, by this office, without a letter. It has been stopped fourteen days. And it is going to be started again.

"Before I say the rest of that, I want to say the number, because I gave it in that lane two days ago and it is the only number I have got. Before the pump ran, the well in Nell Drust's kitchen came up six gallons in three minutes and thirty seconds, which is a hundred and three gallons an hour, and I checked it twice, and there is a slate in that lane with it on it. It is a kitchen. It has never needed a pump and it is not going to be dry while I am alive.

"And when a pump is drawing out of a body of water, that body is not standing in the same place. It is the same water. It is not the same yield. A body that is drawn from gives up less, and it gives up what it has on its own surfaces first, and I have got a physician in that lane who will tell you that she has not got an instrument that could tell you by how much and will say so in a yard, which is what she does.

"So I have come up this hill to tell you two things and I have not come up it to take anything from you and I am not going to ask you for one drop, and I will not be back asking. One: the water you have been drawing for thirty years comes out of the chamber under your houses, and that is not a fault and it is not a debt and nobody in my office put it there and I do not know who did. Two: the well may change, and I cannot tell you when, and I cannot promise you it will be the same water in a month, and I have put that sentence in a signed document two days ago where a hundred and forty people heard me say it, so you will not have to take it on a boy's word on a hill."

Coll Preece had been standing at the back with his arms folded and he came forward about two paces.

"You have come to tell us the pump is the man in my kitchen," he said.

"I have come to tell you the pump and the kitchen are on the same water," said Tarin Rusk. "That is all I have come to say, it took me four hours to find out and I have been down there twice, and it is the only true thing I have."

"Right," said Coll Preece. "Then here is what that is, and I am not going to be dramatic about it, because I have a boy on a staging who has not been paid since the second and I have been four years on that plant and I get paid by the can.

If the pump goes, the well goes down. I do not know how long. I have had thirty years of that water and never had to think about it, and now there is a thing on top of it four miles away in a city that took a month to send a man with a form. And the office that shut me off on the Wednesday sent no letter, and I am not going to pretend I am sorry about it, because I would have shut it too, and I have said that to a man with a lamp in a store and I would say it again.

But I have not been paid since the second, and I have a boy on a staging at night for a plant that is not running, and the answer to that is not a speech at a bench. The answer is a horse and a sweep and somebody at the wheel at four in the morning, and I am not asking you for the horse."

"I know what a horse costs," said Tarin Rusk. "You have not asked the yard for anything, and I would like it written down in this row on a slate that you did not ask."

"I do not write slates," said Coll Preece. "I fill cans."

---

They were still sitting there when Tarin Rusk got the sheet out of his coat, and he stood in front of a bench of twenty-eight people in a row with no row in any schedule in this city, and read out a question he had not written and an answer he had not agreed to, in the order they were on the paper.

> *Whether a district with no row may ask a settlement with no row for water. Asked on the twelfth day of this month, in a letter, in my own hand, by Hanne Roeg of the fourth step, of the district of one hundred and twenty-six persons at the lower terrace road, to the sluice warden, for the district book.*
> *And on the seventeenth day of this month, in the same hand, and by the same person, and I am not a warden's clerk and I am not an office, and I am going to be asked for this sheet.*
>
> *No. We will not ask them for water while the well at Ashgate has never been tested by anybody, and while there is standing in a document signed by a man four miles away that he cannot promise that well will be the same water tomorrow.*
> *And I will put the reason down as well, because a refusal without a reason is a sulk, and mine is not a sulk. We are not refusing them because they are a row with no row on a schedule. We are refusing because on the ninth day of this month a physician told a yard of a hundred and forty people that every drop of water in that lane has come out of a works pump two miles up the mill road, and the people at the end of it are not the people who started it, and we have been drinking it for sixteen days, and four of our children have the marks and a seven-year-old girl at the second step is at the beginning of the fourth stage, and I will not do to a settlement of thirty-four people with a boy on their staging and a man unpaid for a fortnight what was done to us, and I will not do it with a bucket either.*
>
> *I want the examiner to be told there is a settlement at Ashgate. I wrote the question in a letter because I did not know the answer and I am putting the answer on the same paper because I do now, and I am seventy-one and I have a grandson at a school in the fenner district and he is ten and he does not know any of this is going on, and if a man comes down that track with a form he is going to find out about a grandson whatever I do, and I would rather it was in my own writing than found out of a book.*

Nobody in that row said anything at all for about a minute, and it was not the silence of a row that had been frightened. It was the silence of thirty-four people looking at each other.

Then Nell Drust stood up off her step, and went into her kitchen, and came out with the pail she had been holding since before the light, and put it down in the middle of the row on the flags, in full.

"Take that back down with you," she said. "No. Wait. You are not taking it. You are not taking it because you will not have it, which is different, and I want the difference in whatever you write down.

"Thirty-four of us have had a hundred and three gallons an hour out of a hole in my kitchen for thirty years and never been asked for a drop of it by a living soul, not the city, not the office, not the water people, nobody. And there has been a pump on those works taking the other end of it for four years, and I never knew it, and it never crossed my mind to ask whether there was a difference between the two of them, because there has never been anybody in a position to make me.

And now there is a district of a hundred and twenty-six people two miles down this hill with a form coming, and they are the first people in thirty years who have ever been in a position to ask, and what they have done with it is write a letter, ask a question, and then write down the answer themselves, which is the one thing nobody in an office ever does.

you can put it on that slate and it does not oblige me to a drop

And that is my answer, said out loud in front of twenty-eight people, and I would like it wrote down, because I have learned this month that a thing said in a yard is worth about a week."

Niko Venn took out the slate and the chalk and gave it to the girl with the good hand, and she wrote it, and Nell Drust watched her write it, and said, "Is that all of it?"

"That is all of it you said," said the girl.

"That is what goes down," said Nell Drust, "and I want my name on it and not yours. I am thirty-nine and Hanne Roeg is seventy-one, and I have never once been on anything, and if there is going to be one name from this row on a piece of paper then it is going to be mine and not hers and not a boy's."

They came down off the hill at about eleven and Tarin Rusk carried the pail in his right hand the whole two and a half miles and did not put it down, and at the fourth step he put it on the flag and handed Hanne Roeg the sheet and asked her whether it had gone in all right.

"And I have read your face and you have not read the second half of it yet, and I would take it up the mill road again to do it, and you will not, and I am not asking you to. Sit down. You have been up since four."

---

On the morning of the eighteenth the dispensary drew its clean water, and it did it in one day, and it was the last of it. Yara Sen had been holding the eighty gallons four days on purpose and said so out loud on the fourteenth, in front of Bel Crale and two children, and the arithmetic was in her book with the date on it, and there was no argument about it.

> *80 gal, bought the 13th at 1 shilling the gallon, £4, from the fenner turn. Held 14th, 15th, 16th, 17th. Drawn 18th, 70 gal. 10 gal remaining. Nothing clean in this lane from the 19th.*

The day went the way such days go. Bel Crale boiled what she needed and gave the rest out in threes, and it did not last to the middle of the afternoon, and a woman from the top end said there had been a man at the arch at ten offering a shilling for a bucket, and that the doctor should write the ten gallons down and nothing else.

Yara Sen wrote the ten gallons down and did not write anything else.

At about four she came out of the dispensary and crossed the yard to the cistern, where the coping had a chalk line and a date on it because a boy with a stick and a bit of chalk was doing a count nobody had asked him to do, and she put her hand flat on the stone and looked at the water.

"Four hundred and seventy gallons," she said.

"Four hundred and seventy," said Niko Venn. "Four days ago it was nine hundred and fifty, and it was four thousand seven hundred on the second, and I have written every one of them down with a date on it and I have not missed a day and nobody has told me to and nobody has thanked me, and I would like the doctor to say out loud in this yard that this chalk is the only instrument in the district, because I have read what a form is for and I would like somebody to say the other thing out loud for once."

"I am not going to say that in a yard," said Yara Sen, "because a boy with a stick is not an instrument, and the day this lane starts calling things instruments instead of instruments is the day a man comes down that track with a form and gets told that the form is one of them."

She crouched down by the coping and looked at the water, and her hand went into it up to the wrist, and she held it there, and then she took a little of it out in a stoppered jar from her bag, because she had brought the jar down with her and had been carrying it about for four days waiting for something to happen to.
