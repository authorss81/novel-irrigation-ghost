# Chapter 0042 — What The Return Does Not Say

The Open Channel Guild's report on the works at Ashgate came into Cresset on the Wednesday morning of the thirtieth day of this month in a brown-paper wrapper with a Guild seal on it, and it was a **return**, which is a thing with a number on it and a price at the counter.

A Guild return on a works is filed by the district that contains the works. There is no district at Ashgate; on every sheet in this city Ashgate is a structure with a closure date and a warden's seal and a store with a lock on it. So the return went, as returns do, into the warden's book on the terrace road, and was entered on the day's page by a clerk who wrote *received, Guild return, works at Ashgate, no district* and did not ask anybody anything.

A Guild return on a works is filed by the district that contains the works. There is no district at Ashgate. There has never been a district at Ashgate; on every sheet in this city Ashgate is a structure with a closure date and a warden's seal and a store with a lock on it. So the return went, as returns do, into the warden's book on the terrace road, and it arrived at about ten in the morning and was entered on the day's page by a clerk who wrote *received, Guild return, works at Ashgate, no district* and did not ask anybody anything.

A Guild return on a works is filed by the district that contains the works. There is no district at Ashgate. There has never been a district at Ashgate; on every sheet in this city Ashgate is a structure with a closure date and a warden's seal and a store with a lock on it. So the return went, as returns do, into the warden's book on the terrace road, because the warden's book is the only book in this arrangement that a Guild can be certain of being able to open, and it arrived at the sluice warden's station at about ten in the morning and was entered on the day's page by a clerk who wrote *received, Guild return, works at Ashgate, no district* and did not ask anybody anything.

Sluice Warden Pell did not read it in his office. He read it in a yard on the terrace road at eleven o'clock in the morning, in daylight, with about sixty people in it, and he did it because that is what he does with other people's documents and because nobody in this city has ever been willing to do it for him.

He read the whole of it, twice, and he stopped four times to let people argue, and the yard argued three times and did not argue once.

---

> *Return of inspection, Open Channel Guild, works at Ashgate, levels one to four, with the settling tank and the adit and the filter house. Three men, a surveyor with a level, and a hired day-labourer for one day. Ordered from above the Guild on the nineteenth day of this month. Days on the works, the twenty-second to the twenty-fourth.*
> *The works are sealed. The main wheel is shut and chained, the chain and the dogs and the stem are sound, and there is no evidence of recent operation at the wheel.*
> *Level four, at the end of the drainage adit off the settling tank. The adit is driven in the rock, sets squared, every fourth set good. The chamber is tooled rock in courses with the chisel marks standing. A dressed ledge six feet wide on the north-east side with a nine-inch kerb set, and the kerb is sound and was set by somebody who wanted the water stopped and not the people.*
> *Water in the chamber, clear to the bottom as far as lamp carries, standing, no drift, no weed, no wetting of the ledge. A single line cut in the rock at the water line, one inch deep, one foot long, cut and left.*
> *Level by string and rule, twice, by the surveyor: nine feet four inches above the floor of the adit at its mouth. That floor is the floor inside the filter house door. Outside ground at that door is one foot nine inches above that floor. The chamber therefore carries eleven feet one inch of standing water above the outside ground at the door of these works. Unchanged at two visits eleven days apart.*
> *Roof of chamber: not rock. Underside of a floor of flagstones on putty beds, with a hearth and flue and chimney breast, a cut lead stub of a floor drain, and a wooden stair of eleven steps ending in nothing. The flagging changes direction where light carries. It is a street and not a house. The underside of a flagstone is warm.*
> *Stair from the chamber to a door under a store: two hundred and forty-one steps, counted twice by two men. The store is cleared, swept, raked, tarpaulin down. It has a hearth and a shelf of candle ends, and it has been swept on a Monday for at least a year.*
> *Record stone in situ at the head of the ledge, set into the rock at the height of a man sitting. Name cut once and cut again in front of the first set, shallower, half an inch out of register, the second being the better work. Second face blank and not cut. The stone is the twin of a slab on a pending-disposal slip in the record city and is not the same object as it.*
> *Store, first level. Gauge board, chalk, contractors' hand, reads 5·7 for two years and 5·9 for the two years above that. There is a plate in the store and the plate is of a larger bore than the plate it replaced, and the change is not written anywhere, and a plate is a part and not a work. The board is a day-book and not an instrument.*
> *Deal box in the locked store, opened in the presence of the surveyor. Two certificates, both in the acting commissioner's hand, one at decommissioning and one four years ago, both certifying the chamber void, no residual head, no standing water, no maintenance required, both with the word* maintained *beneath in the same pen. Neither sheet states the day it was made. Neither sheet names the person who made the measurement. Neither sheet refers to a plate, a bore, or a method.*
> *Plant: single-cylinder beam pump and horse gin, four years on the contract, pump rod into the bore, a fresh-sawn oak chock under the beam's heel. Resumed at about twenty past three on Sunday the twentieth by the contractor in person, with a horse out of his own pocket, and not paid since the second. The gin is turning. The sweep is about eleven feet across and the count is regular.*
> *Settlement on the works: eleven houses, thirty-four persons, about thirty years, no row, no schedule entry, no keeper, no map. Blue doors, which is common to this basin and is not a finding. The row's well comes up on its own slab on the kitchen floor of one house, six gallons in five minutes twenty seconds, checked again at five minutes twenty-five, and grit in the bottom of the pail which the pail has never had. It was one hundred and three gallons an hour on the eighth day of this month. It has never been tested by any person and there is no instrument in this basin that would test it. Nobody had told the row that a chamber existed under it and the row is not to be told by this return.*
> *No district is served from this works. There is no district to be served from it. The Guild maintains works and not districts and will not enter a supply connection with a party it cannot name.*
> *Four hundred and three sheets, bound. Filed.*

---

The yard took about a minute on the last line, and then a man near the back said, out loud, the thing that about fourteen other people were thinking and had not said, and his name was Old Marden and he was eighty-one years old and he had been in that lane for seventy.

"So it has got their name in it," he said. "Eleven houses and thirty-four people. And it has not got ours in it."

"It has not got yours in it," said Sluice Warden Pell.

"And it has got the *girl* in it," said Old Marden. "*A girl of about fourteen who writes.*"

"It has got a description of a person in it," said Pell, "and I read the whole of it out and there was no name, and I am not going to stand in this yard and pretend a description is not a person, because it is, and because she is fourteen and she is in a return in four buildings now, and that happened on Friday and it was done by four people in a yard on this road who read the whole of it out in daylight before they sent it up, and one of those four is at this gate and anybody who wants to say a word to her about it can say it to her face."

Nobody said a word to her face, and Tarin Rusk, who was at the back by the arch with a bad shoulder and a blue book, understood that the warden had just done the only useful thing anybody could do about it, which was to name it in a yard in daylight, and that it was worth about a week.

---

The report went into four buildings in a fortnight: the warden's book, which has four copies; the Guild counting-house at Vellum, which holds eleven thousand four hundred leaves; the register of the district examiner, because a return is produced to an examining officer on request; and the papers of the Basin Assembly, because returns are laid before the Assembly four weeks before a review and the midsummer review was set for the fifteenth of next month.

That last one is the part that mattered and nobody in the yard understood it at the time. A return naming eleven houses and thirty-four persons on the roof of a chamber eleven feet under the flags of those houses cannot be held back from a ration review. It is a return. It has a number on it. In four weeks it is on a clerk's table whether anybody in the lane wants it there or not, and the only question is whether anybody is standing next to the table when it lands.

That last one is the part that mattered and nobody in the yard on the terrace road understood it at the time. Tarin Rusk understood it at about two in the afternoon, standing in a lane with a copy in his hand, and he worked it out twice and it came out the same both times and he was not glad about it.

A return naming eleven houses and thirty-four persons on the roof of a chamber eleven feet under the flags of those houses cannot be held back from a ration review. It is a return. It has a number on it. In four weeks it is on a clerk's table whether anybody in the lane wants it there or not, and the only question is whether anybody is standing next to the table when it lands.

The copy was what the district actually read.

Returns are printed at the Assembly counter at a penny a sheet, and by the Thursday afternoon there was one nailed to the door of the dispensary with a corner wet, and about nine people had read it standing up in the lane, and every one of them had read the same one sheet in the same order and compared the same lines, which is the thing Marda Ollan had said in a yard a fortnight ago and had said for no reason anybody could remember at the time.

The copy is the report. It is the same report, and it has four more pages than the warden's copy because it has the schedule of what was in the store, and the schedule is a list of object against dimension, and the fifth item down is a planed length of oak, sixteen inches, with a groove cut down one face and a pin hole drilled at the head, and against it, *found in a pack in a store at the works, owner not present, not entered as a work.*

Tarin Rusk read that item standing in the lane with a penny of his own — no money at all — and read it again, and then went and found Marda Ollan and had her read it aloud to two witnesses, and wrote it out in the blue book, because the reading of a list aloud by three people in a room is worth more in a fight than a reading of it by one man in a head.

"That is mine," he said.

"That is yours," said Marda Ollan, "and it is in a list in four buildings, and nobody has a copy of the pin, and the pin is in your pack, and the log is in your pack, and you cannot put a log in a gate, and that is the whole of your engineering and it is item five on page eleven of a return you can buy for a penny."

"Yes."

"So do not stand in that lane looking at it," said Marda Ollan, "because there are four people behind you reading it over your shoulder and one of them is going to work out that the engineer named in the charges owns a piece of timber that somebody has put in a list, and by the middle of the afternoon that will be in a yard and not in a book."

---

The part of it Bel Crale could not put down was the last line of the section on the settlement, and she said so in the dispensary doorway at about four in the afternoon with a sheet in her hand and eleven people listening.

"They have written the number in," she said. "Eleven houses. Thirty-four persons. The girl with the good hand. The well at sixty-eight and the grit in the pail and the fact that nobody had told them, in their own words, *the row is not to be told by this return*, which is the politest sentence I have ever read about a thing that ought to have been said out loud in a yard a month ago.

"And they have not put us in it. Not one word. Not the lane, not the cistern, not the tank, not the four thousand seven hundred, not the man. There is a line in there that says *no district is served from this works, and there is no district to be served from it*, and that is the truest line in four hundred and three pages because it is a form talking about itself.

"So I want to say the thing I have been turning over since eleven o'clock this morning and then I am going to boil water, because there is a girl at the second step who has been at the fourth point for ten days.

"Iven Marrow made that man an offer on the twenty-third: a place, less money, every record in this basin, eleven weeks at a bench at Bexhill, and the price was one line, that the blue steps is not in the report. Nobody in this city broke that price, and nobody in this city could have stopped him keeping it.

"The form kept it. That is all. He wrote down the truth about a works and the form had a field for a supply district and there was nothing to put in the field, and the man was right about all three of his reasons and the third one is the only one that has ever cost anybody anything."

---

On the Thursday night Tarin Rusk did a thing he had not done in six weeks, which was to open the bench book on his knees in a room over the back stair in a low house at the left of the arch, and to copy out into the blue book, in his own hand and with a date on it, the whole of the return's schedule for the store at the works, item by item, because a thing that has been written down twice by two men in two rooms is a thing that can be produced.

Then he wrote four lines under it, and the fourth was the one he had written on the sixteenth day of this month at the top of the mill road and had to say again because a reader in four buildings was going to have both in front of them.

> *30th, this month, night. From a return at one penny.*
> *Item 5: a planed oak, grooved, with a pin hole. Found in a pack in a store at the works. Owner not present. Not entered as a work. That is mine and it is not in a gate and it is not going in a gate and there is no man in this city with the authority to put it in one.*
> *Item 9: two certificates, the acting commissioner's hand, chamber void, no standing water, no method, no name of a measurer, and it is now item 9 of a list that four buildings hold, and it was in a deal box in a store that nobody in this city has opened in six years, and it has been read out in a yard of sixty people this morning by a warden who reads other men's documents in daylight because it is the only thing he has.*
> *And the thing the return does not say, and which is on page two hundred and nine in the same hand as the board, is that a plate of a larger bore changes a figure somebody wrote in a day-book. It says the board is a day-book and not an instrument. It does not say that about the certificates, because the certificates are not a day-book, and the man who wrote the return knew the difference and wrote it in twice, once for the board and once by not writing it at all.*
> *Nobody has asked me whether a plate explains a certificate. Nobody is going to, and I am not going to be the one who asks, because I would rather find out from somebody who has to stand in a room about it than from a man with a bar and a book who is owed nothing.*
