# Chapter 0038 — The Number A Man Invents

Mara Quill gave evidence on the morning of Friday the twenty-fifth day of the month in a room over the sluice store on the terrace road, and she came to it on a cattle-track cart with a printed summons in her bag and a bad night's sleep, because a Vellum day is a day and a night each way and she had not been in this city since the eleventh.

Dunn Averell examined her. He did it carefully and he did not ask her a single question about Tarin Rusk's competence in the first eleven minutes, and when she got to the eleventh minute she understood what he was doing and let him.

"Miss Quill, you are a conservator in the Municipal Memory Office. You are not an engineer, you do not hold a district appointment, and you are not here as an officer of this office."

"No."

"Then I am going to ask you about documents, and I am going to try very hard not to ask you about a man, and if I do, I would like you to tell me and I will stop."

"I have a condition in writing that says I do not use another person's method in public without telling them," said Mara Quill. "You are not using my method. Ask me what you came to ask."

He asked her what he came to ask.

She established in about nineteen minutes that the volume she read on the eleventh was the Ashgate intake maintenance series, folio eleven, one hundred and eighty-one years, seven hundred and twenty-four leaves, four entries a year, withdrawn to restricted handling on the seventeenth of the month before on rule nine; that the last entry, dated the year before the flood, carried a head at wheel of 5·89 and the word *maintained* beneath it in a second hand; that the leaf carrying it had been removed and replaced by a leaf of later stock, sizing and ink; that the pressure shadow of the removed leaf carried at the end of the last line a schedule reference of three digits reading 118; and that the closure schedule of the lower terrace road, entry 118, dated twenty-six years ago, carried the plating of the terrace outfalls of rows seven to twelve and the removal of that row from the distribution schedule.

She was asked, twice, whether the reading 5·89 in that last entry was a true reading.

"I cannot say that it is true," said Mara Quill. "I can say that it was written, and that it was written in a hand and a sequence that is the same on four leaves a year for a hundred and eighty-one years, including one in the first year of the book and one in the last. I can say that the leaf beneath it carries the pressure of the same pen making the same shape. What the head was doing, I do not know, and I am not an engineer, and nobody in that room is going to be able to make me into one, and I would like it minuted that I declined to be asked whether five point eight nine metres was the height of anything."

"It is minuted," said the chair.

"What is not minuted," she said, "and I have asked for it to be, is the sentence about the hand. My certificate does not transcribe the reference. Any person may compare it against the closure schedule in the warden's office, which is a public document. I have made that document falsifiable by anybody who can read, on purpose, because a document only one person in this city could check is a document I wrote about my own uncle."

Nobody in the room said anything at all.

"His name is not in it," said Mara Quill, "and his name is not going to be in anything else of mine, and I have not said it in this room, and Mr Averell, if you want to make me say it in this room, you will have to put it to me as a fact and I shall have to say whether it is one, and I would rather you did not, because a name in a room like this is a name in a book in four buildings, and I have had one this year and I am not having two."

"I am not asking," said Dunn Averell.

"Then ask him," said Mara Quill. "That is what he is for."

---

Then he asked the question, and he asked it well, and the room had been built for it.

"Miss Quill. I have been retained for the applicant in this matter and I told him on Thursday that I was not his friend. I am now going to ask you a question that I have been turning over since the eleventh of this month, and I am going to ask it because I have to know which way to file a mitigation, and I cannot file a mitigation I do not understand, and I would like the chair to hear the question in full so that nobody can say afterwards that I worded it.

"Is his judgment sound."

Mara Quill looked at the table for about three seconds.

"I am going to answer that in three parts, and I would like the three parts minuted separately, because they are three different answers and two of them are not about him."

"By all means."

"First. I cannot answer it and I will not be made to. I am a woman who can read a shadow. I have never in my life made an engineering judgment about a head, a gate or a grade, and Mr Rusk has never once asked me for one, and on the eleventh day of this month he stood over my bench for three hours while I read a number out of a leaf, and he did not tell me once what he thought the number meant until after I had finished and been read back by a second reader. A man with a sound judgment about that would have been in my ear inside a minute. I do not know whether that is modesty or arrogance and I have thought about it and I have not decided, and I would not have decided for a certificate."

"Then what can you answer."

"His record-keeping," said Mara Quill. "And I want to use the whole of my time on it, because he has not got anybody else who will say it and I have got four minutes.

"In eleven years I have never had a person from that office sit at my bench and hand me a figure I did not have to check. I have had two. One of them is a woman who does the fenner returns and who is not here. The other one was him, on the eleventh, and he put a corrected figure in a public register in the same hand in the same book, with a note that it was so entered at the applicant's correction, applicant present, and he asked for that, and I want the chair to understand that no clerk in nineteen years had ever done it for anybody. He did it because he had worked out that a register is read by comparing its lines, and a line that has been struck with nothing beside it is a line nobody can check.

"And he filed his own certificate and refused to have mine transcribe the reference, and he put three names on the desk list and not one on the document, and he wrote six numbered paragraphs and signed them on the fifteenth of this month in a yard in front of a hundred and forty people, which is the first document he has signed since the tenth of this month, when he was stood down, and I have read all six, and four of them are against his own interest, and I would like the chair to have that in the record as well as the rest of it."

"And the second part," said Averell.

"The second part is that there is a sentence about him in a register in that city that I read on the eleventh day of this month and have thought about every day since, and I am giving it to you because it is the honest answer to your question, and because I have signed my own files this year and I am not going to let somebody else's file be the only one in this city that has been kept clean."

"Miss Quill—"

"Consultation thirty-four," said Mara Quill. "Register of restricted consultations, quarter, against folio eleven of the Ashgate intake maintenance series, the eleventh day of this month, ten o'clock until twenty past one. Purpose, eleven words, and then a remark in which the applicant stated that the purpose was had for a district, and the clerk asked for the district's name and its number of houses and its number of persons, because a name in a register with nothing under it is a thing people say is a joke.

"He said twenty-four houses and one hundred and nineteen persons. The count is twenty-four and one hundred and twenty-six. It was taken on a slate on the twenty-second by the district itself, by door, by a woman who had made herself the easiest person in the row to find on purpose. The one hundred and nineteen is not a clerical error. It is the number a man says about a place that is not on a sheet, and he had it in his mouth before he had the true one in his mouth, in a public room, with a clerk waiting, and he did not know he had done it until I read it back to him.

"It is in the register. It was struck with one stroke in ink and the true figure written above it, and the correction is beside it in the same hand, and it is in four copies of that register and in a copy of the entry in a building in this city, because that is what a register is. He told me on the eleventh that he wanted the correction in the same hand in the same book because that is how a register ought to be read. The correction is in the same hand. So is the error."

The chair looked at Tarin Rusk for some time.

"Mr Rusk. Do you wish to comment on the number one hundred and nineteen."

"No, sir," said Tarin Rusk. "It is a true account of a thing I did. I reached for a number about a district of no row before I reached for a number I had counted myself, and I did not know I had done it until a woman read it back to me in a public room. It is struck, and the true figure is above it, and the correction says who made it. I have nothing to add to that and I would rather it went in as a sentence than as a defence."

"Miss Quill," said the chair, "is there anything else in that register?"

"There is a note about the margin," said Mara Quill, "which is dated a Tuesday in the second month of next year and says no further action is proposed at present, and which was put there against a provenance note about two documents. It does not apply to what I filed on the eleventh, because the eleventh is not the same instrument, and I filed it anyway knowing it was there, and I have said so out loud on this record. If anybody in this room wants to look at the note, it is a public document in a public register and they are entitled to it."

Nobody looked at it.

---

Averell asked one more question, and it was the one he had told her he would not ask, and he asked it anyway, and it cost him more than it cost her.

"Miss Quill. In your condition, you and Mr Rusk agreed that neither of you would produce the other's evidence without telling the other first. You told him before you came here."

"I told him on Thursday afternoon, on the terrace road, at four o'clock, in the road, with a cart going past," said Mara Quill. "That is condition four and I am saying it out loud so that it is on this record and not in a corridor. I told him the first time as well, in a room in Vellum in the spring, before I had any idea there would be a hearing. I have told him every time. I have never produced a thing of his in a room without his knowing it first, and I have never used a method of his in a room without saying so, and I would like the chair to understand that I say so at some cost, and I say so because if I do not do it then the only honest account of the two of us is that one of us is holding the other, and that is not what this is."

"Then I withdraw the question I said I would not ask."

"You said you would try very hard not to ask me about a man," said Mara Quill. "You asked me about a man for eleven minutes. That was good, and I would like it minuted that it was good, because a man who examines a witness properly is worth two men who do not, and this office has one."

---

The chair rose for eleven minutes at twenty to one. Averell asked for the third charge sheet again and was told, for the third time in writing and the fourth time in a corridor, that it would be when it was ready. He said nothing further about it, and Ilse Roan wrote nothing further about it, and the sheet did not exist, and a form about a man's descent into a hole had become a conversation in an office that kept a bundle of papers about everybody.

In the corridor on the terrace road, with a cart going past and the wind off the wall, Mara Quill said four things and did not say a fifth.

"The Guild team was at the works on the twenty-second and they are still there, and I know it because a surveyor with a level came to the public desk in Vellum on the twenty-third and asked whether an engineer's statement of the fifteenth is a filed document or a private one, and I told him what it was, and he wrote the name of the office down in a book he has not closed yet.

"And the examiner you have been promised will not come early because you asked. It will come on the date it was always coming on, and I will tell you why for nothing, because you will not like it and it is better that you hear it in a road than find it out in a month. There is a Guild team on that works and a district condition in a warden's book and an engineer's statement going up to the commissioner. Five documents about one chamber in ten days, in a city that has had no document about that chamber in six years. If a district examiner walks into that on the twelfth of next month, this office does not read it as a district with a chamber. This office reads it as a complaint with a man's name on it, and it will delay it, and it will do so without deciding to, because there is a queue and a queue is not an act.

"So the thing that would get the examiner here faster is the examiner being early, and the only thing in this basin that makes an examiner early is somebody who asks for it in a way that costs that office something. And there is not one of those things in this lane, and I have looked, and the closest thing is a letter from a woman of seventy-one that asks for two things and one of them she knows cannot be granted, which is the cleverest thing I have read this year and it will not work."

"Anything else."

"Two things and then I am on the cart." She had her hood down and the wind was coming off the wall onto her face. "I am going to say the register entry again at your office in a fortnight, in writing, with a copy, because I am not going to have it be true in a room once. Condition four says I tell you first and I am telling you first, and I would like you to say something about it, and you are not going to, and I would like to have asked.

"And the other one. When the room asked whether your judgment was sound, you were not asked it, because it was not about you, and the woman who had to answer it had to tell that room about a number you got wrong in public, and I have thought about that on a road for a day and a night and I do not know whether it was a kindness to you or the opposite, and I have not been able to decide, and I have decided to file it under the second one, because you had nobody else who was going to be in that room and I was in it, and that was never going to be a gift."

"I know what you did," said Tarin Rusk. "You did not have to do that."

"No," said Mara Quill. "I did not. It is condition four and I would do it again, and do not thank me for it, because I told you first, and that is the whole of what I did."

She got on the cart. He stood on the step of the sluice store with the summons in his coat and the bench book in the inside pocket and a wind off the wall, and he thought that nobody had thanked him for anything in a month, and that a woman of thirty-one had gone up a cattle track to a room in a city four miles away to be the only person in it who was on his side, and the only person in it who had put a true thing about him into a book.

He had not asked her to. She had told him first, on condition four, in a road, with a cart going past.
