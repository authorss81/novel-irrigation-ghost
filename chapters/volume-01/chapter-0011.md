# Chapter 0011 — A Yard Full Of Water

The watchman came at first light with a lamp he did not need, because the sky over the ridge had already gone the colour of a wet slate, and he stood in the doorway of the cistern house and said that by the regulations a man under a stand-down was to be brought out and walked to the second gate, and no further, and that he was sorry about it.

"That is a great deal of regulation for a walk of four hundred yards."

"It is not a walk of four hundred yards where you are going," said the watchman. "That is what it is a walk *from*."

Tarin Rusk got his coat and his bar and the tin pot out of the dry corner and followed him out, and the cistern house door was stiff, and the sound it made shutting was the sound of a building that had not been shut by a person in a long time.

They went along the inside of the wall to the second gate, and on the outside face of the gatehouse, at eye height, on a board that had three other things on it that had been there longer, was a sheet of good paper with a wax seal on the corner of it.

He had slept eight feet from that sheet for one night.

He read it standing up, in the flat early light, while the watchman pretended to be interested in the road.

The sheet was an order. It set out that Tarin Rusk, senior flow engineer, was stood down from the lower terrace district with effect from the tenth day of the month, and that the seal, the district reference, and the keys to the lower terrace works were surrendered, and that he was not to go past the second gatehouse on the terrace road without a warden. It was courteous. It was four paragraphs of courtesy.

Beneath it there were two numbered paragraphs, and the thing he had never quite got used to, after eleven days, was that the office had found it convenient to number them.

*1. Tampering with city records, arising out of a recalibration log filed on the second day of the month. Preferred on the tenth day of the month. Open.*

*2. Unauthorized opening of a sealed structure and service of water from a reservoir the ledger records as closed, arising out of a sluice warden's entry of the twentieth day of the month, right-hand column, 16:00. Preferred on the twentieth day of the month. Open.*

Two subjects. Two dates. Twenty days between them and a completely different thing in each, and a man at the top of a building somewhere had understood that well enough to number them, and had then printed them on one sheet of paper with one wax seal, which is what made it look, from three feet back, like one charge.

At the foot, in a smaller type: *The hearing in respect of the above is set for the twenty-second day of the month.*

And under that, in ink of a different hand, in a clerk's careful loop: *Adj. — no date entered.*

The twenty-second was the day after tomorrow. It was the day the midsummer ration review was supposed to be sitting, which meant the review had been moved, or had moved itself, and the hearing had gone into the hole it left.

Tarin took his hand off the paper.

"Was that on the board yesterday?"

"It was on the board the day it was nailed up, which was the twenty-first at four in the afternoon, after you'd gone down the lane." The watchman said it without any relish and without any particular blame, which was somehow the worst version. "I came on at eight. I read what's on the board. There is a lot on the board."

"Right," Tarin said.

He walked out through the second gate, and the cattle track went down into the fold, and the whole of the lower terrace was lying on its left hand in a light that was going to be beautiful by nine o'clock.

---

They had put the water out.

That was the first thing. There were pails along the wall of the low house from the fourth step to the arch, thirty-eight of them by his count on the way down, arranged in a line the way you arrange chairs for something, and the yard cistern was up to its coping and dark and still, and the three tanks were full and covered with boards and rugs, and somebody's washing was already on a line between the second and the third door, and there was a smell of woodsmoke off the low house that had not been there yesterday.

A boy of about nine was sitting on the cistern coping with his legs hanging over the edge, holding a bit of chalk.

"Morning," Tarin said.

"Morning." The boy got down off the coping. "It stopped at three, and Dad's been up ever since, and there's about four hundred people' worth of water in this yard and I counted it twice."

"Counted it how?"

"Chalk." He held it up. "I mark the coping and I count the marks in the morning. Dad said the water came up a finger's width every ten minutes yesterday and I said that can't go on all night and he said it stopped by itself, so it did stop all night, more or less."

It was the boy from the gatehouse. Tarin had not asked him anything else and the boy did not appear to expect to be asked anything else, which was, on the whole, the most restful thing that had happened to him in twenty days.

"Go and get your breakfast," Tarin said. "Somebody in this house owes you one."

"I'm not hungry."

"Go and get your breakfast anyway."

The boy went. He had, Tarin thought, exactly the right amount of respect for him and no more, which was more than anybody else in the district had managed.

He got the stick out of his bag, and he did what he would have done on any morning of his working life, which was to go round the vessels and find out the state of them.

The three tanks were full. Not full to the brim — full to the line where the boards sat, which was two inches under, and the water under the boards was warm and had gone a colour that he did not like. The yard cistern was at the coping. The thirty-eight pails ranged from full to a third, and the empties were upside down along the wall drying, and there were four more pails in the row above and two buckets under the dispensary awning, and when he added up what he could see and made the round twice he got a figure he had already written on the back of a ward notice on the twentieth day of the month and had carried down the hill in his coat pocket ever since, as a piece of arithmetic and not as a hope.

Six thousand seven hundred gallons. Three tanks, the cistern, and every vessel in the row.

He wrote it on the back of his hand in pencil, because he had left the notice in his other coat, and then he looked at the number and understood the thing he had not understood at four o'clock the previous afternoon.

There was no room.

Not in one vessel. In any of them. The cistern that had taken eight feet of water in eleven hours and stopped at the balance point was standing at its coping with eight feet of water in it and no headroom at all, and the tanks were the same, and every pail in the row was the same. The eleventh hour's worth of water was already in the district. The window had not bought a cistern with room in it. It had bought a cistern with nothing left to put anything into, which is a completely different thing and is the whole of what a service window is when you have not got a second one.

He stood in the yard with the number on the back of his hand and the good light coming up over the ridge, and it occurred to him that the most successful eleven hours of his working life had ended with the district holding six thousand seven hundred gallons of water and no way to spend a drop of it, and that the reason was that a man with a stick and a pail and no office in the city of Cresset had calculated the arithmetic very carefully and had never once asked the only question that mattered, which was what the water was *for*.

Yara Sen was sitting on the dispensary step with a slate on her knees and three stoppered jars on the flags beside her, and she had been there long enough that the dew had got into her boots.

"Three hours," she said, without looking up. "You are the third person to walk round the vessels. The first was Doctor Sen's patient, who is seven, and who walked round them twice, and who is now sitting on the pump step with his feet in them."

"I am sorry about that."

"You are sorry about it about nine seconds later than you should be and I have not got the time for it." She put the chalk down. "I have three jars and they are all the same water, and I took them at the cistern, at the second tank, and at the fourth pail along the wall, and the cistern is the worst of the three and the fourth pail is the best, and I can tell you why, and the reason is evaporation and time, and that is the entire good news I have."

"How bad is the cistern."

"It is a salt load I would not put in a horse." She said it flatly, the way she read out doses. "I have let a jar stand since four this morning and I will show you the crust at noon, and the crust is the whole of the argument, and if anybody in this row wants to argue it with me they may come and look at the lid at noon."

"And the tank."

"Less bad and going the same way. And the pails, the pails are the best of it, because the pails have been boiled, or most of them, and because they have not stood in one place for eleven days." She looked up. "Tarin. You have a face on you like a bill."

"I have emptied the headroom out of every vessel in this yard."

"You have not done anything of the kind. Nothing has emptied anything. The branch ran eleven hours and stopped itself at three o'clock this morning, and the vessels are exactly as full as they were when it stopped, and they have been losing water to the air every hour since, which on a yard this size in this heat with no wind on it is about fifty gallons a day, and which in nine days is four hundred and fifty gallons, which is nothing." She turned the slate round. "The row is not short of water. The row is short of water it is allowed to give anybody."

That is the sentence he would carry for the next thirteen days, and he did not know it yet.

---

The argument had already been started three times by the time he got the stick down, and it started again on the fourth, and it is worth saying how it went, because it did not go the way anybody expected.

Tam Roke put it first, and put it plainly.

"On the seventh," he said, "that pump ran all morning, and ninety-odd people drank off it, and the water was warm and it tasted of a pipe and my mother said it was not our water, and three children were ill after it, and the three children are better. Doctor Sen has been coming down this road for three years telling us our water is bad and she has never once been wrong. So I am not saying she is wrong now. I am saying that three children took a mouthful and lived, and that there are one hundred and twenty-odd people on this lane who have not had a mouthful of anything that is not this, since the ninth day of the month, and that I would like somebody to explain to me what the plan is."

"That is the plan," said Yara Sen. "There is no other plan. You have no water that is not that, and you have not had since the ninth, and the cistern has been filling with that since the first, which means that anybody on this lane who has drunk from the yard in twenty days has had a mouthful of it, and a lot of people have, because it is the only water there is."

"So it is a slow one." Wick had come out of the low house door and was standing in the yard with his sleeves rolled. "The seventh was a mouthful. This is a mouthful a day."

"It is smaller than a mouthful a day. It is about two ounces a head a day across a hundred and twenty-six people, boiled, and that is what the pails have been doing since the twenty-first, and nobody is going to be ill of it tomorrow." She said it with the flatness of a woman who had said something like it before. "It is going to be four ounces by the end of the week, because that is all there is, and *somebody is going to be ill of it in ten days*, and the children go first, and the children go first because they are smaller and because they are drinking it boiled and drinking more of it per pound than any of us."

Old Marden, from the top step, said the thing that turned the yard over.

"It is a quarter of an inch a day and none of you can taste it," he said. "You are all sitting in a yard listening to a physician talk about what a jar of it does when you boil it, and the cistern is at the lip of the wall, and there is a boy standing on the coping with a bit of chalk. Say the number out loud. How many days has the cistern got left in it if nobody drinks any of it at all?"

Nobody wanted to be the one to say it.

"About a hundred and thirty," said Tarin. "Six thousand seven hundred gallons in this yard and fifty gallons a day going out of it whatever anybody does. Cistern, tanks and forty-four open pails; the pails make it worse and not better, and it does not matter who is thirsty, it is going whether it is drunk or not. Six thousand seven hundred over fifty is a hundred and thirty-four days, and then there is nothing in this yard at all and the tanks will have gone in a month."

"A hundred and thirty days," said Marda Ollan, from the wall, "of the water that will put my children in a bowl. Thank you. That is a great deal of comfort and I would like it in writing."

"That is fair," Tarin said, and it cost him something to say it in a yard, and he said it anyway, because the alternative was to stand in a yard and be a man who thought the arithmetic was the answer.

Hanne Roeg had not moved off the fourth step. She had the mat over her shoulder and she had not beaten it, and Tarin understood that she had not beaten it because it was not afternoon, and then understood that she had understood the same thing about him, and then wished he had not got there.

"The cistern is not the problem," she said. "The cistern is the easiest thing in this yard. The cistern is water we can see, and water we can see we can argue about. What is the problem is that this row is not a row."

---

That was where the runner came in, which was either the worst timing of the month or the best, and Tarin never decided which.

He came down the cattle track at nine in the morning on foot, which meant he had walked from the mill, and he was carrying a satchel with the district returns in it, and he was about seventeen and he was embarrassed.

"Pell sent me," he said. "I'm to do the returns. I'm to do seven to twelve and the blue-step lane and the low pump."

"What do you have for the blue-step lane?"

"Nothing, sir. There's no row for it."

"There's a yard of houses."

"There's no *row* for it," the boy said, and he said it the way you say a thing you have said at a counter four times this week. "I'm to do seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven and twelve, and then the low pump on the terrace road, and then I'm to come back down here, because Pell said to come back down here, and I said there's nothing to do down here, and he said do it anyway and put it down as no return, and I said what do I write in the box, and he said leave it blank and bring it back."

He put the satchel on the wall and took out a form.

It was a single sheet, printed, four boxes across the top and a ruled column down the side. Tarin read it upside down from where he stood, the way you read a warden's entry book, and it was, in its way, the most honest object he had seen in a month.

> *DISTRICT RETURN — CISTERNS AND TAPS.*
> *Row no. ____ | Keeper ____ | Tanks ____ | Gallons remaining ____ | Last serviced ____*
> *Remarks.*

Four boxes. A row number, a keeper's name, a count of tanks, a count of gallons, and a date. A district return is a thing that says: on this day, this row, this keeper, this quantity of water, was the fact the city was entitled to rely on. It is a promise about the future made by a person with their name on it.

"There is nothing in that form for what is in this yard," Tarin said.

"No, sir."

"You have been sent down here to fill it in."

"Yes, sir."

"And you cannot fill it in, and if you leave it blank the Office will mark the day's return incomplete, and if you write something in it that is not true, you have forged a city return, and you are seventeen."

The boy looked at the form and then at the yard, at the yard cistern standing at its coping, at the pails along the wall, at the eighty-odd people who had by then come out of the doors to find out what was happening, because on a lane with no news anything happening is the news.

"Sir," he said, "if I put a number in the row box and it is a number that isn't right, nobody ever knows, because there is no row. That is what Pell said. He said, if there is no row, then no row can ever be wrong."

That was a sentence worth having and it was given away for free by a boy of seventeen on a cattle track, and Tarin wrote it in the blue book that evening in three lines, which was more than he had written in eleven days.

"Do not put a number in the box," he said. "And do not put my name anywhere on it, because I am under two charges and one of them is about a document and I would give any man in the commissioner's office the excuse of a lifetime."

"Then it goes back blank," said the boy, and put it in the satchel, and Tarin watched him do it and understood that he had just watched a form with four empty boxes go back up a hill, which was the entire history of this row written on one sheet of good paper in a boy's hand.

Pell came down at eleven.

He came on foot, with no clerk, and he had walked the terrace road and come off it at the mill culvert, and he was out of breath and did not pretend otherwise. He stood in the yard and looked at the cistern and the pails and the lane and the row of blue doors with the washing out on them, and Tarin watched a man who had spent nineteen years counting channels work out what he was looking at.

"Right," Pell said.

"That is the whole of it," Tarin said. "You can count it yourself. Thirty-eight pails in the yard and four up the row and two under the awning, three tanks and the cistern, and it comes to what I wrote on a notice on the twentieth, and there is no headroom in any of it, and the branch stopped on its own at three o'clock this morning with nobody standing in it, and I was not there and there was nobody there."

"I know. I have the entry."

"Then you know there is no route record. Pell, the entry is a notice about an uncontrolled channel condition with an empty cause box, addressed to no district, and it is the best document this city has about where this water came from, and it is not a route record, and it is not a service window, and it does not put a single drop of water on a schedule."

"No," said Pell. "It doesn't."

---

He stood in the middle of the yard the way a man stands in the middle of a room where he has to say something he has thought about on the walk down.

"Here is what I can do," he said. "I can enter a condition. That is my whole trade. A condition has got a head in it, or a state, and a place, and a clock, and I can put it on a board in an office and it stays there. I did it on the first with your notice and I would do it again tomorrow." He turned and looked at the cistern. "I cannot enter a district. To enter a district I need a row number and a keeper, and the row number comes off the distribution sheet and the keeper comes off the closure schedule, and neither of those has your lane in it, and I have looked, because I walked in on the twenty-first and I took the third tray out and I put it on the plank and I went down the whole column twice."

He stopped.

"There is a thing I can do that you will not like," he said. "I can file a return about the blue-step lane with the boxes empty and the remark filled in. And that return goes into the district book on the twenty-third, and it is a city document, and it is dated, and it will say in a warden's hand that on the twenty-first there was water in the blue-step lane and that there is no row and no keeper and no schedule entry against it. Which is a true sentence, and which is on paper, and which will be in the file for as long as anybody keeps a file."

"And what will that do?"

"Nothing," said Pell. "It will do nothing whatsoever, and I want it understood that I am offering it to you knowing that it does nothing, because in nineteen years the only thing I have ever found that does anything at all is writing a thing down, and the things that get written down are the things that later people can point at, and I have got nothing else to give you this morning and I have thought about it all the way down here."

He was not being kind. He was being exact, and being exact was the only thing he had.

---

The light had gone from gold to white by the time the row started to work again, and Tarin stood against the wall of the low house with the stick in his hand and the number still on the back of his hand, and he did the thing he had been avoiding since first light.

He made a list. He did it in the blue book, on the page after the one with the arithmetic, in the order in which the things were needed.

*Water.* Have it. Six thousand seven hundred gallons of it, in vessels, on this lane, this morning.

*Head.* Have it. 5·89 at the Ashgate main wheel, measured, two places, mine and a page a hundred and eighty-one years old.

*Trace.* Have it. The branch, my own hands in it, and the run coming down the wall.

*Record.* Have it. A stone at the pump plinth with a route name and a date and a man's crooked mark under the date. One face. The other face blank and I cannot cut it.

*Keeper.* Have it. Tam Roke, forty-four, who keeps the pump, and who said no to me in this yard in front of eighty people and a warden, and who was right to, and who has said no to nothing since because he has been too busy.

*Standing.* Have none of it. Two charges, both open, no seal, no keys, no reference, no crew, and a regulation about a gatehouse.

He read that page from the top and understood, sitting against a wall in a yard that was full of unusable water in the ninth-best light of his life, that five of the six lines had a tick against them and that he was not short of a route. He had had a route since the seventh of the month. What he could not make, and had no purchase on whatever, was the thing that turns a route into a service, and the thing was a name in a book.

He could solve for water. He had done it twice in twenty days with a chisel and a length of oak, and it had worked both times and cost him both times. He could not solve for a name, and as of the morning of the twenty-first a name was the binding constraint on every single thing he could do, including the one thing he was best at.

Hanne Roeg got up off the step. It took her a while and she did it without a hand on the cane, which Tarin noticed and would remember.

"Then we will be a row," she said.

The yard was not quiet. He would have been surprised if it had been.

"How?" said Marda Ollan, from the wall.

"You write to them," Hanne Roeg said. "The warden's office. You write and you say there is a row on the blue steps and there are one hundred and twenty-six of us and we have kept a pump for thirty years and we are asking to be put in the book. That is all that is. That is a letter."

"Who signs the letter?"

The old woman got to the bottom of the four steps, and stopped, and looked round the yard at the people on it, and Tarin watched her do the arithmetic that he had been doing for twenty days and had not got a good answer to, which is that the people who had said no were not cowards and the people who had said yes were not available.

"Then one of us signs it," she said.
