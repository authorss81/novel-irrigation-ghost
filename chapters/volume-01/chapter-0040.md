# Chapter 0040 — The Yard On A Sunday

There was no examiner on the cattle track on the Sunday, and there was not going to be one until the twelfth of next month, and the sheet that said so came up on the Saturday afternoon in the bag with Bel Crale's boy and a weekly delivery of nothing much, and it was one page and it had two dates on it and a box.

> *Office of the Acting Commissioner, water and channels, twenty-seventh day of this month. In reply to the communication of the fourth step, and to the condition entered on the fifteenth day of this month, and to a request in a letter of the twelfth day of this month that a district examination be brought forward:*
>
> *The midsummer ration review will be held on the fifteenth day of next month. This is a date and it is not a concession, and this office has no power to alter it.*
> *A district examination will be held on the twelfth day of next month, which is the earliest day this office has offered to any district in this quarter, and the office notes that the request of the twelfth day of this month was made on a day when the condition had not been received and had not been read, and the office does not accept that a request founded on a matter not yet before it can be pressed, and does not refuse it.*
> *And this office invites the district to state, in its own hand, the matters it wishes examined, and will conduct the examination in the order it is given them, and will not add to them.*
>
> *Matters wished examined. [ ]*

Marda Ollan read the last line out in the yard on the Saturday evening at about seven and then sat down on the step she usually sat on, which is the fourth step, which is Hanne Roeg's, and which she sat on because Hanne Roeg was inside and had a cough and had gone to bed at six.

"There is a box," said Marda Ollan. "There is a box at the bottom of a page and it is empty and it is the only empty thing on that page, and every other line on that page has something in it, and the empty one is the only one we are allowed to write on."

Nobody slept much in the blue steps that night.

---

They had it on the Sunday from eleven, in daylight, in the yard, and it went on for four hours, and Marda Ollan kept the time on her own because a thing that is decided at four in the afternoon in a yard of eighty people is a thing that half of them did not hear.

The tank was at the coping. It had been at the coping since the afternoon of Sunday the twentieth and it had not gone down by a measurable amount since, and that was not because nothing was coming out of it; it was because everything that was coming out of it was going into a lane and a shaft that had been full since before anybody in that lane was born. Niko Venn had written the figure on the coping every morning since the ninth and on the Sunday morning the twenty-seventh there were two numbers on the coping, four thousand seven hundred, and under it, in his own hand: *not a reading of a stable tank.*

The pump at the top of the lane was shut and had been shut since seven o'clock on the Saturday morning, and it would be shut on the Sunday, and it would be shut on the Monday, and the queue that turned at the arch had forty people in it on the Sunday morning who had brought pails and had been told, by a man with a bound hand standing beside his own pump, that it would be shut on the Monday as well, and who had gone away with the pails.

---

Three things were said in the yard and all three of them were said more than once.

Bel Crale said the box should be filled with the water. She said it standing in her own doorway, and she was the woman who had run a room in this lane on forty-one pounds for a season and had kept it illegible on purpose for a year, and she had argued against spending the money on the twelfth and spent it, and she argued things out loud and then did them anyway.

"Four thousand seven hundred gallons in that tank," she said, "and eleven gallons of the last rain in a stone jar, and a barrel of clean that came in on a cart in a churn and is down to nothing now, and a butt on a stand. And what has been in that tank since the twentieth is the worst water that has ever come into this lane, and there is a man on the cattle track on the twelfth day of next month with a form and a box that says *the matters this district wishes examined*.

"So put the water in the box. Not the chamber, not the ground, not a name. *The water.* In a district examination, with a form, with a date, in an office — the water. That is the only thing in this yard that anybody could do anything about and every person in this yard has known it for a month and not one of us has said it out loud because saying it out loud means it is a claim and a claim is a thing that can be refused in a room.

"And I am going to say the rest of it. I am fifty-nine years old and I have kept this room open on forty-one pounds and I have not been able to keep it open this month, and I have not had a shilling in it since the thirteenth, and I have got three pounds and sixteen in a bucket that is the district's, and at a shilling the gallon that is seventy-six gallons, and a cart holds forty, and Errol Slee came up this track at four in the morning on the thirteenth and would not look at anybody and has not come back.

"So there are two things in that box and they are both true and I can only put one of them in. That is not a moral question. That is a sheet of paper with a line on it."

Marda Ollan argued for putting nothing in it, and she argued it with a ledger.

"Then we write a letter," she said, "and a letter goes in a bag on a cart, and I have sent one up that track on the twelfth of this month and it has been in a warden's office for ten days and it is in a book in four buildings and the box on the warden's form that would have made this district countable is empty on purpose and is going to read *keeper, none held* in a fair hand, and a man is going to come down here on the twelfth of next month and read those two words in a fair book and understand that a hundred and twenty-six people looked at a box and left it empty on purpose.

"And if we put something in this one, then this one is not empty, and then it is a form with a thing in it, and a form with a thing in it is a file, and a file is a schedule, and Bel Crale has just told this yard herself what a schedule does. Every one of us in this yard has read a piece of paper that somebody filled in for us. I do not want to be one of them.

"We write the letter. We do not fill the box. We tell the man on the twelfth of next month exactly what we have told each other, in a yard, with the door open, and we let the box stand empty and let him see it standing empty, because an empty box that a hundred and forty people looked at is worth more to us than a full one that one woman wrote."

Yara Sen refused to write anything, and refused in a way that was the end of an argument rather than the start of one.

"I will not write a clinical question into a form for a district I have no power in," she said, "and I will tell the yard exactly why, and it is not modesty. If I write *water supply in connection* in that box, then this office has a physician on the record saying there is a matter of water supply in connection in a district that is not on a schedule, and the box I have spent a month telling this yard not to fill is filled by me, and it will be filled by the only person in this lane who has a title, and in six weeks there will be a document in four buildings with a physician's name against a word that was empty on purpose.

"And I have got nothing else. I have got a jar and two white crusts and a sum that was seventy for three weeks and is sixty-eight because a mother told me in a yard that her daughter has had bad hands since the spring, and a girl of seven at the fourth point for a fortnight, and a boy of nine at the top end drinking out of a tank fed by a channel a man has just told us he cannot see the bottom of. And if a man with a form asks me on the twelfth of next month what I want examined, I am going to tell him, in a room, with his clerk writing, and I am not going to put it in his box first."

"Then it is Bel Crale's and mine," said Hanne Roeg.

She came out at about two in the afternoon and did not sit down, and she read out the whole of what she had written before she said one word about it, because she had learned that in a yard.

> *I am the named representative of a district of one hundred and twenty-six persons at the lower terrace road, and I have no document that says so, and I say it here in a yard and a conservator has certified the count in a register in Vellum and Sluice Warden Pell has entered a condition in a book with four copies of it.*
> *On the twelfth day of this month I wrote to the warden of that road and asked whether a district with no row may ask a settlement with no row for water. It is a question and it is in a bag and it has been in the warden's office for ten days and it has not been answered.*
> *On the seventeenth day of this month I wrote my own answer to it in my own hand and I said no, and the reason I gave was that on the ninth day of this month a physician told this yard that every drop of water in this lane has come out of a works pump two miles up the mill road, and the people at the end of it are not the people who started it, and we have drunk it for sixteen days, and I would not do to a settlement of thirty-four people what was done to us.*
> *I was right on the seventeenth day. I am wrong on the twenty-seventh day and I am going to say why in the same hand, because a person who says I was wrong and does not say why is a person who has been frightened.*
> *On the seventeenth day there were eighty gallons of clean water in a churn in a dispensary in this lane, and a jar of eleven gallons of the last rain there has been, and a man with a horse had not come up a mill road, and the tank in the yard had five hundred and ninety gallons in it and was going down at a hundred and twenty a day, and there is no municipal supply in any form coming down this road.*
> *On the twenty-seventh day there is nothing clean in this lane. There is a tank at its coping that has not gone down by a measurable amount since the twentieth, and a physician has said in a yard that the salt in it has been getting stronger for a fortnight because the water goes off the top of it and the sun takes the water and not the salt. There is a man at the top of this lane who has shut his own pump for two days because the channel that fills his tank has cut a quarter of an inch of new bed in six hours and he has one hand and cannot see the bottom of it. There is a girl of seven at the second step who has been at the fourth point since the twentieth and has asked her mother where the front door is three times in one day. There is a boy of nine at the top end drinking out of a tank we cannot test. And there are thirty-four people on a hill two miles up this road whose well comes up out of a kitchen floor, and who were told on the seventeenth of this month by a man with a bar that it may change, and who have told a bench of twenty-eight people that they will not send a drop of it down here while that plant can be started by anybody with a horse, and who had a girl of about fourteen write it on a slate and then made her put her own name to it and not the girl's, and whose name is now on a slate in this lane.*
> *So: yes. We will ask them. I am changing my answer on the twelfth day of this month and I am changing it because a tank went bad and a man closed a pump, and I am seventy-one years old and I have known better than to be sentimental about this lane and I am not being sentimental, I am being out of water.*
> *What I ask to be examined. The condition of the works at Ashgate, level four, and the condition of the ground beneath this district, and whether the district of one hundred and twenty-six persons at the lower terrace road and the settlement of eleven houses and thirty-four persons at Ashgate may be treated as one matter for the purposes of that examination and heard together in one room by one examiner.*
> *What I do not ask for. I do not ask for this district to be put on a schedule. I do not ask for a route. I do not ask for water. I do not ask for a keeper. I do not ask for the setting out of a single one of the charges standing against the man who has carried my water up and down this lane since the fourth day of this month with a bar on his shoulder and his name on everything he has written.*
> *And I want the examiner to read this out in this yard, and I want it read out with the doors open, and I want it read out before he fills in anything at all, and I am told that is not in his power and I am asking for it anyway.*
> *H. Roeg. The fourth step. Twenty-seventh day of this month.*

Nobody in that yard argued with the four lines about what she was not asking for, which Hanne Roeg noticed and was not comforted by.

---

She read the last part out separately, and it cost her more, and she did it standing up.

"There is a line in that I did not read to you, and it is mine and I have thought about it since Saturday, and I am going to say it now in daylight because if anybody finds it in a book in six weeks they will have found it out of a bundle.

"That examiner is coming here on the twelfth day of next month. That sheet is a file. A file is in four buildings. And there is a boy of ten at a school in the fenner district who does not know any of this is going on, and whose school is in the fenner district, and there is a man at that school who knows nothing whatever about me, and the day this lane is on a piece of paper in an office that boy is a thing in a report, and there is no road back off that, and I have known that since the twelfth of this month and I did not stop writing.

"So I have cost that boy. I have cost him a thing he does not know he has got, and I have cost myself four days of not sleeping about it, and I would like the yard to know that both of those are in my hand and neither of them was anybody else's decision, and that I have been a signer of things four times in this lane's life and I have cost somebody something every time, and this is the fifth, and it is the first one I have chosen on a Saturday by myself with nobody asking me."

She put the sheet in Marda Ollan's hands.

Then a boy of eleven came up the lane with a blanket round his shoulders and was put down on the fourth step beside her, because Ondra carried him four feet every hour he could and the fourth step in the afternoon was the warm one, and Hanne Roeg looked at him for a while and did not say anything to him at all, and after a minute she put her hand flat on the blanket on his knees, the way a woman does to a child she has decided not to ask anything about, and left it there.

Marda Ollan wrote the request out in her own hand on the back of the letter, because there is no clean paper in that lane, and then wrote beneath it the two charges standing against him, with the dates on them, because a request that costs a man something ought to carry what it costs, and then wrote on the face of the sheet, at the top, where a man's hand goes:

> *Request of the district of the lower terrace road, the blue steps, 126 persons, no row, made in a yard in daylight on the twenty-seventh day of this month, read out in full, doors open. Signed at the fourth step. Not written, not signed, and not asked for by the engineer named in the charges standing against him.*

Tarin Rusk carried it as far as the bottom of the lane, and Niko Venn took it off him at the cattle track and went up the hill with it in a satchel, because a man who has said out loud that he is not going to chase the Water Office is not going to start now, and because there is a girl of about fourteen at the top of that hill with a pail in each hand and a shutter with a number chalked on it, and somebody has to tell her before the twelfth of next month that a document is coming with her name on it.
