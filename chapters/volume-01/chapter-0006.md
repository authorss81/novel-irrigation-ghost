# Chapter 0006 — The Low Pump on Glassstep

He wrote the answer to requisition eighteen in the low room of the coach between Ferrow and the watershed, with the light going and his knee against a stranger's basket.

> *Ashgate intake, main wheel, shut and chained six years, last dogged by the signatory twenty-one months ago. Residual head 5·89 m above the gate seat, measured on the stilling tube. A shut gate under head is not a contradiction; a shut gate under head is a valve. The Office can confirm the figure. The Office cannot certify the condition of a reservoir it has not inspected in twenty-one months. The recorded condition of a sealed works is not a measurement.*

It was a good answer, as answers went, nearly honest, and somewhere behind it, in the exact place Mara Quill had put her finger, the number 5·89 was now in two documents written a hundred and eighty-one years apart that had no business agreeing with each other.

He had known that with the pen going, and had done it anyway, because the alternative was a paragraph in a woman's careful hand beginning *I opened a sealed structure at ten past six in the morning without authority*, and because some part of him had already worked out that being right was now the expensive option and had decided to buy it.

Then the coach crossed the watershed and the valley came up out of the dark to the chalk ridge with the terraces going down it in steps, and Tarin watched the light come off the cisterns on the lower rows and stopped thinking about the answer, because there were six thousand people between him and the answer and they were the part of it he could still fix.

He came down off the watershed into the fold on a borrowed mountain mule in the middle of the afternoon of the sixth, handed the animal to a boy who did not ask his name, and found Wick in the lane and sent him for the bar out of Hanne Roeg's yard, and the two of them had the branch open by seven and had the stop-gate out and its oak cheeks bare by dusk.

He had not heard the low pump since he was seven years old and he had not expected to hear it again, and at ten o'clock that night, with his forearms in the silt, he heard it go on somewhere under the flags with a slow knock, knock, knock, twice a second, patient as a miller, and he sat back on his heels in a hole in the ground and worked out, from where it came and how deep it ran, that it was a pump under the terrace, running at night, and that it was not on any sheet he had ever signed.

He had no time for that. He wrote it in the blue book with two lines and an underline and went back to the branch.

They dug from ten at night until two in the morning, and he slept forty minutes in the dry corner of an empty cistern house with his boots on, and then dawn came up the fold.

The branch came off the run about thirty yards below the arch, on the low side, behind a wall that had been built a foot in front of the channel at some point in the last century. It was a hand's width across and it went down and away toward the pump, and at the junction there was a stop-gate — two oak cheeks, a lead-threaded screw, a timber stop-log — gone solid with silt and lime and a mat of something that used to be a leaf. By two that morning they had it cleared to bare stone and the silt cut back six yards in the run, and the water had come back into the branch about an inch deep, and Tarin had gone up on the step out of the way, because when it came it came fast and it came sideways.

There was no water in the yard at all. There had been four inches on the first and now there was a dark ring on the flags at the level the water had stood and a smell of wet stone going out of the sun, and the cistern in the corner was at a third and falling, because a cistern at a third on the seventh day of a ration is a thing the row drinks down visibly, an inch a day, in front of everybody.

The blue doors were all shut. Somebody had scrubbed the ring off the third step and left the stone a shade paler than the rest.

Hanne Roeg was on the fourth step with her cane across her knees, and there was a queue at the pump that went down the lane and turned, and it was not a queue for water. It was a queue for the pump's cistern, which was a smaller tank further down the lane and had been all this row had for thirty years, and the row had been managing it for six days by taking turns.

He had a timber stop-log cut to size, a wedge of iron, and no valve seat, and that was the entirety of his engineering. A branch this size off a head of 5·89 metres could not be metered. It would either carry more than the pump's tank could take and put four feet of water into a lane in about nine minutes, or carry nothing at all, and he would have spent the night on a hole in the ground with a stone in his hand.

Which is why he had not opened it.

Niko Venn found him at the wall at ten past five with a lamp and his coat buttoned wrong.

"There you are," Niko said. "I heard you were back on the mountain. I've been standing at the top of the lane for an hour because I've got a bundle of chisel and mallet and a lump of that grey Ashgate limestone that comes out of the culvert, and I was not going to come down here with all that hanging off me in the dark like a body."

He put the bundle down on the flags, and then he saw the branch, and then he stopped talking, which Tarin had known him for eleven months and had never once seen happen.

"You've opened it out."

"I've opened it out. I have not opened it."

"Right." Niko crouched, and put the lamp down so the light went along the branch and not up it. "You've got no seat."

"I've got no seat."

"You're going to put the log in and wedge it and the log is going to sit there for about a quarter of an hour and then that whole lane is going to be a canal." Niko said it without any relish whatever. "You know that, right? You know what's on the other end of that lane."

"Twenty-two houses and a pump tank. Yes."

"And you know what's under it."

Tarin did not answer that, because the boy did not know, and the boy had brought a chisel and a mallet up a hill in the dark for him.

"Which stone will take a name," he said instead.

Niko looked up. "Off the old terrace?"

"Any stone on this row that will take a name."

"That's the thing you've been doing for twenty years, and I have watched you do it twice a year for eleven months, and I know exactly what you mean, and you're asking me wrong." Niko sat back on his heels. "You don't want the good stones. The good stones are the ones set in the wall copings, and there's four of them from the first pump house, and the words on them are weather-gone to nothing, and every one of them has a witness hand at the foot and you could cut a name into one and it would read in a hundred years. But none of them is at the pump. None of them is *about* the pump. You'd be cutting a name on a stone that belongs to a wall."

"The pump's marker," Tarin said.

"Bottom step, inside face, wedged in the mortar of the plinth." Niko got it without being asked, which was the entirety of what he was for and had been for eleven months. It was a slab of limestone the size of a bread loaf, one face worn to a soft grey nothing, the other plain. "Two faces, because the man who set it was a pump man, and pump men are not fools about which side faces the weather. That side's the old one. The letters are still in it — you can feel them with a thumbnail — but they're gone too soft to read. And that," he turned it over and slapped the plain face, "is blank. That's the side he left for the next thing."

Tarin turned the stone over twice in the light. The blank face was smooth and pale and had never been cut.

"Niko."

"I know. I know what it is." The boy wiped his hands on his trousers, and then, unexpectedly, he was angry, in the particular way that twenty-two-year-olds are angry about a thing that is much bigger than they are. "I am not going to be one of the things that happens to this row. I have brought you a chisel because you asked and because I have nothing else to be doing, and I am not standing here watching you find out whether you can do it. If you want a witness, do not ask me. I am not going to be your reason."

"I was not going to ask you."

"You were looking at me."

"I was looking at the wall," Tarin said, "and I was going to ask you in about four minutes, and I am telling you now so that you can tell me not to, and I am telling you the reason I was going to ask, which is that you know this terrace by your feet and I have known it by a sheet since I was fourteen and the sheet is wrong."

Niko Venn stood in a lane in the dark for a while.

"My aunt kept the low pump for nine years," he said. "Nine years, and never once on a sheet, and never a keeper's mark, and never anybody come. And when she died there was nothing to hand on, because there was nothing to hand on. That was the job." He picked up the bundle again. "Go and get your witness, Tarin. Ask one person, not the row — they'll be at each other for a week and the tank's empty now. And tell them what they're agreeing to, because whatever it is they'll be able to tell their grandchildren they did, and a man who does not know what he agreed to will be ashamed of it in ten years and you will have made him so."

Tarin Rusk, who had come down that morning to open a branch with a piece of timber and an iron wedge, walked half a mile down the lane in the dark to ask one person for his name.

Half a mile along it he stopped, and stood in the dark, and did a thing he was going to be glad of later and could not have told anybody about at the time.

He knew that lane. Not from a sheet and not from nineteen years of logs: he knew where the stones came up under the arch, and which of the door sills were worn hollow by forty years of somebody's boot, and where the low step was that you put your hand on when you came round the corner with a full pail at six in the morning. He had run it at six years old with his mother's hand flat on the back of his neck. It was in him the way a staircase is in you, and it was his, and nobody had given it to him and nobody had refused it.

He stood in the dark and worked out, in about four seconds, that if a thing wanted a witness and he had one in his own hands, then the cheapest possible answer to the whole of the day was his own.

Then he worked out, in a slightly longer time, that a private memory is not a public record, that a thing nobody agreed to is a thing nobody is answerable for, and that if the only person holding the route was the man holding the chisel then he would be the same man who cut the name and the man who read the name and the man who checked the arithmetic, and Doctor Sen would be able to write one sentence about it in four years and the sentence would be true.

Also, and he put this last because it was the reason he actually did not do it, nobody in that row had offered him anything. He was not going to take a thing out of a house that had been left to him.

He walked the rest of the half mile.

Tam Roke kept the low pump. He was forty-four, had a white scar along one forearm from a cistern lid that had come off its hinge in 118, and was standing beside the pump tank in the half dark with a dipper in his hand, doing the thing that men do on the seventh day of a ration, which is looking at the thing that is getting less.

He knew who Tarin was before Tarin said anything, which is a thing that happens to engineers and to people who have been on a terrace since they were six.

"You were at the arch," Roke said.

"First day. I've been in Vellum."

"And now you're here about a channel." Roke put the dipper down. "There's a hole behind the wall by the third step. Wick told me there was a hole behind the wall by the third step and that it went down toward me. And there's a man in a wet coat standing in front of me who is not from the Water Office, because the Water Office has never once come down here, and I have a fairly clear idea of what you are."

"My name is Tarin Rusk. My father was Bevan Rusk and he kept the sluice on this terrace, and I was born at the end past the pump."

Tam Roke looked at him for a long moment.

"Roke," he said. "Same as me, near enough. My mother was a Rusk out of the top end."

"I know."

"Then you'll know that our grandmothers did not get that name from a river." Roke sat down on the pump's step. "Right. What is it you want, and I would rather have the whole of it in one go, because I have got a tank and about ninety people drawing on it and I am not in a good frame to be told things in instalments."

So Tarin told him the whole of it, in a lane, in the dark, standing up, in the voice he used for briefing crews at a fault, because that was the only voice he had and because Roke had asked for it entire.

Here is what he said, and he said it in this order because he had worked out the order on the way down the lane.

"There is a head of five point eight nine metres standing behind a shut gate at the top of this hill, and it is real, and I did not put it there, and I cannot shut it from up there. It has been coming down that run for six days. It fills one cistern in one yard to a third and then it stops, because that yard's drain is plated over, and the plate is the only reason the row has four inches of yard instead of four feet, and that plate is why the cistern is at a third.

"I dug out the old branch last night that goes to your pump. I have not opened it. If I open it with a piece of timber and a wedge, it will run about a quarter of an hour, and then something will give, and if it gives in the right place you will have four feet of water in this lane, and if it gives in the wrong place you will have four feet of water in this lane anyway and I will be standing in it.

"And there is one thing I can do about the quarter of an hour, and I cannot promise you what it is, because I have done it once and I did not do it on purpose and I have not been able to repeat it yet. What it needs is three things and I can get all three. A trace, which is the line of the branch itself and I can clean it out and stand in it. A record, which is a stone, and there is a stone at your pump plinth with one face gone soft and one face blank, and I can cut a name into the soft face. And a witness."

He stopped.

"And a witness is a person who has used or kept that route, who stands on the stone and puts their mark on it, and who is responsible for saying out loud that it is there. Not the Water Office. A person. You."

"Responsible for saying what?"

"Where the water comes from and who else it goes past." Tarin said it straight, because the alternative was a lie and this man had asked for the whole of it. "It does not come only to your pump. It comes past every other row on this terrace, and it comes past the yard with the plate on it, and whatever I do, whatever we do, the water is doing that to every row on this terrace and nobody asked a single one of them. That is the part I do not have an answer to, and I am not going to stand here at four in the morning and tell you I have one."

"So somebody gets the cross of it."

"Somebody might. I do not know. I have a water physician looking at that this afternoon and she is going to tell me the truth whether I want it or not."

"Which water physician."

"Doctor Sen."

Tam Roke let out a short breath. "Sen has been coming down here since the spring and she has been telling us for three years that our water is bad and that it is not our fault and that the city is lying to us about where it comes from," he said. "I have had a fight with her about it. Twice."

"Then you two will get on," Tarin said.

He waited.

Roke got up, and went to the plinth, and got the stone out from under the step with a bar, and carried it — a two-handed carry, the way you carry something that matters — and set it down on the flags in front of the pump, and wiped the soft face with his sleeve until the water in it came up grey and shiny under the lamp.

"Before I do this," he said, "I want to know what happens to me. Not the row. Me. Because I have a mark on a stone and stones last, and I have got a boy at the top end who is nine and thinks his mother is a good woman for having me, and I would like to know what I am signing my name to for nine hundred years."

"That's the fair question," Tarin said, "and the honest answer is that I do not know. I know the three things. I know that when I put my hand on that stone in Vellum something in it said it wanted a witness, and that I could do nothing whatever about it. I do not know whether a name makes water do the right thing, or only the thing you are frightened of. And if this goes wrong you will be on a stone in a lane and I will be somewhere else, and I cannot stop that and I could not argue you had a fair warning, because you would not have had one."

"That is a hell of a thing to say to a man at four in the morning."

"Yes."

"Does the row have to agree?"

"No."

"Do *I* have to be able to say I'm sure?"

"No."

"Then I'd rather not be able to." Roke crouched over the stone. "I have been sure of three things in my life and two of them turned out to be the same lie. Go on, then. Cut the name, and I'll tell you what to cut, because you'll have your own name for it and I've had my mother's."

They argued about it for eleven minutes, in the dark, on a step, like two men arguing about a fence post.

Half the row wanted the blue steps, which is what they called the lane, and the other half wanted Glassstep, which is what they called the row, and the argument was not really about the name at all, and Tarin listened to four of them and understood that he was being handed something and that he was not going to be the one who decided it.

"Glassstep," said Hanne Roeg, from the fourth step, without being asked. "It is what the pumps were built on. If you cut a name on a stone put it on the one it was given."

Tarin got out the borrowed chisel and the mallet and the lump of grey Ashgate limestone, and he knelt on the flags on a lane that was not on a sheet, in a district that had been erased from a survey twenty-six years earlier by men who had bolted a plate over the drain, and he cut a name into a face of a stone that had already had one on it once.

He had cut stones before. It was the one piece of craft he had never had to be taught, and it went the way it had always gone: the set of the chisel, the drop of the right wrist at the end of the stroke so the tool bit without a hammer, and the line coming up out of the limestone in a pale curl.

He cut the route name. He cut the date, and the word of the day, and then he turned the stone back and cut the line for a mark, and handed the mallet to Tam Roke and stepped back out of the way.

Roke cut his own mark, and it was crooked, and he said so, and Tarin said it was the mark of a man who does not do it every day, and Roke said that was exactly what it was.

The moment the last stroke came off the stone, the water in the branch changed.

Tarin had his hand in the channel and he felt it happen under his fingers and it was the single strangest thing that had ever happened to him: the water stopped fighting. That was all. There was a five-and-a-bit metre head on this hill and it had been running down a six-foot channel with a foot in the hundred and it had been tearing the bed; and now it was in a hand's width of branch going down toward a pump tank, and it was going at the speed of a person walking, and the bed was not tearing.

He put his whole forearm in to the elbow. The water went past his arm like it was not interested.

"That's the third time," said Roke, from the step. "You have that face."

"It is not doing what I did it," Tarin said, and it came out of him with a great deal more heat than the statement deserved, and the boy Niko, who had not gone and who was standing in the lane with his lamp, said: "No. It is doing what you told it to."

Nobody in the lane had told it anything. Tarin stood in the water with his arm in it and understood, with the ordinary clarity he brought to a head calculation, that he had a clean trace and a cut record and a man who had put his mark on the stone of his own free will, and that he could not account for what those three had in common, and that he had a second of that branch in which to decide whether to find out.

He found out.

The pump ran. Tam Roke put the dipper in and drew water and it was warm and it was brown at the top of the glass and it was running at the rate of a good hand pump and not the rate of a canal, and the lane stayed dry, and at ten past six in the morning a queue of ninety people watched a man with a white scar down his forearm put water into a tank from a hole behind a wall, and did not say anything at all, because there was nothing yet to say and they had all learned not to trust the good mornings.

Tarin filled two buckets and drank from one. It tasted of stone and metal and the inside of a pipe that had not been washed in six years.

He handed the other to the row's oldest woman, who drank a mouthful of it, thought about it for a moment, and then said, in the voice of a woman giving a verdict she has no intention of softening:

"That is not our water."
