# Chapter 0017 — What There Was Room For

The arithmetic on the back of the ward notice had been in his coat for ten days and it had not improved by being carried, and on the morning of the thirtieth day he wrote it out again in the blue book with the arm that still worked, because there was nothing else in the world he was qualified to do.

> *Vessel, Glassstep: cistern 10 ft across, 12 ft to coping, 8 ft room = 4,700 gal. Three tanks, 1,400. Pails, 600. Total 6,700 gal.*
> *Flow, gauged oak at the pin found on the nineteenth: 610 gal/hr.*
> *6,700 / 610 = 11 hours.*

And underneath, on the twenty-ninth, having walked the yard twice with a stick:

> *There is no room. The cistern is at the coping. The tanks are full. The pails are full. There has been no headroom in this district since the twentieth day of the month and there will not be any until there is a hole to put it in.*

And underneath that, on the thirtieth, in the hand he had, at about four in the morning:

> *So make the hole.*

---

He went over it as many times as the problem would let him, and it held every time, and holding was the problem.

There was a head of 5·89 m at the Ashgate main wheel and a run coming down the terrace on a foot in the hundred that had been coming down since the first day of the month without once being asked a question. There was a branch off that run, a hand's width, dug out on the night of the sixth with Wick's bar and six hours of the pair of them, with a two-oak-cheeked stop-gate in it that had eaten a silt mat and a leaf and some lime. There was a gauged length of oak in that gate, planed in a kitchen by a man with a white scar down one forearm, with a groove down one face and a pin hole at the head, and on the nineteenth day of the month Tarin Rusk had found the setting that put six hundred and ten gallons an hour through a hand's width of channel and had driven the pin and wedged it and watched the water come up to a hand's width and lie there and not tear the bed.

The pin was in his pocket. It was the only instrument in the Marrow Basin that measured anything.

The only thing missing was somewhere for the water to go.

---

He went down at three in the morning with the stick, the pin, the bar, and the length of oak, and he woke Wick on the way past, because a man with a head of water coming at a closed gate at four in the morning needs two pairs of hands and one pair of hands is not a decision, it is a hope.

"You have not got a keeper," Wick said, on the step, in the dark.

"I have not."

"You have not got a witness."

"No."

"Then what are you doing down here."

"I am draining the cistern," said Tarin Rusk, "and I am going to be honest with you about the whole of it before we touch anything, because you have been honest with me and I have earned that. When the tank is empty I have eight feet of room in it again, and that room is the only vessel in this district, and with the tank empty I can put six hundred and ten gallons an hour into it for about seven and a half hours, and then the water in the tank will be level with the head in the branch and the branch will stop of its own accord, exactly the way it stopped at three o'clock on the twenty-first with nobody standing in it.

"Every drop in this yard is crosscurrent water. It is in the tank, it is in the pails, it is in the ground under the flags and in the lane, and Doctor Sen has said out loud that it is not fit to drink and not fit to wash in, and the only reason I can do this at all is that a cistern with eight feet of water in it does not care what the water tastes like. It is a hole. I am going to put water in a hole and take it out again and that is the entire engineering.

"What it costs is this. Four thousand seven hundred gallons is going to come out of the bottom of that tank at four o'clock this morning and there is no drain in this lane, and the yard's only outfall is a plate, and so four thousand seven hundred gallons of water is going to sit in a lane over a sealed shaft. That is the price. I have worked out that the price is this, and I am not able to work out a way of not paying it."

Wick looked at him for a moment in the dark.

"You have told me a thing at four in the morning," he said, "and I want to say to you that I have listened to a great many men at four in the morning and the ones who did that are not the ones who got hurt. Get the bar. I will not hold the branch. I will hold a bar."

---

The draw-off was at the base of the cistern, under the flags, behind the pails, and it was a four-inch lead pipe with a wooden plug in it that had been out as long as anybody could remember, because there had never been anywhere for it to go.

It came out at about nine minutes past four and the sound of it in a lane at that hour was the worst sound Tarin had heard in a month.

For two hours the water of the whole district came out of the bottom of a cistern into a lane that was already carrying a few inches down its centre, and Wick and Tarin and, after a while, Marda Ollan with a bucket, stood in it and did nothing at all, which is the only thing you can do with a draining cistern. It went out grey. It went out with a skin of salt on it. It went out into a lane with a plate under it and no drain anywhere in four hundred yards, and the lane absorbed it the way a lane does, and by half past six the flags in the yard had gone from wet to soft and the water was standing in the wheel-ruts about a finger deep and the cistern at the wall was empty and dark and had eight feet of nothing in it.

There was a sound from under the yard.

Tarin had heard the plate tick every ten or twelve seconds since the twenty-first and had not been able to tell whether it was iron cooling in the night or water finding a way round the bolts. It was still ticking. It was ticking about the same. He noted it the way he noted everything, and he put it in the book at ten past six as *plate audible, unchanged*, and he wrote the two words *unchanged* twice, because he wanted to see them twice.

---

He set the log at ten past six and it came up to a hand's width the way it always did, and then he put his hand on the stone.

The marker stone came out from under the bottom step on its plinth, the old weathered face up, the cut face with the route name and the date and the word of the day and Roke's crooked mark under the date, and the other face blank, and pale, and never written on.

He put both palms on it.

"Glassstep low pump branch," he said, out loud, in a lane, at ten past six in the morning, to a man with a bar and a woman with a bucket.

The water did not change.

Not the way it changed on the seventh. There was no moment, in his forearm, of a bed going quiet, of a sound dropping by a third, of a hand pump's handle going from heavy to light. The water came into the branch at the rate of something getting through rather than something being allowed, and it went down the channel and it began, in the ordinary way, to cut.

He had his forearm in to the elbow. He felt the bed.

*Not a route*, he thought. *A hole with a head on it.*

And then, because he was a man who had run out of other ideas at four in the morning and because the offer was sitting in him ready to go, he did the thing that the whole of the twenty days had been about.

He stood in a channel with five point eight nine metres behind it and put the lane in his hands — the stones under the arch, the sills worn hollow, the low step you put your hand on at six in the morning with a full pail — his own, given to him, nobody else's, the cheapest anchor in the world.

"Here," he said. "This is mine. Take it."

Nothing took it.

Nothing happened at all, and the water cut a fresh groove in the limestone about an inch deep in four minutes, and Tarin Rusk stood in a channel with his eyes shut and his hand in the water and understood, in about the time it takes a heart to do three beats, that he was on the wrong side of the rule he had worked out in the dark on the seventh of the month, and that the rule did not care in the slightest that the memory was his.

*A private memory is not a public record. A thing nobody agreed to is a thing nobody is answerable for.*

He said it out loud to a channel, which was ridiculous, and then he laughed, once, with no humour in it at all, and got his arm out and stood up.

---

He tried the stone again at a quarter past six, because the stone was where the answer had been twice.

He got the chisel out of his breast pocket and set it on the blank face, and took the mallet in his left hand, and knelt.

The two fingers of his right hand came down onto the fore-edge of the stone to feel whether it was seated, which is not a thing that you do to a stone, and which he had done every working day since he was thirteen years old, and which was not there any more, and which he had watched not be there on the twentieth of the month in front of eighty people, and which he had apparently been hoping, somewhere, for nine days, might come back.

The stone took a white bruise a quarter of an inch from where he had set it. That was all it did.

He put the chisel and the mallet away and wrote in the blue book, because it had to be on the page in his own writing:

*06:15. Set chisel, blank face, second attempt. Hand will not seat. Name not cut. Blank face not cut.*

---

They watched the cistern.

That was the whole of the job and he had planned for it, because on the seventh and the twentieth the vessel was the thing you watched, and a man with a stick and a bucket and a boy with a bit of chalk can keep a count of a filling tank to the finger every ten minutes.

At about seven he made tea on the low house step out of the tin pot that had been in his bag since the gatehouse, because he had drunk nothing since three and because that step was the only dry place in the lane that morning.

It was hot and it went down the way water goes down and it had no taste of any kind in it, and it had had no taste in it since the seventh of the month, and he drank a cup of it standing up and looked at a yard that was filling with water he had no right to put there, and it did not get any better. He had hoped. That was the whole of it, standing on a step at seven in the morning: he had walked down to the yard for twenty days with a tin pot in his bag and he had hoped, on some ordinary Tuesday, that it would have come back.

They watched it fill. The rate was right. He had the gauged log and the pin and the setting from the nineteenth and six hundred and ten gallons an hour went into a tank that had eight feet of room in it, and at twenty past seven the boy — Roke's boy, who had a chalk and no instructions any more and had simply turned up — marked the coping, and at half past seven he marked it again, and he said, "It is not going at the rate it went on the twentieth."

"No," said Tarin.

"It is going faster."

"Yes," said Tarin Rusk. "It is."

He put the stick in and it came up a foot in eleven minutes, where on the twentieth it had been a finger in ten, and he stood there and did the sum twice, because it was the sum he knew better than any sum in the world.

> *610 gal/hr against 8 ft of room. 4,700 gal in the tank.*
> *4,700 / 610 = 7.7 hours.*
> *Filling at twice that rate.*

"Somebody is putting water in that cistern," said Wick.

"Somebody is putting water in that cistern that I did not put there," said Tarin, "and I have been in this lane for thirty days and I know every pipe in it, and there is no pipe in this lane except the one that comes out of the wall, and the one that comes out of the wall is eighteen inches across and half of it is silted, and it has been running off the run into that yard since the first day of the month at a rate that nobody in the city has ever measured, because there has never once been anywhere for it to go, so nobody has ever had a reason to find out."

He put the stick in again.

"The branch is taking a third of it off the run," he said. "I wrote that down on the twentieth. The sound of the run came down a third when the log went in. I have had that number in my book for ten days and I have been doing a sum all morning without it in. A third of the run is going down the branch. The rest of the run is still coming down the wall and it is coming into this yard through an eighteen-inch lead pipe with a silted inlet, and it is coming in at whatever rate a foot-wide channel from a sealed works comes in at, and I do not know that rate, and it is not 610, and it has never been anything like 610, and if it were, this row would have been flooded on the first day of the month."

"So the tank fills up faster than your sum."

"The tank fills up faster than my sum, and the tank filling up faster than my sum is the one thing in this morning that I am certain about." He got the numbers and wrote them and put the book away. "And when the tank is level with the head the branch stops. It does not matter how fast the tank fills. The head is the head. This cannot overfill the cistern. That is the entire reason there is a balance point, and I have relied on it twice."

Wick said, "Then what is the water in the lane for."

And Tarin Rusk, who had been an engineer for nineteen years, looked at the water in the lane.

The water in the lane was not going away.

He had been watching the tank for an hour and ten minutes and he had not once looked at the lane, because the lane was not a vessel and the lane was where the water you could not use went. There was a finger of it in the ruts at half past four. There was a finger of it at the wall at six. There was a finger and a half of it at a quarter past seven, and it was standing, and it was not moving, and there was nowhere in four hundred yards for it to go except down.

"Get the family out of the lowest room," he said.

"What?"

"Wick. The lowest room, the one with the bed on the frame. Get the family out of it and get them on the flags at the top end and get Ondra's boy up. Now, not in five minutes."

"It is a finger of water—"

"Get them out."

---

He was wrong about one thing and it turned out not to be the thing.

The tank did not overfill. At ten past nine the water in the cistern stood level with the water in the branch and the branch stopped, exactly as it had stopped on the twenty-first and for the same reason, and the sound of the run coming down the wall went back up by the whole of the third it had gone down at ten past six, and Tarin Rusk stood at a cistern with a stick in his hand and had a window of seven hours and forty minutes in which everything he had said was true.

The room he was wrong about was the ground.

The plate had been ticking every ten or twelve seconds for ten days, and at ten past nine in the morning, with three and a half hours of his own work on this lane and a finger and three-quarters of standing water in a lane with no drain, the ticking stopped.

Then, at about twenty-five past nine, the floor of the lowest room began to come up.

It did not burst. It wept. That is the only word for what a cut lime-and-brick terrace floor does when the fill under it has been saturated for thirty years by a district that has been unable to drain since before the register: it weeps, through the joints, through the mortar, through the hairline crack where the flags had been lifted and reset by a man in 118 to take a cistern lid off its hinge. The flagstones at the corner nearest the throat came up about a quarter of an inch and then settled back, and the water came back through in about forty minutes, and it came back not clear.

Wick was already in there. He got the bed frame off the wall and lifted the boy off it in his arms and the boy's mother came out with a bundle and did not leave the room until she had put her feet on the step above, and Tarin Rusk got to the doorway and looked at what was under the door and it was a hand's depth of grey water that had come up through a floor and was not going to stop because the tank had stopped, because the tank stopping had put a third of the run back into the wall and the run had gone straight down into the same ground.

He went up the lane at a run, which is a thing he had not run at since he was twenty-two.

Niko Venn was at the branch with both hands on the bar, holding it, not because he had been told to.

"Close it," Tarin said. "Close the gate."

"You have the log in it."

"I cannot lift an oak stop-log out of a gate with a head on it. That is Tam Roke's job at four in the morning and you and I are not Tam Roke and it is going to take four men."

"I will do it." Niko did not move, and his voice was the flat, unbothered voice of a man of twenty-two who is about to do a thing he has thought about for a fortnight. "Hold the bar. If I take the wedge first the log goes in about four inches under the load and I cannot get my fingers in after that. So you hold the bar and I take the wedge out, and then you hold the bar while I lift the log and slide it. And if I am wrong about that you will find out, because you will be on the other end of it."

They were three men and Wick's bar and it took eleven minutes, and Tarin Rusk put his left shoulder into the bar — the same shoulder, on the same week, twenty-one days healed — and heard something in it go that he had been hearing since the first day of the month, and did not stop, and the log came up out of the gate in one movement and the branch went quiet.

The water in the lane did not go away.

It was there at eleven o'clock, and it was there at one, and it was an inch and a quarter deep across the width of the lane and standing in the ruts, and it was going down by nothing at all, because there was no drain in four hundred yards and the only outfall was a plate, and the ground it had come through was the ground it was standing in, and it would begin to go down when the ground gave it back, which would be tomorrow, or the day after, and not before.

Tarin Rusk sat down on the step outside the low house at half past two in the afternoon with a bar across his knees, in the sun, in a lane that was wet from wall to wall, with a boy's chalk-mark on a cistern coping nine inches higher than it had been at ten past six, and a plate under the yard that had stopped ticking and had not started anything else.

He had been right about the vessel and he had been right about the head and he had been right about the balance point, and the branch had run for three hours and ten minutes instead of seven and a half, and it had stopped itself at the right level in the right tank, and none of it had been any use at all.

He got the blue book out and turned to the page where the two attempts were, and he wrote what he had.

> *30th day, 10:10 to 13:20. Branch opened, unnamed, unwitnessed, stop-log set at the pin found on the 19th. Flow 610 gal/hr as set. Tank filled in 3 h 10 min against a computed 7 h 40 min. Difference accounted for: branch taking one third of the run (sound fall, 20th). Run's own inflow to the yard unmeasured, through an 18 in lead pipe half silted, never measured by anyone, unknown. Balance point reached and branch stopped of its own accord at 10:10, which is correct and is the one thing that worked.*
> *09:25, plate noise ceased. 09:50, lowest room floor weeping, hand depth, grey. Lane standing, no drain, no outfall but the plate. Closed branch at 13:20 with Venn, W., 11 min, my left shoulder is not sound.*

Then he stopped, and sat with the pencil on the flat of the page for a long time, and wrote the thing he was actually obliged to write, which was the reason the whole of the previous month had been a month of a man trying to do a thing in the correct order.

> *I cannot say afterwards whether I lost this at the witness, or at the record, or at my standing, and I am not going to write down a guess in a book, because a guess in this book is a thing I would be tempted to build on.*
> *The three candidates, kept apart:*
> *1. The branch was not named and there was no second hand. The bed cut a groove an inch deep in four minutes where on the seventh of the month it went smooth, and a channel that cuts deeper puts more of the head into the ground, and the ground was the thing that came up. I have no measurement of how much more. I would estimate a third. I would not stake anything on a third.*
> *2. There was no keeper. The route ran because I was standing at a cistern with a stick and the thing that needed watching was under a floor thirty yards away, and there is no arithmetic that fixes that, and the man who could have been in the lane at four o'clock is a man who said no to me in front of eighty people and a warden, and was right to, and is at this moment standing in a house at the top end with his own boy in it.*
> *3. I have no standing. I did this at four in the morning without a warden, without a keeper, without a crew, and without a single person in this district who had agreed to any part of it, and the price of it has been paid by four households and by a boy who sleeps on a bed frame in a room whose floor is now wet.*
> *I do not know which of the three it was. I have three of them and I have learned nothing this morning that separates them, and I am thirty-three years old and I went out there at four o'clock on the strength of a stick.*

He shut the book.

Niko Venn was sitting on the wall with his boots up on the coping of a cistern that had been at the lip on Tuesday.

"I have been thinking about it for four hours," Niko said, "and I want to say one thing and then I am going to go and help Wick get the pails off the flags."

"Say it."

"You had the log in it and it was set to the pin and the water did not do what you said it would do." Niko turned his boots over. "And then you went up the lane and watched a tank for an hour and ten minutes, and there was one place in the whole of it that could have gone wrong, and it was not the place you were standing in. That is not a mystery, Tarin. That is a *job*. You watched the tank because the tank is the vessel and a man has to watch the vessel. And nobody watched the thing under the floor, because you cannot post a man on a floor."
