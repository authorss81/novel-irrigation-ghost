# Chapter 0027 — A Named Applicant

The fee was four shillings and it came out of a bucket in the blue steps that had held chalk, and the reason the fee could be paid at all was that the named representative of a district with no row in any schedule in the city had been told on the ninth of the month by a woman of thirty-one with a ledger in her hand to decide on Saturday, and had decided.

Mara Quill did not know that. She knew only that the four shillings were in an envelope with the warden's note number written on the outside of it in a hand she did not know, and that a man with two open charges had stood at a desk on a Friday morning and put his name down, and that the desk had taken the name without any difficulty whatever, because the desk did not care.

The reading room of the Municipal Memory Office was on the ground floor and it was public, and it was open, and the reason it was public was that anybody might walk in and look at anything on an open shelf, which is the whole theory and the whole cost of the building.

---

The desk asked three things and the clerk asked them in the same order to everybody.

"Name."

"Tarin Rusk. Senior flow engineer, Cresset Water Office, presently under a stand-down which is entered in your registry, and I have a sheet at the Water Office that says so and I will produce it if you want it produced."

The clerk looked up. She was about fifty and she had been at that desk for a long time and she had the manner of a woman who is entirely on the side of the thing in front of her.

"I do not want it produced," she said. "I want the purpose."

"Eleven words," said Mara Quill, from behind him.

"That is not for you," said the clerk.

"It is not," she agreed. "It is his, and the rule is that it is his, and I am a conservator in this building and I have read the reading rules since I was twenty and I am not going to help a man write his own purpose, because in about eleven years somebody will want to know who put the words in a box, and it will not do for it to have been the woman standing next to him."

Tarin Rusk stood at a public desk in Vellum for about a minute and a half.

> *To establish the recorded condition of the Ashgate intake main wheel.*

That is eleven. He had counted them twice, standing at a low house step in the dark on the ninth, with Marda Ollan counting over his shoulder and insisting on the number.

The clerk read it and wrote it and read it back, and Mara Quill watched her write it and did not say a word, because there was nothing to be said and because the eleven words were a fault and the fault was his and the whole of the fault was that the third of them was *recorded* and not *actual*, and there was not one word in the box about a lane of a hundred and twenty-six people at the bottom of a hill who had never been asked.

Then the second reader.

---

"That is you," said Mara Quill, before the clerk could write anything. "I am not able to countersign."

"You are a conservator in this building with eleven years in service."

"I am the officer whose initials are on the shelf-marker of the volume, which is on the west wall at this moment with a withdrawal date on it of the seventeenth, and rule nine says a restricted volume may be consulted by a named applicant and a second reader, and if the second reader is the officer whose hand put it in the strong room then the two readers are one person and the rule is a piece of paper with a hole in it."

Hollis Kreft came out of the office behind the desk at this point, and he had a piece of paper in his hand and he had clearly been going to come out of that office for some time.

"Miss Quill is correct," he said. "And I will say that she is correct and not that she should have said it, because the reason is sound and the delivery was unnecessary. Rule nine has been applied to that volume since the seventeenth and the reason it was applied is that it is contested, and a rule that is applied to a contested volume and then staffed by the officer who contested it would be a rule that had stopped working without anybody noticing." He put the paper on the desk. "Rull has signed. Nineteen years in this building, no connection to the case, and she is at the bench at ten and will give you an hour."

"Thank you," said Mara Quill.

"Not for you," said Kreft. "For the volume."

---

Senna Rull was fifty-four and had a way of standing at a bench that made Mara Quill feel like a woman of twenty-five, and she had one rule of her own that she stated once, in the room, before she touched anything, and which Mara Quill had never heard of and afterwards wished she had heard of eleven years ago.

"A paper of this age is not improved by anybody being gentle with it," she said. "It is a piece of rag that somebody made wet and beat and dried. It gets handled every day. It wants to be *held*, in both hands, off the table, and turned. If I could give you the whole of folio eleven in a cart I would."

"Nobody has ever been able to give me a whole volume," said Mara Quill, and Rull looked at her and said, "No," in exactly the way it needed saying, and that was the whole of their relationship for the day.

The volume came out of the strong room at ten past ten in a cloth wrapper and it was four inches and about six inches and it was the heaviest object Mara Quill had ever handled.

One hundred and eighty-one years. Four entries a year. Seven hundred and twenty-four leaves, and the last third of them were on paper that had gone brown at the edges, and the volume had been rebound twice and once badly.

Every entry for a hundred and eighty-one years said the same four things.

> *Date. Gate state: shut and dogged, dogs on, chain through the wheel spokes, padlocked. Head at wheel, metres, to two places. Chamber: void.*

And the head was 5·7 on the great majority of them and 5·8 on a few and 5·6 on a handful, and the range of it across a hundred and eighty-one years was a hand's breadth.

Rusk stood over the bench for a long time reading the gate column, and then he said, quietly:

"It is the same dogging. Same chain, same spokes, same padlock, same hand writing it four times a year for a hundred and eighty-one years. There is a rust mark under that padlock that has got a shape, and the shape is on four of these leaves, and one of them is dated in the first year of the log and one of them is dated in the last."

Rull said, "And?"

"And it means nobody has opened that gate since the flood," said Tarin Rusk, "and I opened it on the first day of this month with a bar, and every single thing in this book is true about every single leaf in it except the last one."

---

The last one.

The last entry in the Ashgate maintenance series was dated the year before the flood, and it read:

> *Head at wheel: 5·89. Chamber: see ref.*

And beneath it, in a second and heavier hand, driven hard and underlined: **maintained.**

Mara Quill had read the shadow of that line on the second of the month. She had read it on a bench with a press-glass and a raking lamp, and she had said *I will swear to five and eight* and had been right to.

She had never been in a room with the other three words.

"There," she said. "Ref."

"There is a reference," said Rull, over her shoulder. "That is a schedule reference and the form of it is a column. Look at the column. Every other leaf in this volume has a column headed *repair ordered* with a dash in it. This one has a dash in the repair column and a number in the column after it, and the number is three digits and the first of them is a one."

Mara Quill got the lamp and got low and got the press-glass and did what she had done every day for eleven years, which was to look at a thing at the speed of a thing.

The number in the shadow of the leaf beneath was **118**.

It took about four minutes. It took four minutes because the impression had been made by a pen held by a man who was copying a *true* leaf out of another book and was not thinking about pressure, and because the number sat at the end of a line at the same angle as the whole of the line, and because a shadow of a number is a shadow of the pressure of a number and not of the number, and it is a thing you can only read in a room where nobody is talking.

Mara Quill put the lamp where it wanted to be and held it there and read it, and Rull watched her hands, and Rusk watched the leaf, and at some point in the fourth minute she said:

"One one eight. It is one one eight and it is not a page, because the pages in this volume run to seven hundred and twenty-four, and it is not a leaf, because a leaf is a leaf. It is a number of something in a list, and the list is a schedule, and the schedule in this basin that is numbered by the hundred and eighteen is the distribution schedule."

"Then it is the distribution schedule of this city," said Rull, "and we have not got one in this building, and I am not going to be the one to go and fetch it."

"Somebody has got one," said Mara Quill, "and there is a runner in this building and I know him and I am not going to ask you, because a man who is fifty-four and has nineteen years in this building should not be sent across a city for a thing a boy can do."

She went out into the corridor.

---

The boy came back in fifty minutes with a folded sheet under his arm and a face on him, and it was a runner off the lower terrace road with a cattle track of mud on him, and he gave it to Mara Quill and said the name of the man who had given it to him, and Mara Quill wrote the name down.

> *Extract from the closure schedule, lower terrace road, entry 118. Terrace row, lower fold, rows 7 to 12 inclusive. Outfalls: sealed and plated, no key issued, out of service. Item removed from the distribution schedule by consequential correction. Date of entry: twenty-six years ago. The same entry carries the removal of the row from the sheet.*

She read it twice, standing up, in a public reading room on a Friday morning with a restricted volume open on a bench beside her and a man who had been charged twice watching her do it.

"Then it is one act," she said. "Not two documents that resemble each other. One entry, in one schedule, in one year, in one office, and it plated a row's drain and it took a hundred and four names off a survey and it took the last line out of a maintenance book. Twenty-six years ago. All of it before the warden's office at the end of the range, and all of it after the last line in that volume, and the word under the last line is *maintained* and it is in the hand of the man whose initials are on the marker on the west wall."

Nobody said anything in the reading room for a moment. It was a public room and a woman at a desk eleven feet away was stamping a volume, and it was eleven o'clock on a Friday.

"Then that is what it is," said Tarin Rusk. "And I want to say what it is not, because we are in a public room and because it would be very easy for me to say the other thing.

"That is a date and a number and a hand and a piece of paper. It is not a source. It is not a name of a route, and it is not a keeper, and it is not a witness, and it is not a document that anybody may enter a district on, and if I walk down to a warden's office on Monday with a piece of paper with a one-one-eight on it he will tell me, correctly, that a schedule entry is not a head reading, and he will be right.

"I asked for the condition of a gate. I have got the condition of an office. That is what the volume had in it. It is not the same thing and I am not going to stand in a public room and pretend it is, because in three weeks somebody is going to want to know what I said on a Friday morning in Vellum and I would like to have said the true thing."

---

Then Mara Quill sat down and did the thing that she had been dreading since half past ten, which was to write a certificate in a public room with a second reader in it.

She wrote it in four sentences.

> *That the volume consulted is the Ashgate intake maintenance series, folio eleven, one hundred and eighty-one years, four entries a year, withdrawn to restricted handling on the seventeenth of this month on rule nine, and that the reading was had on a scheduled reading day by a named applicant and a second reader of this office, the reader named in the register.*
> *That the last entry in the series, dated the year before the flood, carries a head at wheel of 5·89 and the word maintained beneath it in a second hand, and that the leaf carrying that entry has been removed from the volume and replaced by a leaf of later stock, sizing, and ink.*
> *That the pressure shadow of the removed leaf carries at the end of the last line a schedule reference of three digits, which reads 118; and that an extract from the closure schedule of the lower terrace road, entry 118, dated twenty-six years ago, carries the plating of the terrace outfalls of rows seven to twelve and the removal of that row from the distribution schedule; and that the two are of the same date and the same office and the same year.*
> *That no part of the restricted volume has been copied out. This certificate records the existence, form and date of a passage and a reference. It does not transcribe the reference. Any person may compare it against the closure schedule in the warden's office, which is a public document.*

She read it out in the room. Rull read it after her. Rull agreed with it and signed it.

Rusk read it after both of them, and then he said: "There are three names on the desk list. Mine, yours and hers. There is not one of them on this by itself."

"There are three names on the desk list," said Mara Quill, "and there is one on the certificate in the piece of it that matters, which is the sentence about the hand, and that sentence is going to make a registrar in the commissioner's office examine a man of forty-one who is my mother's brother, and I have put that sentence in because the alternative is a certificate that is weaker by a measurable amount and true, and I have spent the last three weeks teaching a yard the difference between those two things and I am not going to be the exception."

"You told me first."

"I told you the first time, and I am telling you now, and that is condition four and you are going to get it in writing from me at the bottom of the hill if I have to walk down there." She capped the ink. "And the marginal note against the last one is dated a Tuesday in the second month of next year and says no further action is proposed at present. I want you to know that I know that, and I want you to know that I filed it anyway, and that the reason I filed it anyway is that the note was against a provenance note about two documents, and this is not that."

At the desk, the clerk wrote the entry into the register of restricted consultations for the quarter, in the ordinary hand, with the date and the two hours and the folio, and the *purpose* in eleven words, and the names.

The first thing in the last line of that entry was wrong, and it was wrong in a way that only one person in the room knew was wrong, and it took forty minutes to put right.

"I want it amended," said Tarin Rusk, at the desk, at twenty past twelve, with a clerk waiting to close the register for the hour. "I want the district in the purpose, or under the purpose. I am the named applicant and I am the man in box five of a stamped form, and if this register carries a district on it then this register is a document about a district, and a document about a district is a thing a court can be given, and I cannot get one in a court in Vellum on a Friday and I would like to be able to get one before the twenty-seventh of next month."

"You could not have the purpose amended," said the clerk, "because the purpose is the applicant's and I write them as I am given them, and I do not amend them. I write them."

"Then write it in the remarks. That the applicant states the purpose is had for a district. That is not a purpose. That is a sentence, and a sentence is not held to eleven words, and you have told me so yourself."

"That I can do."

She wrote it. And then she said: "I will want the district's name, and its number of houses, and its number of persons, because a name in a register with nothing under it is a thing that people say is a joke, and in nineteen years I have written three of those and I have regretted two."

"Twenty-four houses," said Tarin Rusk, "and a hundred and twenty-six persons, per the warden's return of the thirty-fourth, and the district is the lower terrace road, the blue steps."

The clerk wrote it.

"One hundred and twenty-four," she said, reading it back, "and one hundred and nineteen."

"Yes."

"That is not what you have just said to me," said Mara Quill.

It was not.

He had said twenty-four and a hundred and twenty-six. He had said them in a yard on the ninth in front of a hundred and twenty people with two slates on a step, and he had taken them off a slate that Marda Ollan had taken off a door-to-door count that a woman had taken with a pencil because a man under a stand-down was not allowed to take a census of a place, and in the four seconds it took a clerk to put a nib on a line he had gone back to a number a man invents about a place that is not on a sheet.

Tarin Rusk stood in a public reading room in Vellum and found the floor of it gone, because both figures were in his mouth at once and one of them was a fiction a man in an office invents about a district, and the other was a count taken over a day and a half by door, by a woman who had made herself the easiest person in the row to find on purpose so that it could never be said of her that she was hard to reach.

It was the first time in nineteen years he had caught himself doing the first thing.

"One hundred and nineteen," he said. "I have given you the wrong number. It is one hundred and twenty-six. It was taken on a slate on the twenty-second by the district and not by me, and what I have just given you is the number a man says about a place like this, and I would like the register to have the right one, and I would like the correction in the same hand in the same book, because that is how a register ought to be read, and I have spent a month teaching a yard that."

The clerk looked at him for a moment. Then she struck the figure out with one stroke, in ink, and wrote the true one above it, and wrote beside it: *so entered at the applicant's correction, applicant present.*

Then she turned the book round and pushed it at him so that he could read both entries side by side, which no clerk in nineteen years had ever done for anybody, and had the grace not to look pleased about it.

Then she read the whole of it back, and it went into the register, and it is this:

*Consultation 34. Folio 11, Ashgate intake maintenance series. Restricted, rule nine. Eleventh day of this month, ten o'clock until twenty past one. Applicant: Rusk, T., flow engineer, Water Office, under a stand-down entered in the commissioner's registry. Second reader: Rull, S. Present: Quill, M., conservator.*
*Purpose: to establish the recorded condition of the Ashgate intake main wheel.*
*Remarks: applicant states that the purpose is had for a district, and requests it noted. District: the lower terrace road, the blue steps. Twenty-four houses, ~~one hundred and nineteen~~ one hundred and twenty-six persons, per the warden's return of the thirty-fourth of this month and per a count taken by the district itself. No row. No schedule entry. No keeper. Name entered in this register at the applicant's own hand and not by the office.*
*Reader's note: no such district appears on any schedule in this jurisdiction, and the office has no power to enter one, and the warden's note of the thirty-fourth of this month in the district book of that road is the only document in this city in which the words the blue steps have appeared.*
