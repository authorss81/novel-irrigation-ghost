# Chapter 0035 — A Man Who Pays By The Day

Three of them came up the cattle track on the morning of Tuesday the twenty-second at about ten o'clock, and there was no Guild carriage and no banner, because the Open Channel Guild does not send a banner into a district that is not on a schedule, and a man of some sense in the Guild's counting-house had worked out that a banner on that track on that morning would be worth nothing to anybody who saw it.

Iven Marrow was fifty-four and had been a sluicewright before he was anything else, and he came up the last half mile on foot with his own satchel because the last half mile is the half mile where a man's boots tell you what he is. He was not large. He had the hands of a man who has set a gate bed in cold water, and a way of stopping in the middle of a sentence to look at a thing.

The other two were a surveyor with a level and a young man with a case of bottles.

---

Wick Venn was at the top of the lane at about half past ten, sitting on the coping of the pump's cistern with a barrow and a shovel and a hod, and he had been asked for in a way that made the asking worth repeating.

The man with the bottles had asked for a man who knew the ground. Wick Venn had said he did not know the ground, he knew one street. The man with the bottles had then asked whether he could carry a hod and could push a barrow up a hill in a day, and Wick Venn had said yes, and the man with the bottles had asked what a day was, and Wick Venn had said he did not know that either, and the man with the bottles had taken four shillings out of a leather purse and put it in Wick's right hand and said it was for one day and the rate was the Guild's rate and the rate was the same for everybody and he should not argue about it.

"You are going to clear the store at the top of the works," Iven Marrow said. "The filter-bed store with the hearth in it. It has four hundredweight of junk in it and a shelf of candle ends and it is the only room on that hill with a roof on it that a man can stand up in. We are going to work in it and we are not going to leave a mess in a woman's house."

"That is not my house," said Wick Venn.

"Then it is not going to be left a mess in," said Iven Marrow.

---

The three of them came up the mill road at about two in the afternoon, and Wick Venn went up pushing a barrow, because the switchback is cut in three pitches and a barrow does not walk, and he went up it at the pace of a man who has done nineteen years of carrying things up a hill and who has, since the sixth of this month, discovered that he can carry almost anything at all as long as he is not asked to hold it.

He was on the first pitch when the surveyor came past him with a level under his arm and said, "You are quick for a man with a barrow."

"My left hand does not work," said Wick Venn.

That was the whole of the conversation, and it happened halfway up the second pitch, and the surveyor said "Ah" and went on up, and Iven Marrow heard it and said nothing and then, about an hour later, when the surveyor was not within hearing, said:

"How long."

"Since the sixth. Sixteen days."

"Can you tie."

"I can tie," said Wick Venn. "I cannot hold."

"Then you are more use to me than the man with the level," said Iven Marrow, "and I am going to be careful about how I say that, because a man with a bad hand who has been told he is more use than somebody else usually finds out afterwards that it was said to be kind. It is a trade. There are four men on a barrow in this basin and three of them can hold a hod and one of them can set a knot that will not slip in water, and the one who can set the knot gets put on the ropes, and I have a rope job on the twenty-eighth."

Wick Venn pushed the barrow up the third pitch.

"I have not been on a rope since the sixth," he said, "and I am not going to be on one again, and you can put that in whatever book you keep. I can tie a line to a ring and I can cut it off and I cannot go down a ladder, and if a job wants a man who goes down, then the answer is no and it is not going to be different on Thursday."

"Noted," said Iven Marrow, and made a mark in a small book, which was the first time in this lane that anybody with a satchel had written anything down about a man in it, and which cost him nothing and which Wick Venn did not see.

---

They worked in the store until the light went: four hundredweight out and down the switchback in nine trips, the shelf of candle ends swept, the hearth raked out, a tarpaulin over the floor. Then Iven Marrow sent Wick Venn down and kept the others.

The clearing of that store was the whole of Wick Venn's day, and it was four hours, and it earned four shillings, and at the end of it the surveyor — not Marrow — put a hand in his pocket and gave him another four, and Wick Venn said that was not the arrangement.

"It is a bonus for a job done well," said the surveyor.

"It is a job done well in four hours with a barrow that has to be pushed up a hill by a man with one hand," said Wick Venn. "Nine years at the Reach was a shilling and six a day for six days, and in the winter a man could get a quid, and I do not get a quid any more and I am not going to get one out of a bonus, because the day that a bonus is a shilling and six is a day I am going to be glad about is a bad day."

He took the four shillings and went down the hill.

At the bottom of the track a woman from the top end waited with a slate, because the woman from the top end was always waiting with a slate, and she said that a man had come in at the top of the lane and had asked whether a person could carry a hod, and Wick Venn said that a man had come in at the top of the lane and had asked, and that he had said yes.

The woman wrote it down, and then looked at him, and said, "You have not said what you were paid."

"I have not been asked what I was paid," said Wick Venn.

"I am asking."

"Four shillings," said Wick Venn, "and a shilling of it was a bonus I did not want, and I have given it back, and I have told you that for no reason except that I would rather somebody in this lane knew it than found out in a fortnight from a carter."

And he went home, and nobody in the blue steps thanked him, and nobody mentioned it again for six weeks.

---

Iven Marrow came down the hill on the Wednesday and asked for the cistern, and Tarin Rusk took him to it, and stood over it with him for two hours without saying very much, which was the correct thing to do.

The man read the coping, and the chalk, and the dates, and the two figures for the twentieth, and then he read Marda Ollan's ledger, which was produced without being asked, and then he read Yara Sen's book, which was not produced, and which he asked for twice, and which was refused twice, in a yard, in daylight, by a woman of forty-one with a full week's argument about it.

"Two refusals," he said. "I am going to write on my sheet that I asked twice and was refused twice, and I would like you to be in the yard when I write it."

"You may write it."

"I would like a man I have not met to be in the yard when I write it," said Iven Marrow. "That is a habit and not an insult. A thing written in a room gets read in a room four years later by somebody who was not there, and a thing written in a yard gets read in a yard, and I have lost an argument once already by being the only person in the room and I have thought about it for eleven years."

Then he went up the hill, and he went down the two hundred and forty-one steps, all of them, with a lamp, on the twenty-third, and he was gone four hours, and he came up and said four things about it in the yard in about nine minutes, and one of them was the most useful thing anybody from outside that lane had said in a month, and one of the other three I have thought about since and have not been able to put down.

"The ledge is dressed and the kerb is set and the line is cut at the water and there has been no wet mark on the ledge, and there has been no drift on it, and that is a chamber somebody looked after, and I am going to say something to you now that I have said at four other works in this basin and I have been wrong at two of them: I believe the water in there has not moved in a long time, and I cannot tell you in a hand's breadth, because I have no instrument for a standing body and neither have you, and the honest end of that is that somebody with a gauging bottle and a titrate ought to have been up this hill forty years ago and there is not one in this basin."

"That is what I have written in a signed document," said Tarin Rusk.

"Yes. I have read it. It went past the office of the acting commissioner on the fifteenth of this month, which is a piece of paper I did not know existed until Monday, and it is on my desk in a copy of a book that came up the cattle track with a return of supervision, and I would like to tell you that I have read it three times and that I think it is the best-kept document anybody has produced about a chamber in this basin in my working life, and I would like to tell you also that it is going to be read in about six weeks by a man who will be pleased with it, because it says a head of five point eight nine metres and a chamber that is intact, and both of those are things a form likes."

"I have never said the chamber was intact in a form."

"You said it in a form. On the fifteenth, paragraph two. And you also said, in paragraph four, that there are eleven houses and thirty-four persons on the roof of it and a well in one of them, and I am going to come back to paragraph four, and not today, and I would like you to know that I have not forgotten it and that I am not going to forget it, and that is not a threat and there is nothing in my book about you at all."

---

He said the other thing at the bottom of the mill road, in the failing light, with a lamp on the barrow and Wick Venn three hundred yards off at the pump with a piece of chalk, and he said it without any weight on it at all, as though it were a fact about the weather.

"There is a gauge plate in that store," he said. "Have you looked at it?"

"There is a board."

"There is a board, and the board says five and nine for the last two years and five and seven for the two years before that, and you have written that down and said nobody wrote down why it changed. I can give you a why. A gauge plate is a brass ring with a bore in it, and a plate of a bigger bore reads a bigger number at the same head, and a man paid by the can is not going to argue with a plate about a bore, he is going to write down what the plate says. I have seen a board like that once in nine years. A new plate went in and nobody wrote a line about it, because a plate is not a work, it is a part."

"Thirty yards from that board," said Tarin Rusk, "in a deal box, in a store with a lock on it, there are two certificates. Both in the hand of the acting commissioner. One at decommissioning, one four years ago, and both of them certify that the chamber is void, that there is no residual head, that there is no standing water, and that no maintenance is required, and the word *maintained* is under the second one in the same pen."

Iven Marrow looked at him.

"I know what that is," he said. "And I am going to say three things and then we are going to go down this hill.

"First: that is a gauge plate explanation, and I am offering it to you because it is the best explanation available and I have offered it to myself, and I have known about those two sheets since the twenty-first, because a return of supervision goes through a counting-house and a counting-house has a store, and a store has a deal box in it, and I did not ask for them and I would not have opened them if I had not been sent to this works at all.

"Second: I do not know whether it is true. I am telling you that I have offered you the explanation that protects the man who signed them, and that I have done it before I have asked you a single question about them, and you may draw whatever you like from the order of those two things.

"And third. The plate explanation is about the board. It is not about the certificates. Nobody certifies a chamber void because a plate is the wrong bore. A plate changes a figure somebody wrote in a day-book. A certificate is a document, and a document takes a hand and a decision and a reason, and there is no bore in that, and if you are ever in a room where somebody is explaining those two sheets to you with a brass ring in his hand, that is the man, and not the ring."

He picked up his satchel.

"And I will say one more thing and then I am going to stop, because you have been in that lane since the fourth and I have watched you stand at a coping for two hours without putting your hand in the water once, which I would have done by Tuesday, and there is a thing I have been turning over since the twenty-second and I am not going to keep it.

"The Guild's high gate at Sallow has read dry for six years. Six years against a schedule that says it is fed from a catchment that has never failed and has never once been dry in a wet winter or a bad one. Our own men read it. Our own men are the ones who read it. I have had a plate out of it and a new plate in it and I have had a man go and stand under the channel at the far end with a rod, and there is nothing coming, and I have not got a story that fits it, and I have not got anybody in the Guild who thinks the story is in this basin, and I have had the reading card for six years and been in the counting-house four months, and I can put a name on a thing nobody here has ever heard of, and I cannot put a number on the gate at Sallow, and it is the only gate in the basin's history that I have not been able to put a number on."

"You have come three days to a district that is not on a schedule," said Tarin Rusk, "to tell me about a gate at Sallow."

"I have come three days to a district that is not on a schedule," said Iven Marrow, "because somebody above me wanted this works looked at by people who could be relied on to find nothing, and I have known that since I was handed the order on the nineteenth and I have not pretended otherwise and I have not come to find nothing. I have come because on the twenty-first I read a return of supervision that names a district and no man, and a physician's statement with a jar in it, and an engineer's statement with a head in it, and three documents in four days in a lane this city's own water office has not set foot in since the ration began, and I have been doing this for thirty-one years and I know what a place looks like when it is being kept by hand, because I am one of the people who is paid to keep places by hand, and there is not one person in that lane who is being paid for any of it."
