# Chapter 0033 — Somebody Who Did Not Ask

The horse came up the mill road on the Saturday evening of the nineteenth with a deal box of harness on its back and a man leading it who had walked it up from the cattle track at the fenner turn, and eleven people in the row at Ashgate watched it come and none of them said anything, because a settlement of thirty-four people that has lived on the top of a works for thirty years has a long habit of not saying anything about what a horse is doing at the top of it.

Coll Preece had not asked the district of a hundred and twenty-six people. He had not asked the commissioner's office. He had not asked the Water Office, or the warden's station, or a man with a bar and a blue book who had been up the hill that morning. He had asked a man in the fenner district with a horse that was not his, and he had paid him a day and a half's wage out of his own pocket, and he had put the sweep clear and taken the fresh-sawn oak chock out from under the beam's heel with his own hands.

He told the girl at the well-head at about eight o'clock, and he told her because she was the only person on that works who was likely to be awake, and what he said was: "If a man comes up here tomorrow with a bar, I did not have your leave and I would not have wanted it, and you can tell him that and I will not blame you for saying it."

"You have not asked us," said the girl.

"No."

"Then I shall not say you did and I shall not say you did not."

"You can say what you like," said Coll Preece. "I have told you because there are thirty-four of you and a horse on a works that the city sealed, and if you do not know about it then somebody who should have told you did not, and I would rather you heard it from a man who fills cans than from a form."

He led the horse up the mill road himself on the Friday, and at twenty past three in the morning of the twentieth he started the plant with a lamp on the beam and his own hand on the horse, because the boy had not been paid since the second and was not going out in the dark again to hold a horse for a plant that had not run for seventeen days, and he had sent him home on the Friday at eight with four days' warning in his own mouth and the boy's wages for the week in his coat.

The four-bar sweep came off its pinion at a quarter past four with a noise like a cart reversing off a bank of shingle, and the walking beam began to lift, and the pump rod went down into a bore that had been standing full since the second, and the column came up, and the rising main took it, and at about twenty to five the first of it arrived in the blue steps.

Niko Venn was asleep on the wall of the dispensary awning and heard it.

---

He was in the yard with a stick and a chalk at about six, because he was up at six every morning now and had been since the ninth, and the water in the tank was up a finger's width, and he wrote the number and the date on the coping and then stood looking at the tank for a while with the chalk in his hand.

It came up a finger's width in the first ten minutes and a hand's width by the hour, and the hand's width was a rate he had not seen since the twentieth day of the month before, in a yard with a warden and a stone, and he wrote the two of them down side by side and then wrote underneath it: *No log in. No setting. No meter of any kind. This is not the same as the twentieth.*

By eight there were ninety people in the yard and somebody had turned the butt at the top end over and stood a bucket in it, and the woman who had put the butt on a stand on the fourth evening of the month was putting pails into it and taking them out full, and a man down past the arch was shouting that the water was up at the arch, and Old Marden was on his step in his shirt with the mat over his shoulder at seven in the morning, which is a thing he has not done since the spring.

"Well," said Old Marden. "Well, well."

---

Yara Sen came down at about half past eight and put her hand in the tank and brought up a stoppered jar, and then went back up the lane to the dispensary, and then came back down with the other jar, which she had taken out of that tank herself with her own hand on the evening of the eighteenth and had been carrying about ever since.

She boiled a pint of each of them down in a glazed jar on a ring on the step outside the dispensary, in the open, in front of anybody who wanted to stop, and it took her the better part of an hour because she was doing two, and she did not go in.

"Both of them are salt," she said, when the ring had gone cold. "Both of them are salt enough to write with on a glazed jar and let it dry, and I have had a basin boiled down twice a week since the second of this month and it has been salt every time.

"And here is what I want said in a yard. This is the eighteenth. This is this morning. Look at them. I am not going to give you a number, because I have not got an instrument that gives me a number, and I am not going to invent one to be impressive in front of nine children. Both crusts are white. The one on the right has more of it, and it has run up the glaze in a ridge where the other has not, and I would not stake a child's dinner on that with a pint and a ring and an hour's boiling in it.

"What I will stake a child's dinner on is this. Four days ago, on the sixteenth, I stood in this yard and told a hundred and forty people that the salt in this tank is not being used up, it is being left, because the water goes out of the top of it fifty gallons a day and the sun takes the water and not the salt. And I told them that a fortnight of that is a fortnight of a tank getting stronger, and that on the ninth it was one thing and on the sixteenth it was a different thing out of the same hole.

"And I said the first water out of that rising main would be the worst of it, because the main has been standing full and still for seventeen days and everything that has come off the inside of a pipe in seventeen days comes off it at once when you start moving it again.

"I said both of those things on the sixteenth, in daylight, in a yard, and I did not enjoy saying them, and I have been wrong in this yard once and I have it written down in a book in front of four people, and this time I would like somebody to check me. Will somebody check me."

Nobody said anything, and then Niko Venn said, "I will, and I cannot read a word, so I will have to get somebody to read it to me and I will have to trust them, and I have already been told by a woman in this lane that that is how things are done here."

---

What was happening at the top of the mill road at the same hour was that Nell Drust put a six-gallon pail on her well-head slab at ten past six in the morning because a woman doing a thing out of a kitchen floor for sixteen years does not get to be surprised at it twice, and she had a watch, and when it had not filled at three and a half minutes she did not say anything to anybody and she put her thumb on the rim and counted.

She wrote it on the back of a shutter with a bit of chalk, and then put the pail down in the middle of the row on the flags again, because a row of thirty-four people needed to see it.

"Two miles up this hill there is a pump that a man started at twenty past three this morning without asking us, and I have not got a horse, and I have not got a boy to hold one, and I have not been down that hole in my life and I am not starting on a bad leg.

"So that is the trade. The horse trades the well. And I have had two days' notice and no more, and I want it wrote down that I had two days' notice and not none, because I have heard it said in four buildings this month that nobody decides anything, and no, somebody decided it, at twenty past three this morning, and it was not the city and it was not the row, and it was a man who fills cans and has not been paid since the second of this month and who has a boy on a staging who is not to be sent out in the dark again.

"I am not going to stand here and be frightened at a man for doing a thing I would do, and I am not going to stand here and be grateful either. I am going to say the thing that has to be said, which is: if that pump keeps drawing, this well keeps dropping, and the day it comes down to forty gallons an hour we will be going to the far well, and the far well is dry, and there is a cistern at the bottom end of the row that the row filled once in the year before the flood and has not been touched since because we have never needed to touch it.

"And I do not know what is in that cistern. Nobody in this row has ever opened it. It has been in the ground under eleven houses for thirty years with a lid on it, and there is a pump on this works drawing off the same water it is standing in, and I would like a man with a bar who is not frightened of me to come and look at the lid of it before anybody in this row is thirsty."

She looked at Tarin Rusk, who was standing at the end of the row with a pail of his own in his hand.

"I am not asking you today," she said. "I am asking you to have heard me say it in front of twenty-eight people."

---

He did come up on the twenty-second, and that is another day.

The rest of the twentieth went on the way such a day goes. The tank in the yard came up to the coping by about two in the afternoon, which is four thousand seven hundred gallons in a tank that holds four thousand seven hundred, and there was nowhere for the rest of it to go, because this lane had had a lid of iron with four bolts on it over its only outfall since a city bolted it there twenty-six years ago, and thirty years before that the lane had been a closed sponge.

At half past three the flags by the throat of the yard were soft underfoot, and at four the flag at the corner nearest the throat came up about a quarter of an inch and settled back, and at twenty past four the water came back through the joints, and it came back not clear.

Ondra was out of the low house before it did, because Ondra has been listening to that floor since the thirtieth of the month, and he had the boy awake and had him into a chair at the top of the lane and had his own bed frame off the wall and out into the flags, and the whole of that household's belongings went up on the flags of the top end under a tarpaulin that had not been a tarpaulin in its life and had come from Wick Venn's cart on the second time that cart had been used for somebody else.

He found Tarin Rusk at the wall of the arch and said, in a voice with nothing in it at all:

"You are going to tell me you did not know."

"I did not know," said Tarin Rusk. "I knew the pump would come and I knew the tank would fill and I told a physician on the sixteenth that I could not tell her what the body of water would do, and I have been up that hill this morning telling thirty-four people that their well may change, and a woman up there has been timing it since six o'clock this morning and has written it on a shutter."

"Who told you to start it."

"Nobody. A man called Preece started it at twenty past three this morning with a horse he hired out of his own wage, and he did not ask me, and I was asleep when he did it, and I would have argued with him if I had been awake and I am not certain I would have won and I am not certain I would have tried."

Ondra looked at him for a while.

"My boy cannot walk," he said. "He was in that chair on the thirtieth and he is in it now and he is not getting out of it, and the woman at the top end has a shelf of stuff on the top step and I have put him on a board because a chair is not a bed, and that is a Sunday and I have a landlord's inspection on Tuesday and a rate on it that I cannot pay in this weather, and a man came up the cattle track on Friday and read out a piece of paper and one of the boxes on it has something in it about water supply in connection.

"So do not tell me about the sixteenth. Tell me the thing you would actually say if it were your floor."

"I would say that your floor is going to be wet this evening and again tomorrow morning, and that I would take the family off the flags tonight and put them in the top end on the boards, and that the water will begin to go down in about three days because the ground has to give it back and not before, and that if the water comes up over a foot in that room I would take the boy out of the chair and put him on the top step, because a chair is furniture and a step is a way out of a door."

Ondra considered that.

"That is a thing," he said. "All right."

---

At about five in the afternoon three children came into the dispensary from three different streets inside half an hour, which had happened once before in that lane and had happened on a Tuesday in a book, and Yara Sen dealt with them in the order she dealt with everything, which was the youngest first, and the youngest was not one of them.

The fourth came in at twenty past six, carried up the lane by a woman of about thirty with a shawl over her head, and she was seven and she had been sick twice that day and had asked her mother three times where the front door was, and the mother had said it three times.

Yara Sen had the book open on the counter and the boy's pads and the mother's hands and about nine other people in the room.

"Four," she said, and wrote it, and then stopped writing, because a thing that has been written down can be read back to the room, and this room had learned in the last month not to be told a number twice without somebody asking how it was got. It was three on the twenty-eighth of the month before and three on the sixth and three again on the ninth, and each time it had been for longer, and it came up to four this morning.

She put her hand flat on the closed book.

"Sit down at that table," she said to the mother. "I am going to do what I have done every third day for a month and I am going to do it in front of you this time, because you are the one person in this room whose business it is, and because a mark that goes up to four in front of the person who has to live with it is worth more in a book than a mark that goes up to four at night while she is asleep."

She looked at the pads of the index and middle fingers and the web between them for a good deal longer than she needed to, and the room waited.

"It is four," she said, "and it has not gone down since this morning, and that is the finding. Not that she is worse. That she is not better. A mark that is not going away is a mark that has stopped being an early thing and started being part of a child, and I have not got a word in this basin for what comes after that, and I am not going to invent one for a room.

"And I have got one thing to say about the window and I am going to say it now. She will ask again. She asked three times today and she will ask tomorrow and she may ask for a month, and that is the fourth thing this sickness does and it is not the last, and it does not come back by itself. She needs to be able to find that door in the dark, and so do you, and so does every other person in that house, and I am going to give you three things to say to her and I am going to say them twice, and I am going to make you say the third one back to me before you leave this room."

She said the three things. The third one was the name of the step outside the door.

Then she wrote the entry in the book, and dated it, and put the jar on the step, and it was the twentieth day of the month and the tank in the yard was at the coping and there was a hand's depth of grey water in the low room on the left of the arch and a settlement of thirty-four people on a hill was timing a well that had lost a third of itself in a day, and the district had drunk its last clean water that morning and had ten gallons of clean left in a churn and a jar of eleven and a butt on a stand and a tank that could not be emptied, and Niko Venn wrote both figures on the coping in chalk, one over the other, with the dates on them, which is the first time in this lane that a tank has had two figures on it in one day.
