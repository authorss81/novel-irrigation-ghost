# Chapter 0001 — The Gauge at Ashgate

Rain, when it came at all, came the way a creditor comes. It was already in the air when Tarin Rusk started up the last hundred yards of the Ashgate access track, and by the time he reached the intake house it had settled into something too fine to measure honestly, the kind of rain that gets into a collar and does nothing at all for anybody's crops.

Below him the city was coming out of its doors. That was the thing he would remember afterwards, and not the gate. Forty thousand people woke every morning now to the sound of somebody else's bucket hitting a lid, and a morning like this one broke a rule so deep in them that they came out into it with basins and jars held over their heads like people at a burial who have heard the wrong name. The fountains on the terrace road had a posted schedule. Cisterns had a posted number. Nobody had posted anything for rain.

He stood on the intake platform and watched it fail. It reached the middle terraces and stopped. It never got the chance to disappoint anyone at the bottom, which is where he had lived until he was seven.

The chalk board at the upper pump house had said what it said now, before dawn: *low terrace cistern rows, twenty days. Low pump tank, two.* Tarin had copied the figure into the blue book and underlined it, because he was the kind of man who underlined nine. Nine days was not a promise. It was what was in the tanks, less what the city drank, less what the pumps lost, less what the dead slots under the terrace row gave back when the pipes rang and went cold.

He shifted the recalibration kit onto the bench inside the intake house: the brass float gauge in its oilcloth, the folding rule, a two-pound bar, a chalk stub, and the blue book with the waxed cover. The midsummer ration review was set for the twenty-second of the month, and would be set for the twenty-second again after that, and again after that, and the only number that had changed since the last postponement was the one on the board.

The intake house was a stone room the size of a small cart, half sunk into the hillside, its doorway worn into a curve by a century of shoulders going in bent. Inside, the well was dry to the floor. That was the first thing, and it was the first thing wrong.

The old mark was nine feet up the wall, a painted line, and above it the wall had been limewashed and below it had been left, so the whole history of the chamber could be read as a set of horizontal marks going down toward the floor like a tide going out. He had walked this room once a month for the twenty-one months since the works were sealed, and twice in the last four months because the Office wanted a figure for a file, and in all of those visits the well had been dry and the gauge had read nothing. He had written *no residual head* in the book five times and once *structure void, no measurable pressure*, and had let the Office close the file on the strength of it.

He unwrapped the float gauge and set it on the bench.

Calibrating a decommissioned float gauge is not delicate work, but it is fussy, and there is a great deal of it. He checked the wire for kinks by drawing it through his closed fist. He checked the float for a leak by setting it in a tub and pressing it down with two fingers and watching whether it came up honest or came up buoyant. He checked the stops. He checked the stilling tube, which was the brass finger that hung down the well and let pressure in from below without letting the well's own slop up the wire, and which was the part that always told you the truth about what was underneath.

The float came up honest. The wire ran clean. He lowered the gauge into the well on its wire and let it find its level.

It came up hard against the top stop.

Tarin frowned at it. A float that pins at the top stop is a float with pressure coming up the stilling tube faster than the chamber can equalise it, or a float that is mechanically stuck, and he had a rule about which of those he could tell apart without taking anything apart. He gave the wire a shake. The float dropped an inch and sprang straight back and pinned again, and the wire went into a slow shiver he could feel through his gloves.

He closed the tap.

The float sank to the bottom stop and lay there.

He opened the tap a quarter turn. The float climbed the whole scale in the time it took him to blink and hit the top stop hard enough to ring the bell wire.

"Five point eight nine," he said to the empty room.

He wrote it down. Then he wrote the time, and then he wrote *stilling tube live, pressure from below, source not gate* and looked at the two words *from below* for a while before he underlined them.

Because that was the problem. The intake gate was the Ashgate main wheel, forty feet back along the conduit, and it had been shut, chained and sealed for six years, and he had dogged it himself the last time, twenty-one months ago, and signed for the dogging. There was no water above it, the well was dry, and the wind came in under the door and made the wire sing. And there was a column of water five point eight nine metres tall standing under a floor that was not supposed to have anything under it.

He had been right about this room for six years. He knew exactly how he had been right, and he was now going to find out exactly how he was wrong, which is the difference between an engineer and a man with a lucky guess.

The pressure gallery door was behind the conduit, at the foot of the stair, and the inspection gate inside it was what the recalibration would have to pass through. He had the key on his belt. It was a square-head inspection gate, four feet by two, cast iron, set in a housing that had not been moved since the Orisene works were new. Six years of silt in the gallery had gone into the housing and hardened there, and iron on iron with silt between them does not open; it welds.

He got the bar off his belt and set it in the gap and put his shoulder into it.

The first push did nothing at all, and he felt the gate move about a millimetre, and the second push moved it the width of his thumbnail, and by the fourth he had both feet set and his whole weight coming down through the bar and his left shoulder going into a noise that would be with him for a week.

Then the silt gave.

The gate came off its seat with a bang that came back off the tunnel and came back again, and water came through the gap in a flat white sheet, and hit the gallery floor and did the thing that Tarin had been arguing with a record about for six years.

It ran uphill.

Not a splash, not a pressure jet that curved and fell. The head came through the opening and found a floor, and the floor went *up*. The gallery outlet channel under the intake house had a grade of about a foot in the hundred yards, laid that way on purpose by somebody who wanted the overflow to climb away from the building rather than undermine it, and there was six years of dead leaves and grey dust lying in it like felt. The water went over all of that without slowing, filled the first four yards of it, and climbed.

Tarin stood in the doorway with the bar in both hands and watched a dry stone channel carry water uphill, out of a well, away from a gate that had been shut for six years, at six in the morning, in the first measurable rain in fourteen months.

He was aware of several things at once and did not enjoy any of them. He was aware that there was no rain on the mountain. He was aware that no pipe on the intake side was open, and that the wheel gate was chained, and that a hundred and eighty-one years of flood law exists because men have made exactly this mistake. He was aware that he had just opened a sealed structure with a bar, at a time he was not authorized to be on the platform, and that the standing rain had nothing whatever to do with it.

The water was residual. That was the word. It had been sitting under a floor for six years under a pressure nobody had troubled to relieve because every man with a chain of custody had signed a piece of paper that said *structure void* and gone home.

He put the bar down and went to the wheel gate.

It did not move. He put the bar in the stem housing and leaned on it until his teeth gritted and got a quarter of an inch, which told him the stem was not seized at all, it was *loaded*, and the load was the five point eight nine metres standing in the gallery right now with nowhere to go. If he got that gate shut he would be closing a door on a hundred and fifty feet of water, and every man in this city who ever had a wet coat knew what a door like that does when you close it fast.

He came off the wheel and stood in the water with his sleeve to the elbow.

The kit had gone in. The float was under the gallery floor somewhere with the wire, and the wire had carried a great deal of grit, and the float came up in his hand grey and gritty, and on the flat of it was a bloom that was not rust. It came off on his thumb and felt like ground glass, and it smelled faintly of turned-over stone, and Tarin Rusk had handled enough bad water in his life to know that a wet channel does not come out of a stone building clean and it certainly does not come out of a chamber that has been shut since the year the mountain road was cut.

He wiped the float on his coat because there was nowhere better, and got the blue book out with his right hand, and wrote the reading down, and wrote the time, and wrote the word *unsealed inspection gate* because it had to be on the page in his own writing that he had opened it, and then he stopped and looked at the line for a while and added, *by bar, at 06:11, before the warden's check.*

Eight words. He had more entries in that book than he had meals, and he had never once written down anything he had not been told.

Then he shut the book and went to the platform rail, because there was one thing he needed before he went down the hill, and it was not an answer, it was a direction.

The water in the gallery was going south-west, down a channel that left the intake hillside and joined an old covered run under the terrace. He knew that run. Everyone in the Water Office knew that run, because it was on the sheet: *terrace overflow, row seven to twelve, outfall to lower terrace main, level four, no settlement beyond*. He could follow the channel downhill for six miles with his hand on the wall and find where it came out.

It took him four hours, because six miles of covered run is not six miles of anything, and because he had to climb down off the intake hillside and pick the run up again at the old culvert near the mill, and because he stopped twice to sit down.

The rain had stopped before he reached the mill. By the time he came off the terrace road onto the lane above the lower fold, the light had gone flat and white, and the city below him was steaming very slightly where the wet stone was giving the morning back to the air.

The run went under the terrace at a culvert he knew by name and did not know by heart, and it came out on the far side of the fold at a low arch with the water still in it, still moving, still climbing its foot in the hundred. He could not see where it went after the arch. He could hear it, and under the sound of it he could hear a door being worked on with a bar, and somebody saying a name over and over to somebody who was not answering.

Tarin put his hand flat on the arch and stood there with the grit drying in his sleeve.

The sheet in his head said the run was *level four, no settlement beyond*. He had signed four inspections against that line. He had a bar, a wet coat, an open gate, a book with a confession in it, and no way at all of telling that arch from any of the dozen other arches on that terrace without the row sheet, and the row sheet was nailed up inside the warden's station half a mile back up the road with the rest of the standing ration and the closure notices.

There was only one thing worse than being wrong about a row of houses. It was being wrong about a row of houses with water moving through it while you found out.

He turned back up the hill.
