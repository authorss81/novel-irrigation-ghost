# Chapter 0024 — Beneath The Houses

The adit was four feet high and six feet wide and had been driven into the rock by men with a bar and a lamp, and the sets of pine that held it up were squared to a pattern, and every fourth one was a good one.

Niko Venn was at the first bend, sitting on a piece of packing case with the lamp standing on a ledge of rock beside him, and he had made a camp.

That was the first thing Tarin Rusk understood, and it took about two seconds, and it was the difference between a boy who had gone into a hole and a man who had gone into a hole.

The floor under him was dry and swept. There was a stone with a notch cut in it and three stones beside it with a notch cut in them, and a fifth stone half notched, and the notches were cut with a knife, small, careful, and in a row, and they meant days.

"Four," said Niko. "I know it is only two. I am counting in part-days, because a part-day is what I have, and because if I get to three full days I am going to have to do something about the oil, and I would rather do that on purpose."

He had rationed the lamp to half an hour at a time and four goes a day, and he had been to the end of the adit four times, and he had come back every time.

"Are you hurt?"

"No." Niko Venn said it in a flat voice, and then he stopped, and then he started again. "I am not hurt and I am not lost and I have done everything right, and I need to say that first, in case anybody starts. I made a camp. I swept it. I went down twice, and the first time I went down I took a chalk and I could not write anything I could read in the dark, and the second time I took no chalk at all, and I went down and stood at the edge and I did not go over it, and I came back up. Both times. Yes."

"Then show us."

They were on the ledge and in the chamber and it was the morning of the seventh day, and it had taken them the whole of the afternoon and the whole of the night of the sixth to get there, because the adit goes down and it turns twice and a man carrying a lamp in it does not hurry.

---

The adit ran four hundred and some yards and it went down, and it turned twice, and on the second turn the air stopped being the air of a works and became something else.

Tarin noticed it before he could have explained it. It was not cold and it was not warm and it was not moving. It was a pressure, behind the ear, the way a thumb pressed into a soft place is a pressure and not a sound, and it had the quality of a wall on the other side of which a street was going on with its business.

He put his hand on the wall. The wall was damp and it was dressed stone and there was a groove in it at hand height a hand wide and a half deep, and the groove was a channel, and the channel had been a conduit once, and it was dry.

They came out of the adit on a ledge.

---

The chamber was the largest enclosed space Tarin Rusk had ever been inside, and he had been inside the low room of the terraced filter houses at Vellum and thought that was a large room.

There was no end to it in the direction the ledge ran, which was north-east, and the light did what light does in a place like that, which is to come in as far as a lamp can reach and then stop. The floor of the ledge was dressed stone, six feet wide, with a kerb on the water side that was nine inches high and had been set by somebody who wanted the water stopped and not the people. The walls were tooled, in courses, with the chisel marks still in them, in a rock that was not limestone.

And the water was there.

It was absolutely still. It was clear to the bottom as far as the light went, and it had a level, and the level was not a guess. There was a mark cut in the rock at the water line, a single line an inch deep and a foot long, cut the way a man cuts a line to say *I have been here and this is where it is*.

Tarin got the folding rule out of his coat, and the piece of string he kept a knuckle-length of on the loop of it for marking, and he put one end of the string on the ledge and dropped the other and let it settle, and then he counted it off against the rule and then he did it again with a lump of chalk on a stick because a folded rule held against a plumb line in a lamp is a folded rule held against a plumb line in a lamp.

> *Level of water above floor of adit at mouth: 9 ft 4 in.*
> *Floor of adit at mouth is the floor inside the filter house door: level.*
> *Outside ground at the filter house door, above that floor: 1 ft 9 in.*
> *Therefore, water stands eleven feet one inch above the outside ground at the door of this works.*

He wrote it on the page in the blue book with his right hand, and he wrote it twice, and the second time he wrote the sum out longhand, because a man who has been charged over a figure should never write a figure in one operation.

He looked at it for a while.

Eleven feet and an inch of water, standing, under a lane, in a basin in the rock, in a hill three miles from a city of six thousand people, and there was no wet mark on it, and no weed, and no drift on the ledge, and the mark cut in the rock was at the level of the water and the water was at the level of the mark.

Then he went to the edge and looked up.

---

It took him a long time to see it, and he was not able to see it for a while, and that was the honest truth of it and he wrote that down too.

The roof of the chamber was not far away. It was up where a roof is, eleven or twelve feet, and it was a curve in the stone where the top of the excavation had been left to span, and the curve was grey and it was the underside of a floor.

Flagstones, laid on putty beds, with the pattern of the joints still in the bed and the dust of somebody's cellar stuck in the corners.

A hearth. Not the stump of one. A hearth, with the fireback, and the flue going up into the curve, and the back of the chimney breast standing eleven feet above a chamber of standing water, in a building in a district of a hundred and twenty-six people who had been drinking it out of a cistern for a month.

A floor drain, with a lead stub of it hanging, cut off, forty feet away and up in the corner.

And a door, on the same side, higher up, with a wooden stair going up to it in eleven steps that had been left hanging over the water with nothing at the top of them.

He went down off the ledge into the chamber and stood on its floor, and the water came to about the middle of his thigh, and it was cold, and it did not move when he moved, and it did not come off his leg. He put his good hand flat on the underside of a flagstone and it was warm.

He took his hand away and put it on again, because he wanted to be sure.

---

The stone was at the head of the ledge, where the wall and the ledge met, and it was not on a plinth, because it had never needed to be: it was set into the rock of the wall itself, low down, at the height of a man sitting.

Tarin Rusk got the lamp and read it.

The name was cut once and then cut again in front of the first set, shallower, half an inch out of register, the way a person reads a thing through a dirty window, and the second set had been done by somebody who had run out of time or patience or both, and the second set was the better cut of the two.

Under the name were two lines, and a date, and a witness hand at the foot of it, and the whole of the lower half had been taken by damp.

He put his hand flat on it.

The lamp went out.

---

Not out. *Away.* The lamp was on the ledge, three feet from his hand, and it was still on the ledge, and the chamber went on being dark, and he understood before he had finished understanding it that the dark was not the absence of anything.

There was a pressure. He had a word for it and the word was *weight*, and the weight was not on him and not in the room and not under his hand, and the way it came was the way a sound comes up through a floor from a room where people are working and you cannot make out the words.

Then it was not weight.

It was a room with a lot of people in it going about their work at the same time and not one of them for him, and it was not a crowd shouting, and it was not a crowd answering, and the thing that made it a crowd was that there was no silence anywhere in it and no beat in it, and the thing that made it a *room* was that it had a floor and a ceiling and the noise stayed on the floor.

> **The Gauge:** This is not beneath the works. It is beneath the houses.

He took his hand off the stone and put it back and the words did not change, and he took his hand off it and stood in the middle of a chamber with eleven feet one inch of water in it under a hearth and a hundred and twenty-six people, and the pressure did not stop.

---

He gave it an hour. He gave it an hour because that is what he would have done with a stuck valve, and because the alternative was to stand in it and be moved by it, and he had watched a man in a yard once be moved by a thing and had not been able to argue with him afterwards.

The first thing he tried was counting. He got to nine, and lost it, and started again, and got to eleven, and on the third attempt he could not separate the same two, and at the fourth attempt he stopped, because a count you cannot reproduce is not a count.

Then he tried to time them, and there was no gap. Not one silence in an hour, and the sound did not rise and fall, and the pressure behind his ear did not go up and down with it, and that was the thing that told him this was not a room with people in it: a room with people in it breathes. A room with people in it has somebody moving a chair.

After that he tried speech, and he tried it properly, standing up, in the middle of a chamber of standing water under the floor of a house, in the voice he used to brief a crew at a fault.

"I am here."

He waited. He said his own name. He waited again.

Then he said his mother's name, once, and took it straight back out of the air, because a name put into a thing that has not asked for it is a thing he had refused to do to a man on a stone in Vellum and he was not going to do it to a wall in a hole.

With both hands flat on the stone he asked it, through the stone, for anything at all: a number, a direction, a date, a word with a vowel in it.

Nothing in the pressure changed. Not one grain of it. The work went on at the floor of it at the rate it had gone on at, and it had not been doing it for him before he arrived and it was not going to do it for him now that he had asked.

And that was the answer, and it was the whole of the answer, and he was obliged to sit down on a kerb in the middle of a chamber of water in the dark with it and take it.

---

The work was work.

That was the thing he could not get past and could not get out from under for the rest of his life. There was no lament in it. There was no accusation, no plea, no bargain, and nobody in it was asking him for anything, and the crowd was not a crowd that had been wronged, it was a crowd that was late.

A man was saying a number, the same number, over and over, and the number had the shape of a reading, and after an hour Tarin Rusk was fairly sure it was five and seven, or five and eight, and he could not get the rest of it and could never afterwards get the rest of it.

A woman was calling a child's name, twice, and then not, and then something else.

Two men were arguing about a boundary, patiently, the way men argue about a boundary that has been argued about since before either of them was born, and one of them said *that is the low field* and the other said *it has never been the low field* and the first said *it has always been the low field* and neither of them was excited and both of them meant it.

Somebody was counting out loud in tens, and going past a hundred, and starting again, which meant they were counting stock and not money.

Somebody said a price.

Somebody told somebody else to bring the ladder.

A woman said the water was up to the third step.

And a child asked one question, over and over, in a voice with no patience left in it at all, and it did not get answered, and it did not get louder, and it did not stop.

And four women were saying things about pumps at the same time.

One of them was giving directions. *Down past the low one, not the high one. Mind the step, it is loose.* It was a woman's voice, and it was the voice of a woman who had said that to somebody who was seven, and Tarin Rusk put his hand flat on the wet wall of a chamber under a house and could not tell whether that was his mother's voice, because there were three others doing the same kind of thing in the same moment in the same flat unhurried way, and he had listened for an hour, and there was no way on earth to tell them apart.

He had wanted to tell for twenty-six years. He had wanted it the way a man wants a thing he has built the rest of his life on, and there were four of them, and he could not pick one, and a man who cannot pick one is not entitled to build on it.

He got up. He got the lamp and the string and the blue book, and he sat down on the kerb, and he wrote it down.

> *7th day, evening. Chamber at the end of the adit off the settling tank, level four of the works at Ashgate. Tooled rock, dressed ledge on the north-east side, 6 ft, kerb set. Water clear to the bottom, standing, no drift, no weed, no wetting of the ledge. Level by string and rule, checked twice: 9 ft 4 in above the floor of the adit at its mouth. That floor is the floor inside the filter house door. Outside ground at that door is 1 ft 9 in above it. Water therefore stands 11 ft 1 in above the outside ground at the door of this works. It has not moved and there is a line cut in the rock at the level, one inch deep, one foot long, cut by a man who expected to want it again.*
> *The roof of this chamber is not rock. It is the underside of a floor. Flagstones on putty, a hearth and flue, a chimney breast, a cut lead stub of a floor drain, and a wooden stair of eleven steps left hanging with nothing at the top. I have been under a dwelling for four hours. Nobody has come down. I have called out twice and there is a building above my head with a fire in it and nobody has come down.*
> *The roof is not one building. Where the light reaches, the flagging changes direction, and there are joist ends, and the level of the floor is not the same all the way along, and this is a street, not a house.*

He stopped, and turned the page, and started again, because there was a second thing and he was not going to let it go on the same page as the first.

> *Voices: not one. Nine, then eleven, then not countable. Work, and a boundary, and a price, and a stock being counted, and a child asking one question without a pause. No addressee. No response to a name, to speech, or to the stone, before or after. Nothing in the pressure altered at any point of an hour's trial and I have not got a single fact out of it that I could put in a report and I have tried.*
> *There is a woman in it giving directions about a pump. There are three others doing the same thing at the same time. I could not tell which of the four it was, and I have wanted to tell for twenty-six years, and a man who cannot tell is not entitled to build on it, and I have written four and not one.*

He read it back twice. Then he tried to cut a name on the second face of the stone, because the second face was blank, and because it was the whole of what he had, and because it is what a man does.

He got the chisel out and set it and put the mallet in his left hand and knelt in water over his knee at the foot of the ledge with a lamp on the flags of a house, and the two fingers came down onto the fore-edge of the stone out of forty years of habit, and were not there.

The stone took a white bruise a quarter of an inch from where he had set it. That was all it did.

> *No name cut. Hand will not seat. Stone left as found. Second face blank and I have not put anything on it.*

And at the head of the ledge, where the wall came up to the roof, there was a stair.

Niko Venn went up it twenty steps and stood with his head above the level of the flagstones and looked back down at the water, and then came back down and did the thing that he had been doing for four part-days, and put his finger on the stones one at a time from the bottom.

The water in the chamber did not move when Niko counted. Nothing in the pressure behind Tarin's ear moved. It was eleven in the evening on the seventh day of the month, and there were two hundred and forty-one steps in a stone stair going up through the roof of a chamber to a door eleven feet over a woman, and Niko Venn came back down and said:

"Right," he said. "Then somebody has to climb it with one hand, and I am going to be the one who does, and I would like it written down that I said that first."
