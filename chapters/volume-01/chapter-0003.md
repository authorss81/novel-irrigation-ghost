# Chapter 0003 — A District of Blue Doors

He got down off the track at the arch and the water was over his bootlaces.

It came out of the low stone span in a slab a foot wide and went down the middle of the yard in a sheet that broke white every time it hit the flags, and the yard was four inches deep across nine paces, and there were nine people standing in it up to the calf. Not panicking. That was the first thing that stopped him. Two of them had hold of a door frame and were braced against the pull of something inside the wall, and a third was on their knees at the sill with both arms to the elbow, and a woman on the steps of the row above was beating a mat, and she had not stopped beating the mat since he came round the corner, and her rhythm had not once faltered, and Tarin understood that she was beating the mat because the mat was the thing in front of her that was still doing what it was supposed to do.

Nobody ran. Nobody screamed. Somebody's mother said, to nobody, *there it is, I told you, there it is*, in the voice of a woman enjoying herself.

"You will take that door frame down before evening," a man said to the two holding it, "and we will not have it back by morning, and it does not matter, because the door is not going to be in that wall at all by morning if this does not stop."

They saw him. A man of thirty-three in a wet coat, with a bar in his hand and grey grit in the creases of it, walking into a yard in four inches of moving water.

"What is it," said the man on the sill, not a question so much as a place to put his attention. "Is it the arch?"

"It's the arch," Tarin said. "It's Ashgate. It's coming down off the intake on the old overflow run. It has a head of five point eight nine metres on it and it cannot be shut from up there. How long has it been running?"

"Hours. Before noon. We heard it in the wall."

"How much water is this?"

"Some," the man said. "Enough that it is in the rooms. Enough that Ondra's boy has got his bed up on the frame, and the old woman at the end is moving her things to the dry side, and my mother is not moving anything at all, she is sitting on the step and she is going to sit on the step until it stops."

Tarin went to the side of the yard, where the run came out of the arch on its stone lip and turned along the wall. He knelt. The flags under his knee were slick with something that was not mud and not soot; it was fine and pale and it came off on his trouser leg and dried to a grey bloom inside a minute, in the sun that was coming in low off the ridge.

Along the wall the run climbed its lip, and the lip was two feet above the yard, and at the end of it the yard's outfall was a stone throat with a channel beyond it running down and away under the lane, and the mouth of the throat was covered by a plate of iron three-eighths thick, bolted at four corners with heads the size of a fist, and a white seam of dried salt ran round the join of the plate to the stone above it, thick as a thumb in places.

He put his hand flat on the plate. It was warm.

"That's your drain," he said.

"That is nobody's drain," said the man on the sill, and got up out of the water, and wiped his hands on his coat. "That has been ironed over since before my mother was a grown woman. We have a cistern and a pump and we have a well, and we have thrown the wash out of the back for thirty years, and the water goes where it goes."

"Where does it go?"

"Into the lane, and along the lane, and down to the low pump, and the low pump is the whole of what we have." He stopped. He looked back at the plate. "Where does it go now?"

"Nowhere." Tarin pointed with the bar. "It has been ironed over. It is not a grate, it is a lid. Everything that comes down that run has exactly one place to be, and the place is your cistern, and the cistern is a tank with a silted inlet, and that is the only reason you have four inches of water instead of four feet."

He said it fast, in the voice he used to brief a crew at a fault, because it was the shape of the truth and because he was angry about it in a way he had not been angry since he was nineteen years old. He was not frightened of the water in the yard. He was frightened of a piece of iron that a city had bolted over a drain in the year he was seven and then written the district off its sheet, so that a hundred and fifty feet of head would have nowhere to go but into one family's tank, and nobody would ever have to be told that this was what had happened to them.

He got up and looked at the cistern.

It was a masonry tank in the corner of the yard, ten feet across, and the water in it stood a third of the way up, and the surface was not still. It moved in the slow way that still water moves when something is pushing at it from a long way off, and the inlet was a lead pipe the width of a wrist coming out of the wall at a height of eighteen inches, and the water came out of the pipe in a thread and dropped into the tank and made a ring that spread and met itself.

"That is the balance point," Tarin said. "The tank has filled until the head it holds is most of the head coming down. It will not fill any higher while that pipe is half silted. If somebody clears that pipe, this yard will be under two feet before dark, and then the whole row is under two feet, and I cannot do anything about that from up on the intake hill."

"Nobody is going to clear that pipe," said the man. "Nobody has cleared it in my life."

"Then you have a yard under four inches and a cistern that stops, or you have a cistern and a flood. I am telling you so you can decide which one you would rather be standing in."

The dog came round the corner of the row and went into the water without any expression at all, and came out the other side, and shook itself against a door.

The doors were blue.

Not one of them. Not a painted blue that had been repainted, but a row of them, the old vegetable blue that goes chalky and pale along the grain, nine doors down the side of the row, and every one of them the same, and the fourth one from the near end had a brass plate on it gone green and a number under the green that Tarin could not read from here and did not need to.

He had a bar in his hand and a yard of water at his bootlaces and a man's whole neighbourhood at his back, and he stood there and made himself look at the fourth door until the yard went blurry, and what came up in him was not a memory so much as a thing he had been carrying in his body for twenty-six years without knowing what it was: his mother's hand flat on the back of his neck in a storm, and her voice giving directions — *left at the pump, not the high one, the low one, keep off the stones, they're wet* — and the smell of wet lime, and being six years old and putting his feet where she put them.

He was a stranger in the row. That was the shape of it. Twenty-six years is nothing, and it is the whole world; and nobody in this yard knew his face, and every one of them knew the row, and the woman beating the mat had beaten it long before he was born and would beat it long after.

The man on the sill came up the two steps to where Tarin was standing and looked at him with the flat appraisal of a man deciding whether to be worried later.

"You're the Water Office," he said. "You've the look."

"I am the Water Office," Tarin said. "My name is Tarin Rusk. I was born in the row, at the end past the pump. My father was Bevan Rusk, and he kept the sluice on this terrace, and you will not remember him because you would have been a child, but there is a woman here who will."

Hanne Roeg was on the fourth step of the row with a cane across her knees and the mat over her shoulder, and she had stopped beating it about ninety seconds before he said the name, and she had not started again.

"You said Rusk," she said.

"Rusk."

"Bevan Rusk's boy." She said it the way you check a thing you already know. "There were three of you. The eldest went off to Vellum and learned letters. The middle one died of a wet chest when he was nine. And this one." She came up off the step, slowly, the way old people do it when they have decided to look properly at something, and she got hold of his coat sleeve and turned it, and looked at the bar, and at the grey grit ground into the metal. "He used to hold a chisel the wrong way up and his father used to hit him for it. Bevan Rusk could seat a float with two fingers on the flat of his hand and never touched it once."

"He could," Tarin said. "He taught me."

"Where have you been, then." She let go of his sleeve. It was not a gentle question, and it was not about him. "Where has your mother been, and why has nobody come and looked for her, and don't tell me the Office looked, because I have been on that step thirty years and I would have heard a boot."

He stood in four inches of his own water in a yard on the terrace, and there was no version of the answer that was not a small, ordinary, human cowardice.

"She went into the breach," he said. "The official account is a landslide on the seventh slope. She was not on the seventh slope; she was in this row at four in the morning, and the man who signed that account did not put it in writing because there was no paper for a place that was not on a sheet."

Hanne Roeg looked at him for a long moment, and then she looked at the water coming down the yard.

"Then you've come down that run for nothing," she said. "You should have said that first and saved us the worry. Nobody's fault. The mountain let go."

"It has not let go," Tarin said. "It is a gate I opened with a bar this morning, and I opened it because the intake still has water in it behind a gate the city says has been shut for six years, and I did not know that when I started, and I am telling you that now instead of at the end of the week."

"You have a gate and a bar," she said, "and you have no office."

"I have no office."

"Then you are a man with a bar in a yard." She turned to the row. "Wick! Take the door frame off. It is going. Take it off and bring it here and stand it across the run, and it will not hold the water but it will take the grit out of it before it gets in the rooms."

A young man came out of a doorway and started wading over, and then stopped and turned round, because the woman at the far end of the row had got up off her step.

It was not the water. The old woman had got up because the yard had filled another inch while Tarin Rusk was standing in it explaining himself, and the water was over the sill of the lowest room on that side, and it was coming in under the door and going out across the flags in a fan, and there was a smell coming off it that was not a smell of stone.

Tarin went down into it knee-deep with his bar and got the corner of the cistern up on his shoulder and looked at the plate, and the plate was still warm, and the four bolts were rust-bloomed, and the salt seam had grown a quarter inch since morning.

Somewhere above him, a hundred and fifty feet up the hill, a gate he had opened with a bar was still running, and there was nothing between that water and a row of blue doors except nine inches of silted lead pipe and a piece of iron that no one in the city had a key to.

A boy came round the corner of the row at a run, seventeen or eighteen, wet to the shoulder, with a roll of papers under his arm.

"Engineer Rusk!" He was panting. "From the upper office. There's a notice for you and they've already put your name on it. It's from the Memory Office in Vellum, they exhibited a volume this morning and the Office has got to answer it by the sixth, and the commissioner said—"

"Who said?"

"The commissioner said you were the one who was at Ashgate." The boy thrust the roll at him. "It's the maintenance volume. It says the gate was shut before the Ninefold Flood and it says there was no head on it at all, and they want the Water Office to say how a shut gate stayed dry for a hundred and eighty-one years, and the commissioner said you'd know, and he's put your name against it and told me to find you before dark."

Tarin took the roll. The paper was good stock. He had held a sheet of the same stock in a warden's hut that morning and put it in his coat pocket without writing in the box.

"Somebody," he said, "is going to have to explain that number."
