# Chapter 0012 — A Named Representative

The form was called an application for entry of a district row in the distribution schedule, and it was one sheet of good paper with a printed border, and it cost four shillings in advance and was not refundable, and it had six numbered boxes.

Pell laid it out on the plank counter with a brass weight on each corner so the stove draught could not have it.

"Read it out," he said. "Not me. You."

"You have read it."

"I have read it. It is the warden's office's form, which means I have to be able to fill it in blind, and a form I can fill in blind is a form I can give to a man who cannot read." He turned it round. "Read it out."

Tarin Rusk read it out. He had a sheet of paper and a wet coat and no office, and he stood in a hut on a terrace road and read a district's future to them off a counter, and it went like this.

*1. Name of the district as claimed.*

*2. The name of the person signing as representative, and the household in that person's keeping.*

*3. The number of houses and the number of persons resident.*

*4. A description of the water works maintained by the district.*

*5. The source of the district's supply.*

*6. The date from which the district has maintained its own supply continuously.*

"Box five," said Bel Crale.

"Box five is the whole of it," said Pell.

"Is it." Bel Crale came up onto the step to look at the form upside down, which she did with the fluency of a woman who had spent the spring signing dispensary returns. "Box four you could put *one hand pump and three tanks*. Box five you could put *a well*. There is a well. Bel Crale's dispensary, a bucket, opened in the spring with forty-one pounds." She straightened up. "There is a well in box four and a well in box five and this form takes a morning to fill in, and you have all been standing on my step since before the light like men waiting for a doctor."

"There is a well," said Pell, "in a building that was opened in the spring by a private subscription, and it is not a district well, and if the application says a district well then somebody will come and inspect the district well, and there is no district well, and that is a different kind of letter."

"Then what is in box five."

Pell put a finger on it, and then took the finger off, because a warden is careful with a form.

"Box five is the document the district's legal existence is made of," he said. "Everything else on that sheet is a description. Box five is a source. If you put a private bucket in box five you have applied for a row on the strength of a bucket, and the answer will come back in four months and say so, and it will be the correct answer and it will be final, and nobody will ever look at this lane again."

Nobody in the hut said anything for a moment, and outside a barrow went over the flags and stopped.

"Then it has to be true," said Hanne Roeg, from the doorway, "and it is going to be the gate."

---

The count took a day and a half and it was the first civic work the lane had done in twenty-six years, and it went badly, in the way that honest work goes.

Tarin was not permitted to do it. That was the first rule and it was Pell's rule and Pell was immovable about it: a man under a stand-down does not take a census of a district on behalf of the warden's office, because a census is a document and the day a document from this lane has his name at the top of it is the day the two charges become one charge with a district on it.

So the lane counted itself. Marda Ollan, who kept the keys to the two rooms at the top end, took a slate and a stub of pencil and did it door by door, because a slate does not go into a file, and she came back at four o'clock on the twenty-second with a number she did not like.

"Twenty-four houses," she said. "Twenty-two of them on the low pump's lane and two at the top end above the arch, which is how the count came out and which I have checked twice. One hundred and twenty-six people. And I have left off two, and I am telling you I have left off two because I did not want to argue about it in front of a slate."

"Who are the two?"

"The old woman at the end of the row above the pump has a ration card in the Fenner district, because her daughter married into Fenner nine years ago, and she lives here, and she does not want to be counted here. That is her business and I am not going to take her card off her by writing her down." Marda put the slate down. "And there is a man at the top end called Perre who has been here nine years and works at the filter works and keeps himself to himself, and he would not open the door, and I stood in his yard for ten minutes and he would not open the door, and that is also his business."

"Twenty-four houses and one hundred and twenty-six persons is what goes in," said Hanne Roeg. "And I want the two of them written in the margin, not counted, not named if they do not want to be named, but written in the margin as *two persons resident in the district declining to be enumerated*. Then it is in the paper and nobody can say we cooked the number."

"Who does that protect?"

"It protects the form," she said. "The form is the only thing any of us own right now."

---

The argument about who signed went on for the whole of the second evening in the yard, and it was the best argument of the month, and Tarin Rusk listened to it and tried twice to stop it and was refused twice, in front of the yard, by a woman on four steps.

His first objection was the one any engineer would make and it was the correct one.

"You are about to put a name on a form," he said, "and the warden's office is under the acting commissioner's office, and the acting commissioner's office has a registry, and a registry is a list of names with addresses on it, and a name on that form is a name in that registry by the end of the week. Right now the only reason the commissioner's office cannot touch this lane is that there is nothing on paper to touch. You are handing them a piece of paper with a household's name at the top of it."

"Yes," said Hanne Roeg.

"I want to be sure that you have heard that."

"I have heard it. I am seventy-one years old and I have heard everything anybody has said to me this month." She came down one step, which cost her something, and looked at him. "Tarin. You want to know what the worst of it is, so here it is, and I will say it once. If they come to this lane, they will come to the house with the name on the form. Not to the yard, not to the lane, to the house. And they will not send anybody you would send. And I have spent my life on this step being un-findable, and I am un-findable in a way you could not buy in Vellum, and I am spending it."

"Then why—"

"Because the boy in the row is nine," she said, "and he is going to be nine for another four years and then he is going to be thirteen, and at thirteen somebody is going to ask him where he is from, and he is going to have to say a word that is not on any sheet in this city. Do you know what he says now? He says the blue steps, and the blue steps is a thing you can see. He has never once had to say *I do not exist*, and that is because of the twenty-six years, and it has run out."

Nobody in the yard argued with that, because it was not arguable, and because it was the first time in a month that anybody had put the cost of the silence on the table instead of the cost of the water.

His second objection was worse, and it was the one he actually believed.

"Then the form will come back," he said. "You are giving a name to a piece of paper in exchange for a piece of paper, and I have watched a piece of paper in this city go into a file and come out of one. It will go up and it will come down and it will be handled by people who are not going to be unusually hostile, and in eleven months the answer will be *no schedule entry is possible in the absence of a documented source*, and by then this lane has a name in a registry, and a name in a registry is a handle."

"Then in eleven months," said Hanne Roeg, "we will be a thing that can be refused, instead of a thing that cannot be."

Tarin had no answer to that and it turned out he did not want one.

"Do not make me into a brave old woman," she said then, and there was real temper in it, the temper of somebody who has been turned into a symbol by well-meaning men in a bad month and is tired of it. "Do not make me into that. There are four of us who will sign this and I am the oldest and I am the one who can write a legible hand and that is the entire reason. Mrs Ollan is thirty-one and has two children. Wick is thirty-eight and cannot write. My son is in Vellum and will not put his name on a thing for a lane he left when he was seventeen. That is the whole of the choosing. Do not make it more than it is."

---

They signed it on the second evening, on the dispensary step, in the last of the light, because Bel Crale brought out the one good table and there was nowhere else in the lane with a flat top.

There were twenty-two who had been asked and nineteen who said no, and Hanne Roeg had the two numbers written on a card in her own hand and read them out before she signed, twice, to make sure the clerk of the minutes heard both.

"Twenty-two of us will put a name to this," she said. "Nineteen of us were asked and said no, and I am going to write that on the card as well, and I am not going to soften it or leave it off, and both of those are true on the same evening in the same yard. And I am not going to tell you that the nineteen are frightened, because some of them are and some of them are not, and it does not matter. They are on this lane and so are we and that is the whole of what nineteen people have.

"And I am going to write one more line on the card, and the warden is going to hate it. The eighty-seven of us who have not been asked are not being counted as agreeing to anything. We were not asked. Asking eighty-seven people is a day's work and a day of arguing, and I am not going to put eighty-seven names on a card because it would look better on it. If a man in an office reads this form in October and finds twenty-two names, he will know exactly what twenty-two names are, and if he has to be told the rest of us are on this lane then he was never going to count us anyway."

Then she filled in the boxes, and Tarin read them over her shoulder and did not say anything, because it was not his form.

*1. The blue steps, lower terrace. Also called Glassstep. Also called the Fold. No other name is in use.*

~~*2. Roeg, Hanne, of the fourth step, the blue steps, holding the household at the fourth step.*~~

*3. Twenty-four houses, one hundred and twenty-six persons. Two further persons resident and declining enumeration, entered at the margin. Twenty-two signatories, of whom one is the named representative; nineteen persons asked and declining; the remainder not asked.*

*4. One hand pump at the low pump, kept by Roke, Tam. Three covered tanks. One courtyard cistern, ten feet across, twelve to the coping. One well at the dispensary, private subscription, opened the twelfth day of the third month. No district outfall. The terrace overflow beneath the row has been sealed, plated, no key issued and out of service since the year the row was removed from the distribution schedule, closure entry one hundred and eighteen.*

*5. A branch off the Ashgate intake overflow, taken at a head of 5·89 metres above the gate seat, through a channel cut and cleared by Rusk, Tarin, senior flow engineer, and named on a stone at the pump plinth, the name being Glassstep low pump branch. The gate at the head of that branch is recorded in the city ledger as shut, and the water is not shut, and the reading of that head is entered in the maintenance volume of the works at Vellum.*

*6. The pump has been kept by the household of Roke, and after him by his mother, and before him by his aunt, without a break, for thirty-one years.*

Then Hanne Roeg put the pen down on the second box, and made Marda Ollan scratch it out, and wrote it again differently, and read the first version out loud to the yard before she scratched it, because she wanted the yard to hear that it had existed.

*2. Roeg, Hanne, of the fourth step, the blue steps, holding the household at the fourth step, signing as the representative of the district at the district's request and answerable for the truth of it, and not having been put forward by any office in this city.*

Tarin Rusk read box five twice and had a bad feeling arrive in him from about the level of his chest, which was the same place the other thing had come from in Vellum and he put that thought down on the spot and did not look at it.

"She is going to hang me with that," he said, when she had put the pen down.

"Yes," said Hanne Roeg.

"You wrote the name of the gate and the name of the engineer and the reading off a Vellum volume into a district application on the terrace road, and the warden's office is a room the commissioner can walk into, and that is an admission in a public document."

"It is a true statement in a public document," she said, "which is a different thing, and which is the only kind of paper I have ever wanted in my life." She put the card with the two numbers in with the form and squared the edges. "You told the yard on the twentieth that you did not know where it came from. Put that in box five as well, if you like. *Source not known to the district. Head known to the district. The engineer says he does not know.*"

"That would hang me harder."

"Yes," she said. "It is not my job to keep you out of it. It is my job to say it once and be exact. I have watched four men this month explain this lane and I have watched all four of them edit themselves. Do not edit yourself on my paper."

He did not add it. He thought about it for the rest of the year.

---

Bel Crale paid the four shillings and would not be argued out of it, and made Hanne Roeg take the money off her before she signed, on the grounds that a woman signing a thing had to be holding something when she signed it, and Hanne Roeg said that was the most sentimental thing anybody had said in the dispensary since it opened, and took it.

Pell stamped it on the twenty-second in the afternoon with the warden's date stamp, in the middle, and read the whole thing over once, and then made them wait while he entered it in the district book, and the entering took him under two minutes.

"This is the last time it will be a surprise to anybody," he said, when he had shut the book. "It goes up to the commissioner's office with the returns on the twenty-third. It will not be read by a person. It will be read by a clerk who has a tray and a rule, and the rule is: a district row is entered on a schedule, or it is not, and the schedule is the standing distribution schedule and it is amended by the Assembly or by a return signed by the keeper."

"And which is it going to be?"

"I do not know and I am not going to guess at you, because a man who guesses gets guessed back to." He wiped the stamp. "What I will tell you is what it will cost, because you have been honest with me and I owe you the shape of it. In four days it will have a number on it. A file number. And a file number is a handle, exactly like your man said, and the worse of it is that from then on the answer to it is the commissioner's office and not mine, and the commissioner's office has got a hearing list with your name on it twice, and if anybody ever decides that your two charges and this application are about the same matter, then a warden who is a fair man is not going to be able to stop it being about the same matter."

"How long?"

"Twelve days is the ordinary run. It is not twelve days of anybody reading it." He set the stamp in its tin. "I will tell you exactly what I have seen happen nine times in nineteen years, and then I am going to shut up about it, because it is a bad thing to say out loud. The application goes up. It gets a number. It gets a tray. Somebody's tray, in an office with eleven people in it, and it sits on the tray, and one day somebody puts the tray down and it becomes the tray at the bottom, and the tray at the bottom is not a decision, it is only a thing that has not been looked at yet. And the date on it does not run. That is what I have seen nine times."

The lane stood in the road outside the station in the last of the light with a stamped form in a satchel going up the hill and one hundred and twenty-six people in a count on a slate, and nothing at all visible to show for any of it.

"That is the part I can fix," Hanne Roeg said.

"You cannot fix a tray."

"I am not fixing a tray. I am fixing the date." She was looking up the road, at the satchel, and not at Pell, which was correct. "When it comes back and it is a refusal, and it will be, then it is a refusal on a dated paper, and it goes in a file, and a man in that office has to sign his name at the bottom of it. And when that man signs his name at the bottom of it, he will have to answer for a district that exists, on a day, in a year. And the day that somebody in this city is asked *are there people at the blue steps*, there will be a piece of paper in a file with a date on it, and his handwriting, saying yes."

Nobody had anything to say to that, and the satchel went up the road, and Tarin Rusk stood in the road outside a warden's station and worked out that he had spent the day watching a district choose to be countable and that in the whole of it he had contributed exactly one thing, which was the correct advice to stay quiet, given for the wrong reason by a man who wanted it to be the other way.

The blue steps behind him were lit up along the whole of their length, because the row had put lamps on the flags at the fourth step and the low house and the top end, which is a thing you do when a district has done something, and which nobody had done in the twenty-six years any of them had been alive for.

Marda Ollan came past him on her way down to her own door and stopped.

"Twenty-two of us signed," she said, "and I signed, and I have two children, and if they come to this lane they will come to my door first, because I put a hand on a form in front of a warden. I have been sitting in my own kitchen since four o'clock being sick about it. My mother is dead and there is nobody in that house but me and the two of them and I have made myself the easiest one to find in this entire row."

She looked at him.

"So if you have any advice for me about the next four months," she said, "now would be the time for it."
