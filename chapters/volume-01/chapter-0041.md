# Chapter 0041 — The Name On Somebody Else's Paper

They went up the mill road at ten past five in the morning of Monday the twenty-eighth day of this month, and it was the third time in six weeks that Tarin Rusk had walked two and a half miles uphill with a paper in his coat, and it was the first time the paper was not his.

Marda Ollan had copied the request out on the back of Hanne Roeg's letter on the Sunday afternoon and she had made three copies, because Marda Ollan made three copies of everything after the thirtieth of the month before. He had one. Tenna Sagg had the other, because she was walking beside him and he had given it to her at the bottom of the track without asking, on the grounds that a document which named her ought to be in her own hand for two and a half miles before it got to a row.

She read it on the way up. She is fourteen and she reads better than she writes, which is a thing her mother has said about her often enough, and she got to the paragraph about the well at Ashgate and the slate and the girl of about fourteen and she stopped walking for about four steps and then started again.

"That is me," she said.

"Yes."

"You had it in your pocket on Friday."

"I had it in my pocket on Friday," said Tarin Rusk, "and I did not come up this hill on Saturday, and that is the whole of what I have to say about it and I am not going to make it longer in a road."

---

They came round the corner above the mill road at about twenty to six and the row was already awake, because in a row of thirty-four people where the water comes up out of a kitchen floor at sixty-eight gallons an hour, somebody is always up before the light. On the morning of the twenty-eighth there were nine people standing about in the yard at Nell Drust's, because everybody in that row had watched a man and a girl come up the track with a lamp four times in six weeks and had stopped pretending it was nothing.

Nell Drust was on her own step with the shutter that had her six gallons an hour and the watch on it chalked on the back, which she had put there herself on the twentieth, and she looked at the two of them and at the paper in the girl's hand and said:

"You have come about the paper."

"I have come to tell you what is in it before it is in an office," said Tarin Rusk. "It went up the cattle track yesterday morning in a satchel. It is in the city this morning. I have got a copy of it and she has got a copy of it, and I would like to read all of it out in your yard in daylight, in front of whoever wants to hear it, and then I would like to be told what you think about it, and I am not going anywhere until you have."

"Read it," said Nell Drust.

They put a bench out in the row because thirty-four people cannot hear a man on a doorstep, and by the time the sun was up over the ridge there were twenty-six of them sitting or standing and the rest inside with the doors open. Tarin Rusk read out the whole of it in the order it was on the paper, including the parts about a boy of ten at a school in the fenner district, and skipped nothing, as he had skipped nothing three days ago in a yard of a hundred and forty people.

Then he put his hand flat on the paper and said the other thing.

"Three days ago I read this in a yard four miles down this hill and every person in that yard said afterwards that we had been careful, and I have thought about that word all the way up here and I have decided it is the wrong word and I would rather say so myself than have it said for me.

"We were fast. That is what we were. There was a tank at its coping with nothing clean in it and a man who had shut his own pump and a girl of seven who has been at the fourth point since the twentieth, and a form coming down a cattle track on the twelfth of next month, and we had a day and a half. And in a day and a half I could not write down what we wanted examined without saying what the thing we wanted examined was *under*, and the thing it is under is this row, and there is no way to write a sentence about a chamber without writing down the houses it is under.

"So we wrote you down. Without asking you. In a yard, in daylight, with the doors open, and four of the people who signed it are your neighbours' kind of people and one of them is a woman of seventy-one who has never signed a thing without costing somebody something, and none of that makes it a thing you agreed to."

---

Nobody shouted.

He had said on the seventeenth of this month that a row of thirty-four people who have drawn their water out of a hole in a kitchen floor for thirty years is not a row that has ever had a reason to be frightened of what is under the houses, and it turned out to be a row that has had plenty of reasons to be angry about a great many other things, and about half of those reasons were in that yard.

Nell Drust let about twenty people get a sentence out before she stood up off the step, and when she did she did not raise her voice, and every word of it was accurate, and by the end of it Tarin Rusk had to put his hand on the back of a chair.

"Right," she said. "Here it is, and I am going to say it once, and then I am going to sit down, and anybody else who has something can say it and I will not interrupt.

"On the seventeenth of this month a girl of about fourteen wrote on a slate at this bench that this row will not send a drop of water down that hill while that plant can be started by anybody with a horse. And I made her put my name on it and not hers, and I said why, and it was because I am thirty-nine and I had never once been on anything, and because a thing that is written down has to be answered and a thing that is not written down does not have to be answered at all.

"And that is the last time in my life anybody in this city asked me for anything, and I said no, and it was mine, and I want it understood in this yard that it was *mine*. I wrote it in my own hand and I read it out at this bench and I did it in front of twenty-eight people and I did not ask anybody's leave.

"And now I am not a woman who said no on a slate. I am a matter to be examined. I am a paragraph. Four miles down a hill there is a district and I am in their paper, and I did not put myself there, and I am not able to take myself out of it, and the only thing I have got to hold on to is that I am in it in a piece of paper instead of a piece of paper about me.

"And here is the part that is worse and I have not heard anybody say it yet so I am going to say it.

"You have written down four things you are not asking for. I have read them. I read them on Friday night with a lamp and I have read them twice. You are not asking for a schedule. You are not asking for a route. You are not asking for water. You are not asking for a keeper. And in four months there will be a file in four buildings and the woman who reads it is not going to read those four lines, because those four lines are the only part of that paper that is about anybody.

"I have been answered by a piece of paper once in my life and I did not like it. And I am not going to be a piece of paper twice, and I am telling you now, in front of these people, that the four lines are the reason I am not standing in this yard with a bar in my hand, and I am going to be one of the people who says those four lines out loud in a room, and if there is a box on that paper for the things a person is not asked for, then I am the one who is going to fill it, and my name goes in it and not a girl's."

The row argued for forty minutes and did not shout. About eleven of the twenty-six people said something, and Tarin Rusk wrote down every word of all eleven of them in the blue book with the hand that still worked, and did not once say that he was writing it down.

---

The girl spoke last, and she was the worst affected person in that yard and the only one who did not raise her voice at all.

"Is my name on it," said Tenna Sagg.

"Only as a description," said Tarin Rusk. "*A girl of about fourteen at the top of that hill.* That is the whole of it. Your name is not on it. I read the whole of it out just now and I would have said so if it were."

"That is what I mean," she said. "It is not my name, and it is going to be me. When somebody in a room four miles down a hill says *the girl of about fourteen*, there is going to be one, and everybody in this row is going to know it, and there is not one of them going to say it out loud, and it is going to be me for the next thirty years and there is not a single line in that paper I can object to.

"I wrote the slate because Nell told me to and I would write it again. And I am not saying that to be brave, I am saying it because it is true and because it is the only part of this I am able to be calm about. But nobody has asked me whether I want to be in a piece of paper. I am fourteen. I have been in this row since I was six. And a man I have met four times read a sentence out to twenty-six people on Friday and it had me in it and I did not know about any of it until this morning, two miles up a track, when he handed me a copy of it and told me to read it on the way up."

"Nobody asked you," said Tarin Rusk. "And I wrote the sentence, and I did not come up here on Saturday, and I have not got anything better than that to put in front of you this morning, and if you want me to say it in a form I will say it in a form."

---

The answer went on a slate, and it took forty minutes and two versions, and the girl with the good hand wrote both and struck the first herself. Nell Drust dictated it and did not soften one word of it, and Tenna Sagg wrote it, and then she made Tarin Rusk read it back, because that is a rule in that row and he was not going to be let off it.

> *Ashgate, the twenty-eighth day of this month, at a bench, in daylight, twenty-six of us present.*
> *1. We have read the request of the district of the lower terrace road. It is headed not written, not signed, and not asked for by the engineer named in the charges standing against him. We have noticed that.*
> *2. It asks that the works at Ashgate and the ground beneath the district be treated as one matter and heard together in one room by one examiner. We are not asked about that. We have been written into it. We are in it, and we did not ask to be, and we do not consent to it and we do not refuse it, and both of those are in this row's own hand and not yours.*
> *3. The girl of about fourteen is taken out. Not out of the facts. Out of the paper. We are not a document and we are not a girl of about fourteen. If a paper is going to hold thirty-four people it is going to hold our names or it is going to hold nothing.*
> *4. A person from this row will read that paper in that room, aloud, and it will be this slate, and it will be read before anybody fills in anything, and a woman of thirty-nine will read it, and she is not a district and she has not consented to be one.*
> *5. Nobody in this row will be asked for a drop of water again, by anybody, for as long as the plant can be started by anybody with a horse. That is not new. It is the seventeenth of this month again and it is in a different hand and it is a different piece of paper and it is the same answer.*
> *6. And we will come to that room. Because a thing that is written down has to be answered, and it has been written down, and we are not going to be the only people in this city who have never been asked anything and can prove it.*
> *N. Drust, of this row, keeper of it for sixteen years. The twenty-eighth day of this month. I have put my own name here and not anybody else's.*

---

Afterwards Tarin Rusk got himself down to the bottom end of the row and looked at the lid, because Nell Drust had said on the sixteenth day of this month that she had asked for a man with a bar to come and look at it before anybody in that row was thirsty, and nobody had come, and she had not asked again.

It is an iron lid about two feet by three in a frame of the same iron set flush in the flags of what used to be a yard, and the flags under it have worn a shallow dish an inch and a half deep, which is thirty years of feet and carts and a barrow wheel and a woman walking to it every morning. There is a ring of white salt standing on the inside face of the frame, about four inches above the top of the iron.

He put his right hand flat on the lid, knelt on the dish in the flags, put his ear against it, and heard nothing at all, because there is no instrument in this basin that would test a standing body of water and he is one of the men who says that out loud.

"That is a salt ring four inches above the frame," he said. "Which means whatever is under this has been up to four inches over the top of it, and has gone down again, and has done it more than once, because there is a ring and not a tide mark."

"I am not going to tell you what is in there. I have got a rule and I am going to say it out loud, which is that I do not open a thing in a row that drinks from under the row, and I am not doing it in a yard with thirty-four people standing round it in case the floor comes up while I am looking.

"And I am going to write down that ring, and the date, and that the frame bolt is the original bolt and has not been turned since it was set, because that is a fact and facts are the only thing this row has ever been short of."

"Then come back on the twelfth," said Nell Drust, "with the man with the form."

"I am not the man with the form," said Tarin Rusk. "I have been the man with the form four times in six weeks and I have not got one. Come back when it is a document and not a favour, and I will be in the room when it is opened, and you will open it and not me."

He came down off the hill at about eleven and was two and a half miles down the track before he stopped, because a man who has read a district's request out in a row at a bench in daylight and been told what is in it has to stop somewhere before he can carry it the rest of the way.

They had been told a day before the paper arrived. That was the only thing he had, and it was not nothing, and it was not a great deal, and he went the rest of the way down with a copy of a request in his coat that no longer had the last line on it, because Tenna Sagg had put it in the fire of her own kitchen at about eleven and had said, without being asked, that a copy of a document that had not happened yet was not worth the wood.
