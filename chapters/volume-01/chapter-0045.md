# Chapter 0045 — A List With Names On It

The Basin Assembly's standing orders of 114, which are printed in a pamphlet that nine hundred people in this city have read, say two things about hearings about water, and that this basin has not had one in this chamber since the standing orders were printed.

The first is that a hearing about a route is held at the route. Not in a room off the Assembly's yard, not by letter, not by a committee sitting over a river. At the route, on the ground, in daylight, with the parties standing on the thing they are talking about.

The second is that the parties to a hearing about a route shall be named in print, in advance, together with the name of every person who intends to speak to the route, and that any such person may decline, and that a declination made in writing and delivered to the clerk before the sitting begins shall be read into the record and shall not be held against the person who makes it in any office of this city or in any proceeding arising out of the hearing.

It is the second clause of the second of those two things that had not been used in the history of this Assembly, and the clerk who read it out on the morning of the seventh of this month, in an office with a wall on one side of it, said so himself.

"Two hundred and nine years," said Oriel Fenn. "I have kept the minutes of this Assembly for nineteen of them and I have never once seen that clause worked, and I have wanted to see it worked since I read it, and I would like it understood that I am not going to do anything unusual this afternoon. I am going to write down what I am given and read it back before the sitting, and that is the whole of my trade."

The direction of the Assembly of the sixth was three lines. It was posted in four buildings, on the warden's board, and on a hoarding at the arch on the Saturday morning, and it said that a hearing would be held at the arch in the blue steps on the fifteenth of this month at four of the clock, on the matter of the works at Ashgate and the ground beneath the district; that the midsummer ration review of the same date would be taken in the same room and the chair would take the evidence first and the review after; and that the parties and the speakers would be published in accordance with standing order 114.

The third line is the one that changed the shape of the week, and it was put in by the chair and the chair defended it for about nine minutes and then let it stand, and what he said was that a hearing about a route that is held in a room four miles away from the route is a hearing about a document, and this Assembly has had a great many of those, and it is not going to have another one in midsummer.

---

The list of speakers was made on the Thursday afternoon and it was made by Marda Ollan, because Marda Ollan keeps the book, and nobody asked her to, and the reason she was the one anybody asked was that on the Monday she had copied a district's request out in her own hand and written two charges under it with the dates on them, and that had been noticed.

"There is a rule," she said, to the yard, on the Thursday, holding the slate. "There is a rule about this and it is not a rule of this Assembly, it is a rule of Tarin Rusk's, and he has said it out loud three times in a month and once to a man with a satchel. He says *nobody's memory goes on a piece of paper without their answer*. And I have decided that a witness list is a piece of paper and that I am not going to put one name on it that has not said yes."

"So I have gone round. I have gone to seven people and I have asked six of them and I have read the whole of the order out to each of them and I have told them what they would be agreeing to and what they would be agreeing *not* to be able to say, and I have written down what each of them said, and here is the list, and I am going to read it and then it goes on the hoarding at the arch tonight and anybody who is on it and does not want to be on it has got twelve hours."

> *Speakers, hearing of the fifteenth day of this month at the arch in the blue steps.*
> *1. H. Roeg, of the fourth step, named representative of a district of one hundred and twenty-six persons which holds no row, no schedule entry and no keeper.*
> *2. Y. Sen, water physician, of the blue steps, who has not been asked for a clinical question and will say so.*
> *3. N. Drust, of Ashgate, keeper of a settlement of eleven houses and thirty-four persons which holds no row, no schedule entry and no keeper, and who has read the district's request of the twenty-seventh day of last month aloud at a bench in that row and who has refused that district a drop of water in her own hand on a slate.*
> *4. W. Venn, of the blue steps, day-labourer, engaged at four shillings a day for the works required about the branch, and which works he will describe himself.*
> *5. M. Ollan, of the blue steps, keeper of the keys and the ledger, who holds the count.*
> *6. S. Pell, sluice warden for the terrace road, who is an officer of this city and will minute what he sees and has not been asked to be on this list and has asked to be on it.*
> *7. C. Preece, of Ashgate, keeper of the plant.*

She read the seventh out in the same voice she had read the other six in.

---

She had written it on the Thursday afternoon and had it copied by the girl with the good hand and posted on the Thursday evening by a boy from the fenner end who wanted the two pence, and she had not shown it to anybody, and the reason she had not shown it to anybody was not a plan.

It is the plant," she said, on the Friday morning, in the yard, to about thirty people, before anybody had accused her of anything. "The plant is the reason the water is where it is. I read that return out — Pell read it out — and it says the gin is turning, and it says the well in Nell Drust's kitchen came up sixty-eight an hour on the twentieth where it came up a hundred and three on the eighth, and it says that in three lines with no opinion in it, and it says it in a return that four buildings hold.

And every one of us has spent this month asking for a route and a witness and a keeper, and we have got a list of six people who are going to stand in a room and say what they know, and not one of the six is the man on the gin. And if we put him off that list then on the fifteenth day of this month a room is going to ask a straight question, which is *where is the water coming from*, and we are going to have to say *from nowhere*, and we are going to have to say it in front of a clerk.

"So I wrote his name down. And I did not ask him, because I have been round this lane all week asking everybody else and I ran out of road at about four o'clock on the Friday and I told myself it was obvious, and it was not obvious, and that is the whole of what I did wrong and I am not going to be let off it by anybody including myself."

---

Tarin Rusk read the list on the Friday morning at about ten, off the hoarding at the arch, in the rain, with his bad shoulder against the post.

He read it twice. Then he stood in the road for a while in the rain, and then he walked up the mill road, two and a half miles, in the rain, with a bad shoulder, and arrived at the top of the works at about half past one in the afternoon.

Coll Preece was not on the plant. The plant was shut. He had been sitting on an upturned pail at the filter-bed door with a knife and a piece of soft wood, cutting a wedge, because a wedge is what you cut when you have decided to stop something at six in the morning and you have no idea yet what you are going to stop it with.

"Read me the seventh line," he said, without looking up.

Tarin Rusk read him the seventh line.

Coll Preece cut one more shaving off the wedge and then put the knife down on the step and stood up, and he did not shout, and he had a great deal to say and he took it in the order it came to him, which was not a good order, and which was the order of a man who has not been asked a question.

"She is right," he said. "I will say that first and I would like it written down that I said it first, because in about a week everybody in that lane is going to be saying she was out of order to write me down, and she was not, and I want to be on the right side of it before that happens.

"The plant is the reason the water is where it is. If the gin is not turning, there is no discharge, and there has been no water in that tank at four thousand seven hundred gallons for a month. She is right and I would have written it myself if I had thought of it, and I did not think of it, and I have been up this hill four times in six weeks and I have not once thought about what I would say if somebody put me in a room.

"And I am not going to stand there and be asked about it. Not by her and not by the man with the book.

"Here is what the seventh line is going to mean, and she has not thought about it, and I am not angry, I am telling her because she is the only person in that lane who reads out what she has written.

"That plant has to be stopped before the water can be counted, and it has to be stopped by somebody, and the only somebody on this hill is me. There is a boy on a staging who is fifteen and is owed every week since the second of this month and has not had a penny of it, and I sent him down the hill on the Friday evening at eight with a day and a night's warning and his wages for the week out of my own coat, and I walked a horse up the mill road on the Saturday evening to start her at twenty past three on the Sunday, and the child has not had a wage since and neither have I.

"And if I put my name to a piece of paper that says *this water comes from this works*, then in a fortnight there is a document in four buildings that says it, and the office that reads it will do arithmetic, and the arithmetic will come back to this hill and it will come back to one man, and the man will be me, and the boy on the staging will be nothing at the bottom of it. There is one more week of wages in me and it is not a week's wages.

"So: no. I will not be on that list. Write it down, because you are the one who writes things down and I cannot read."

---

He wrote it himself, in his own hand, on the back of a contractor's tally counterfoil, and he would not have a slate and he would not have a copy of the list, on the grounds that he could not read a slate either and did not intend to be shown round the lane with one.

> *C. Preece, of Ashgate. Eighth day of this month.*
> *I am not on that list and I did not agree to be on it. I was not asked and I am not complaining that I was not asked, because a list that has to be asked is a list that half of a lane will not be on.*
> *I keep the pump. I have kept it four years and I get paid by the can and I have not been paid since the second day of this month. If that pump is stopped for anything I do not get paid for it and I have a boy on a staging who does not get paid for it either, and I have already sent that boy home once with my own money in his pocket and I am not going to do it a second time for this city.*
> *And I am not refusing the water. I want that in whatever room it is read in. I have never once in four years had anything to do with where the water goes and I have carried it down a hundred and forty yards and set it in a yard a hundred and forty times and I never asked and I never once thought about it, and I am not going to start refusing it in a room when somebody I have never met writes my name on a slate and does not ask me.*
> *I will stop the pump at six in the morning on whatever day you say. I will do it whether I am on the list or not and I will not be paid for it and I would like that written down too.*

Tarin Rusk read it back to him out loud twice, and Coll Preece made him read the third line twice, and the third line is the one about the water.

Then he asked for one thing and got it, and it took about nine minutes and it was the only nine minutes of that whole week that anybody on either hill spent on a document for a reason that was not a fight.

"A sheet," he said. "In the district's book, in the warden's book, and up here on the works. I have never had a piece of paper in my life that anybody could find me with. I would like the three of them to have my no in them and I would like to be able to send up a girl of fourteen with a pail in each hand to fetch it, because I cannot read it and I will not be able to tell you in a month whether it is still in there."

So Sluice Warden Pell entered it in the warden's book on the terrace road on the Friday afternoon, in a fair hand, with the hour, against a condition of his own on the condition of the district, which is the only way anything has ever got into a room in that city; and the warden's book has four copies and three of them left the district; and Oriel Fenn, minute clerk of the Basin Assembly, received one on the Monday morning at about eleven and entered it as received, and read it back, and asked the question his standing orders required him to ask.

"Mr Preece. Do you understand that your declination will be read into the record of the hearing, and that it may be read by any person who obtains a copy of that record, and that no office of this city may act against you on account of it."

"He will not be able to read it," said Coll Preece, from the far end of a room, "so somebody will be standing up in a room reading it out to him, and I should like the man who reads it to say the words *he is not refusing the water*, because there will be a great many people in that room on Friday afternoon who will hear *no* and only hear *no*."

"He will say it," said Oriel Fenn, "because you will have said it on the record first, in your own hand, and a record is read in the order it was made."

---

The rest of the week was the district doing the two things it had always done, which is arguing and counting.

Wick Venn was engaged on the Friday as a day-labourer at four shillings a day for the works about the branch, out of the Assembly's witness fund and not out of the district's three pounds and sixteen, and the fund's rate is two shillings, and he took it and asked for the other two to come out of what the hearing would cost, and it did.

"I have been a day's labourer on this hill since the sixth day of the month before," he said, to the woman from the top end with the slate. "Four shillings, the Guild's rate, the same for everybody, and I have had a bonus of four shillings handed to me that I gave back in a lane in front of a surveyor, and I am not going to be thanked for this either. You can put that on the slate and read it out, and the woman in the lane will not say it because she has never once said it to anybody and I am not asking her to start."

And the warden's return for that week went into the district book on the Saturday morning with two of its five boxes filled, as it has been since the twenty-second day of this month, and *row number* reads **none held**, and *tanks* reads *three tanks, thirty-eight pails in the yard, the yard cistern, the dispensary churn*, and *gallons* and *last serviced* are empty because a gallons box can hold only one of the cistern, the dispensary and the daily draw, and a last-serviced date is a date a keeper writes, and the box headed *keeper* reads, in Pell's fair hand, in a book with four copies of it in this city:

> *keeper, none held*
