# Chapter 0028 — Two Hundred And Forty Gallons

Hanne Roeg had called the yard for eleven o'clock on the twelfth and had not had to call it, and she had thought about that for two days and had not enjoyed any part of it.

The thinking went like this. She had put the date on the fourth step in chalk on Friday night, the way her mother had put dates on things, and on Saturday morning at about a quarter past ten there were ninety people in the lane, and by a quarter to eleven there were a hundred and thirty, and they had come without being asked and they had brought their pails and stayed in the sun, and she stood on the fourth step looking down at them and thought: *a hundred and thirty people have come to a meeting that I did not have the authority to call, because I am a woman on four steps who signed a form.*

"That is what this is," she said out loud, to nobody, before anybody else was awake enough to hear. "That is what signing is. It is not being counted. It is being spoken to at eleven o'clock on a Saturday by a person who cannot be spoken back to."

She got up the step with her cane and made them wait four minutes, on purpose, because her mother had told her once that a thing asked for is a thing given, and a thing waited for is a thing owed.

---

She had three things on the slate and she had not written them, and she stood on the step and read them off the slate she could see from the top, which was all of them at once and out of order, which is how they came.

"Water first," she said, "and I am going to say why water first, and it is not because a physician is frightened, it is because a yard that argues about paper for two hours while a tank goes down is a yard that has decided what it is, and I have decided what I am on Friday and I am telling you now so that nobody can say afterwards that I waited.

"Doctor Sen says there is one thousand nine hundred and fifty gallons in that tank and that it is not water. There is a dispensary with seven gallons in a jar and twenty-three gallons of money in it. There are four shillings of the eight pounds gone on a morning in Vellum, and I want it read out, because four shillings is nothing and four shillings is a fifth of what we have, and this lane has not had five pounds in one place since the flood year.

"Seven pounds sixteen. And the last two men who came up this road with a cart would not come into the lane at all. They would come to the cattle track and stand there. So the money in the bucket has no seller. That is the thing I want said first, before anything else, because everybody in this yard has been doing that sum since Tuesday and every one of them has been doing it as though there were a man at the other end of it, and there is not, and it is not anybody's fault and it is not a market because I said no to a market on the thirty-first and I meant it."

Nobody contradicted her, and two men who had been carrying pails up the road for nine days had the grace to look at the ground.

"So the first thing to decide is not whether to spend the money. It is who fetches it, and how far, and out of whose cart, and I am telling you now that I have not got an answer and that I am asking."

---

The answers took forty minutes and were better than she expected and worse in one respect.

Marda Ollan did the sum again in front of everybody, because she had the ledger and because Marda Ollan would not have it said twice.

"Eight pounds at eightpence is two hundred and forty gallons," she said. "That is the best price anybody has quoted and it was quoted by a man who would not come up the track. At ninepence it is two hundred and thirteen. At a shilling it is a hundred and sixty, and a shilling is what a man with a barrel of his own charges to come up a road in this country, and I have had that quote from the cattle track twice this week and I have written both of them in this book.

"And I have sat here since Tuesday letting a yard do that sum as though the only thing standing between this lane and two hundred and forty gallons of clean water was the price. There is another thing standing in the way and it is a cart. A cart holds forty gallons. Two hundred and forty gallons is six cart-loads and a road of four miles and two days each way, and there is no cart in this lane that is not already doing something. So the eight pounds is not four hundred gallons of water. It is one day's worth of water for four children and whatever the man in the barrow can carry on his shoulder, and I want the yard to have that on the slate before it decides anything at all."

"Two hundred and forty gallons is three days and ten hours of what Doctor Sen needs, at seventy a day. It does not carry Sab Doulton into next month. It is three days and ten hours and after that we are back to a jar and a butt and a plate, and if we had the six cart-loads it would take us past the twentieth and no further, and I have not got a way of saying that without it sounding like a speech, so I have said it."

"How much of that is for the Doulton child alone," said somebody at the back.

"None of it," said Yara Sen, from the front, without turning round. "None of that figure is hers. I told you on the ninth and I will tell you once more. Seventy a day is the whole lane. If you spend the whole of the eight pounds on the Doulton child and do not spend it on the rest, you have bought a child three days and you have told every other family in this lane what a child is worth in gallons, and I will not write either of those things in my book."

"What will you write?"

"That I was asked the question on the twelfth, in a yard, by a person I could not see, and what I said."

---

Bel Crale spoke third and she spoke for about a minute and a half and she had prepared it, which Hanne Roeg could tell because Bel Crale had a piece of paper.

"I do not want to spend it," Bel Crale said. "I have run a room in this lane on forty-one pounds for a season and I have kept it illegible on purpose for a year, and if I start buying water with the district's money I am a room that can be found. That is my trade and I am not giving it up for Sab Doulton.

"And I am going to spend it, because on the twenty-seventh of this month a man is going to come down this track with a form and one of the boxes on it says *water supply in connection*, and the answer to that box is going to be a plate and a cistern and a jar, and if the answer to that box is a cistern and a jar and a barrow and eight pounds, then this lane is a place where the water comes in and goes straight out again, and there is a district in this city with a name on it that is going to be like that for a hundred years.

"So I want four things done with the money and I have written them down, because I am a woman with a piece of paper and a bad memory and I have written them down. Water for the clinic, fetched by somebody who can fetch it, and put in the book with the dates on it and no name on it if the person fetching it would rather not have a name on it. A day for the yard's water, at whatever a pail costs, to anybody who wants it, and not to anybody who does not. And the fourth one is that we buy it and we record it, and the record says who bought it, from where, at what price, and what it was for, and I want Marda Ollan to write that in her ledger in her own hand, and I do not want it ever read out loud again."

Nobody argued with Bel Crale, which is not the same as agreeing with her, and Hanne Roeg, who had been on four steps for seventy-one years and had never once been able to get a yard to agree with anything, noticed the difference and let it stand.

---

The second thing was the letter, and it was the second thing because Hanne Roeg had put it second on purpose, and she said why before anybody asked.

"Because if I put the water second we will spend the morning on water and go home, and a lane that has spent its morning on water has not decided anything. So: the second thing, and it is a piece of paper, and it is going to cost us more than the water and I have costed it."

She had costed it. That was the thing about her that Marda Ollan resented most and respected most.

"It goes to Sluice Warden Pell. Not to the commissioner's office. To Pell, for the district book, because a warden cannot refuse a thing — he can only return it with reasons, in a form, on a date, signed — and because in nineteen years nobody in this city has ever found one word of his to be made up, and because I would rather be described by him than by a man in an office with a seal.

"And it says four things. It says that on the ninth of this month three men of this district returned from a descent on a sealed works and reported a chamber on the works at Ashgate with standing water in it and a head of five point eight nine metres, and that the chamber is beneath this row, and that there is a second settlement of eleven houses on the works above it with thirty-four persons in it, and that the settlement is not on any schedule in this city and neither are we, and that the pumping plant on that works has been abstracting from that chamber against a certificate which certifies the chamber void, and that the certificate is signed.

"It asks for two things. It asks that the district examination promised on the twenty-seventh of this month be brought forward, and it asks for a structural examination of the works at Ashgate, which is a thing a warden's office has no power to order and which the letter says so in its own second paragraph, because a document that asks for what cannot be given is a document that cannot be refused for asking too much.

"And here is what it costs, and I have written it on the slate and I am going to read it out." Her voice did not change and her hands were flat on her knees. "There are now three documents in this city with the name of this lane in them, and there were none on the twelfth of the twenty-second, and there are three because I signed a form, and because a conservator wrote a tracing in my hand, and because this letter. And a district examiner with a box headed *water supply in connection* who arrives early has a chamber under the lane, thirty-four people over it, a plate, a weeping outfall, a cistern at three foot four inches, a physician's book with nine children in it, and eight pounds. And he will fill all of it in, and every column of it will be true, and the third column of it will be the name of a district on a schedule, which is the only thing any of us has ever wanted."

"Ma'am," said a man near the wall, "that is a good thing to ask for."

"It is a good thing to ask for," said Hanne Roeg, "and I did not sleep on Thursday. I want that said in a yard, and I want it said by me and not discovered about me. I am seventy-one and I have a grandson at a school in the fenner district and he is ten and he does not know that there is any of this, and if that box gets filled in this lane stops being a place he can be told he came from and becomes a thing in a report, and there is no road back off that, and I would have put it in the letter and let the examiner find it out on his own if I had not thought about it for two days, and I have thought about it for two days and I am telling you instead."

---

The third thing she left until four o'clock in the afternoon, when the sun was off the yard and the flags were warm, and she said that she was leaving it to last because it was the only one of the three that she could not decide on her own and she was not going to have decided it by eleven o'clock in the morning like a woman who had not slept.

"There are thirty-four people two miles up that hill," she said, "whose water comes out of their own kitchen floor at about a hundred gallons an hour, and who have never been asked a question by anybody in this city in thirty years, and two of them are this boy's aunt and the boy that carries the pail, and I am not going to name them because naming people is what got this lane into a book in the first place.

"We can ask them for water. A hundred and twenty and six people asking thirty-four people for water, when the hundred and twenty and six have two hundred gallons left in a tank they cannot give to a child, is a reasonable thing to do and it is a thing I would do for a stranger in a wet week.

"And it will mean that the first thing a person in that row ever hears from this city is a knock on their door from a district with no row in it, asking for the thing they have never once had to share, and there is a pump on that hill that has not been paid since the second of this month, and a man in an office four miles away who certified their well dry six years ago, and if the office ever finds out that we have been drawing off a works that it has certified void, then the row is finished, and eleven houses are finished, and it will not be our fault and it will not matter whose fault it was."

The yard was quiet.

"So I am not going to decide that on a Saturday," said Hanne Roeg. "I am going to write it down in the sheet and I am going to send the sheet, and I am going to put that question in it in my own hand as a question, and a person in Vellum is going to read a sheet from this lane with a question in it that nobody in this city has ever put in a city document, which is whether a district with no row may ask a settlement with no row for water, and I do not care what the answer is. I care that the question is on a piece of paper with a date and a name on it, because in four weeks a man with a form is going to come down that track and this lane is going to be a question on a piece of paper instead of a hole in a district schedule."

---

She wrote it on the fourth step, at about five o'clock, with her own hand, and it took her until about seven because her hand got worse toward the end and she did not pretend it had not, and she had the whole of it read out in the yard before the light went, because a document nobody has heard read is a document half the lane believes is a lie, and because she had been told that by a woman from Vellum in a yard on the twenty-eighth and had thought at the time that it was a trick and had since worked out that it was a rule.

And at the foot of it, in a hand that had got worse in the last two lines and had not got worse in a way that made it a different hand, she wrote the third thing, which was the one about the fifth, and the sixth, and the seventh, and the ninth of the month, and which was not part of the letter to the warden at all and which she read out separately and which cost her more than the letter.

> *And I record, in my own hand, on the twelfth day of this month, that on the fifth day of this month at dusk three men of this district went down to a cut on a sealed works without the knowledge of the district's named representative, and without the fourth step's consent, and were not asked and were not stopped, and I had already refused them on the first of the month with six reasons, and I have read those six reasons over again in the last eleven days and I do not withdraw one of them and I was right.
> *And I record that I did not stop them, and that I was not able to, and that a man of thirty-three came up my four steps on the fourth and told me he was going and I heard him say it and I said a thing about the door and he went round it, and that this was not an accident of the morning, it was a decision, and it was his and I want it in a book with a date on it in case a man with a form asks me in October why there is a hole in a hill in a district of one hundred and twenty-six persons.*
> *And I record that I hold, in my own hand, a second tracing of the survey redaction folio of this lane, made on the twenty-eighth of this month by me, and certified by a conservator, and that it is the only copy in this city that is not in the possession of the Memory Office, and that I have been thinking for three days about whether to hand it over, and that I have decided to keep it, and that if a man from Vellum ever stands on the fourth step and asks for it he will have to come and sit down.*

Nobody said anything about it. A woman near the arch said afterwards, to another woman, that it was a shame, and was told by the second woman that it was not a shame, it was a *record*, and the first woman said she had meant that kindly and was told again, patiently, that it was a record and not a thing to be sorry for.

And Bel Crale put four pounds in a slate in a barrow at the wall at half past seven that evening, at a shilling the gallon, for eighty gallons, and Marda Ollan wrote it in the ledger in her own hand and said the sum out loud and said the words *entered in this book* twice, and then wrote the balance, which was three pounds sixteen shillings, under it, and underlined that too.

And it was Wick Venn's cart, because it was the only cart in the lane that had anybody standing at the shafts, and he did it in the dark on the morning of the thirteenth because he was a man who did things in the dark now and had stopped finding it remarkable.

Nobody slept much. The tank was two feet eleven inches.
