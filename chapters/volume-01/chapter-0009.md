# Chapter 0009 — The Commissioner's Kindness

The Water Office kept the acting commissioner's office on the top floor because the top floor had the good light, and the good light was the reason the acting commissioner's office had been the top floor since the building was new. Tarin Rusk came up those stairs at twenty past nine on the tenth day of the ration with the blue book under his arm and a recalibration log in his coat, and he was let past the outer door by a clerk who had been told to let him past, which was the first thing that went wrong.

The second thing that went wrong was that Halden Roe had cleared the far end of the long table.

That was all. He had not locked the door or taken the seal off anything. He had a chair pulled round to the side of the table with its back to the window, a cup of water, a clean sheet of good paper, and Deryn Fenn sitting on the bench against the wall with a requisition pad because Fenn was the calibration clerk and Fenn was the only person in the building who was allowed to witness a charge. Osla Vint stood at the far end by the press with her arms folded.

"Sit down, Tarin."

"Commissioner."

"Sit down. That is not a request and it is not a favour; it is a man being charged being given the chance to be sitting down when it happens." Roe was about fifty, and greying at the sides in the way of a man who had spent thirty years in a building with bad ventilation, and he had a face that gave nothing away at all, and he pushed the good paper two inches towards the empty chair. "You have a log."

"I have a log." Tarin sat. "And I have the answer to requisition eighteen, which went by coach on the fifth, and I have not had an acknowledgement of either, and I have been three days out of this district and a day and a night of that was a mountain road in the rain."

"You have had neither because nobody signs for them until they are read by a person with a room," said Roe. "That is not discourtesy. It is what happens when an office is short of eleven people and one of them is a commissioner. I will read the log now. Fenn will witness."

Tarin put the log on the table.

It was four pages and it was a model of what a decommissioning recalibration should look like. It gave the date and the time and the instrument and its last calibration, and the stilling tube condition, and the closure state of the main wheel, and eleven readings taken over two hours with the floats reset between them, and it recorded the discovery of live pressure on a sealed structure, and it recorded in the plainest language in the office that the inspection gate had been unsealed with a bar by the signatory at 06:11, before the warden check, without authority, and that he had done it because the floats would not settle and a man who cannot trust a float must not file a float.

There was not one dishonest sentence in it. That was the point of it, and Roe took four minutes to arrive at the point, reading with a finger, and Tarin watched him get there.

"Five point eight nine," said Roe.

"Yes."

"Eleven readings, all within two millimetres."

"Yes."

"Out of a sealed structure, at six in the morning, on a works the Office has recorded as void for twenty-one months, using an instrument the Office certified as unserviceable in the fifth month of last year, which is certified here—" he turned the page over and laid it flat, "—on the eleventh of May, in your own hand, at my request, because you told me the float would not seat at any head."

"Three millimetres of float travel over the whole scale," Tarin said. "You were right and I certified it right."

"So an engineer certified an instrument unserviceable at a sealed works on the eleventh of May, and the same engineer wrote a valid log from that instrument at that works on the first of this month, and the figure he obtained is five point eight nine metres." He put his hands flat on the table. "Tarin, I am going to be fair to you, and then I am going to be fair to nobody, and you are going to hate the second part, and I would rather you did not waste the afternoon by defending the first."

"Go on."

Roe turned the log round and put his finger on the top of the fourth page, which was a sheet Tarin had not seen, because Tarin had not been in the building since the second.

"This came up from Vellum on the third. It is a certified discrepancy entered in the requisition book by a conservator in the Municipal Memory Office, under her own hand, in which she declines to certify folio eleven of the Ashgate maintenance volume." He read the passage. "One leaf substituted: different stock, no watermark, reduced width. Pressure shadow of removed writing on the reverse, showing an intake head at the main wheel, gate state shut, and the word *maintained* in a second hand. And the figure, which she certifies to two places, is five point eight nine."

The room was extremely quiet. Fenn's pen had not moved.

"That is where your number came from," said Roe. "There is nowhere else in the basin where that number exists, and it existed before you were born, and you have gone up a hill with a bar and come back with it to two decimal places."

"I measured it."

"You measured something." Roe did not raise his voice and did not need to. "I have been doing this for twenty-two years, Tarin, and I have watched men who measured something come down off a mountain with a number in a book and a story about pressure, and every single one of them was measuring the thing they had gone up there to see. You went up there at six in the morning without authority to a sealed works and you opened a gate, and now there is water in a channel, and you have a figure, and it is *wonderful* that it is the same figure. It is wonderful. It means somebody kept a copy."

He let that sit, and then he said, more quietly:

"There is also a notice of uncontrolled channel condition on the warden's board with the cause box empty, sent to this office on the first, and returned to this office on the third with a line in the margin saying that no district in the schedule corresponds to the channel described. That is not a service failure. That is a man who found a condition he could not put on a form and decided that the form was the problem. And I know that, because I have done it."

"For two days you did not answer it."

"I did not answer it for two days because I could not answer it, and by the third day I had a man in a cellar in Vellum with a number, and the number turns out to be a hundred and eighty-one years old, and I have to decide this morning what the Water Office is going to say in a public proceeding about a page that somebody in this city cut out of a book." He sat back. "So let me tell you what I have decided, and then you can be angry with me, which is cheaper than what I was going to have to do."

Roe reached into the second drawer and brought out a folder with a printed heading on it and put it in the middle of the table, and Tarin did not need to read it to know.

"Before that," Roe said. "North Sallow pumping district. Nine hundred and forty properties, four pumps in good order, a cistern programme that is actually finished, and a district superintendent who retires in the spring. It is a real post. The pay is the same. You would have a desk, a budget you could spend, a workshop with a roof, and a district that has not had a cistern at risk since the year before you were born." He folded his hands. "I would like you to take it. I would like it very much, and I am going to say the reason, and the reason is not that I am fond of you."

"Then say it."

"Because the terrace district has a man in it who can do something nobody in this building can do, and I have watched for three weeks as that man has turned a sealed works into a live argument, and if I leave him there then within a month this office is going to be asked by a council whether the lower terrace has water or not, and the honest answer is that it has five point eight nine metres in a pipe, and the true answer is that the pipe is somebody's, and I would rather he was in North Sallow making four pumps work while I find out whose pipe it is."

Tarin looked at the folder.

"That is a good offer," he said.

"It is."

"It is a better post than this one. It is a district with a cistern programme that is finished. There are nine hundred and forty properties on it and not one of them is under a plate." He put his hand flat on the log. "And if I go to North Sallow, the reason will be written down, and the reason will be that the man who opened the Ashgate inspection gate was moved, and then in four months somebody will ask why the Ashgate inspection gate was open, and the answer will be in a file with a word about *transferred* in it, and that file will be the only record of the gate in this office, and you will have made it yourself."

"That is the entire benefit of the offer," said Roe. "It is that after today you will not be the man who did it."

"No," Tarin said. "I won't."

He stood up. He was aware, with a clarity that arrived slightly too late to be useful, that he was about to do the single most expensive available thing.

"Commissioner, before you read that. I want to say something and I want Deryn Fenn to hear it and I want it minuted if there is any way at all in this building to minute it."

Fenn looked up. Osla Vint unfolded her arms.

"The figure of five point eight nine on that page is not a number that anybody invented," Tarin said. "It is the true reading of a head that has been standing on that gate since before the flood, and it is on the page because somebody took it off the record and put a clean leaf in its place so that the record would say the gate was dry. That is the work of an office, Commissioner, not of a forger, because a forger writes a number that suits him and this has a real reading lifted out of a book and a dull column of true readings left above the hole. And the word on the page is *maintained*, which means the man who did it expected to be found and wanted somebody to answer for it."

"That is a great deal of accusation for a man who was in a cellar five days ago," said Roe.

"It is one accusation, and it is the only one I can make, and I am making it to your face and to Fenn's face and not to a magistrate, because I have no page, no witness and no standing, and if I wait for standing I will not make it at all." Tarin's voice had not risen and he was aware of every person in the room looking at him. "The decommissioning certificates in that folder, and the gauge certificate in your own hand, were filed by this office over the last six years, and they say there is no residual head at Ashgate. There is a residual head at Ashgate.

"And there is a pumping plant in the old filter house under those works that has been running at night for the better part of a year. I heard it in a hole in the ground at ten o'clock on the night of the sixth, in a lane, under the flags, knocking twice a second, and it is not on any sheet I have ever signed. And you know it is there, because you certified the works as dead, and a dead reservoir has no lawful allocation, and no man pumps from something that does not exist without somebody having decided that it does not exist."

Roe looked at him for a while.

The expression on his face had not changed once since Tarin had come into the room, and now it changed, very slightly, into something that was almost respect, and Tarin understood that he had lost.

"You have one of those," said Roe, "and you have no idea what it is. You are telling me that this office falsified a record in order to permit illegal abstraction. In a city where the Office of the acting commissioner is a temporary appointment held by a man whose predecessor resigned over a smaller version of the same sentence." He opened the folder. "If that is true, then I am either the last honest man in Cresset or the man who has been covering it, and I have not got a third option, and I have got nine clerks above me and a council meeting on the twenty-second."

"You have got a piece of paper in Vellum with a real reading on it."

"I have got a piece of paper in Vellum that a conservator has declined to certify and a log from an engineer who unsealed a structure, and I have got a notice with an empty box in it, and I have got a pumping noise in a building that two hundred people have slept beside for a year." He put the folder down and squared it. "And I have got a man in front of me who has known about all of it since the first of this month and who sat on it for three days out of his own district with a day and a night of it on a mountain road, and then came back up that hill, and posted nothing, and told nobody, and did the one thing I could not have stopped him from doing, which was to stand in my own office and say it out loud in front of witnesses."

He turned to Fenn.

"Calibration clerk. You have heard a charge preferred. I am going to read it and you are going to witness it. The charge is *tampering with city records*, arising out of a recalibration log filed by this engineer on the second day of the month, and out of the correspondence with the Municipal Memory Office concerning folio eleven of the Ashgate maintenance volume, and I am going to say now, in front of you, that the log is the primary evidence, and that if the log is a copy then the copying is the tampering, and I am going to read the log's own words back to its author, which is the only place this city ever gets its justice."

He read Tarin's own sentence back to him, word for word, from the fourth page, and it was the sentence about the float, about a man who cannot trust a float, and it sounded in his own mouth like something written by a man who had already decided to be charged.

"The stand-down takes effect now," said Roe. "You will surrender your seal, your district reference, and your keys to the lower terrace works at the desk outside, and you will not go past the second gatehouse on the terrace road without a warden, and the desk, the seal, the reference and the keys are the whole of what I am taking. Fenn will escort you."

"Am I dismissed?"

"You are stood down pending a hearing, which is not the same thing, and which is worth ten days of nothing if the hearing finds for you and worth a great deal less if it does not." He held out his hand. "I am sorry. I have read the log four times and I think it is the work of an honest man who did a dishonest thing for the right reason, and I have no idea what to do with a man like that, which is why I offered you North Sallow first."

Tarin looked at the hand and did not take it, and then he took it, because not taking it would have been a smallness, and Roe's grip was dry and brief and entirely fair.

At the desk outside, Fenn took the seal and the keys and read the reference off the slip and put all three in the box, and Fenn's hand was shaking, and Fenn said, in a voice with nothing in it at all, "I did not think you would say it."

"No," Tarin said. "Neither did I."

He took the blue book and the bar and the folding rule and the chalk and the tin pot out of the low drawer of his own desk, because those were not the office's, and because the drawer was not locked, and because he had been putting his hand on the front of that drawer for four years when he was frightened, and it was not there any more, and he took everything out of it anyway.

Osla Vint was at the top of the stair.

"The terrace cisterns," she said. "Row seven to twelve. Eleven days as of this morning's return, and that is out of what is in the tanks, not what is being drunk, and the low pump has been dry since the ninth."

"I know."

"You sealed the branch."

"I closed it because three children were sick."

"Yes," said Osla Vint. "I know that. Everybody in the building knows that. It is on the warden's board in your handwriting, sort of." She looked at him properly, for the first time in four years. "You have got until the twenty-first. The review is on the twenty-second, and it will be put off, and the twenty-first is not the review. The twenty-first is when there is nothing left in those tanks, and after that the row is drinking out of the courtyard cistern, and the courtyard cistern is the one your water has been going into since the first."

"Right," Tarin said.

"There is a plate under that courtyard," she said. "I have never seen it. Nobody has ever seen it. I asked, once, when I was a junior, and I was told there was no plate and that I should be more careful about asking questions about plates."

He walked down the stairs and out into a city that was eleven days away from empty, holding a bar, and there was nobody at all in the basin to ask.
