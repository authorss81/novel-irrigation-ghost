# Chapter 0026 — The Word Discharge

The tank in the blue steps was three feet four inches down on the morning of the ninth and the woman who had been selling it out of it at sixpence the gallon was standing at the wall with her pails stacked and her arm in the water up to the elbow, feeling for the bottom, because she had stopped trusting her arm.

"I have been doing this thirty-one years," she said. "I know the tank. I have never once had to find the bottom."

Yara Sen wrote **three feet four inches** on the slate, and the derivation under it, and put the slate on the low house step where a yard could read it, and then she went and stood in front of the yard and let the three men come down the cattle track, because she had spent a month learning that a fact put up in a yard in daylight survives a week and a fact said in an office survives a day.

They came down at about eight. Tarin Rusk, a young man with a lamp and a burnt-out hand in a clean dressing, and a boy of twenty-two who had not slept a whole night in four days.

Hanne Roeg was on the fourth step with the mat over her shoulder and had been there since seven, and she had the warden's note from the thirty-fourth in her apron because there was nowhere else in a house that kept papers, and she said, to nobody in particular:

"Well. Say it in daylight, then, and I will hear it in daylight, because I have had eleven days of hearing things at night."

---

Yara Sen got in first, which she had been thinking about since the sixth and had not been sure of until half past seven that morning, when she had understood that if she was going to say it she was going to have to say it before anybody asked her a question, because a physician who answers a question has already lost the ground.

"Water," she said. "That is what I am here about, and I am going to do it first and in the wrong order, and I would like the yard to let me finish.

"I am not going to tell you the word conspiracy this morning. I do not have one and I am not going to invent one, and the man I would have to invent it about is in an office four miles away and has never heard of this lane, which is the whole of the difficulty. So here is the word and I want it on the slate under the tank.

> *Discharge.*

"A discharge is not a leak. A leak is a fault and a fault gets mended. A discharge is water that somebody is moving, on purpose, out of somewhere, into somewhere else, and the two somewheres are both a long way apart and the people at the end of it are not the people who started it. That is all the word means. I have wanted a better word since Tuesday and there is not one, and a word I cannot improve is a word I can defend.

"Every drop that has come into this lane since the first day of the month has come off a works pump two miles up the mill road. Not out of a rain. Not out of a well. Not out of the hill. Somebody has been pumping it and pushing it down a covered channel laid at a foot in the hundred and it has come out of a stone arch in the yard behind the third step and gone into a cistern, because this yard's drain is a lid of iron with four bolts on it and there is nowhere else for it to go.

"And it has been in the ground under this lane since the first day of the month, and it is in the tanks, and it is in the pails, and it is in the lane, and it is in the boys' hands, and it is in Sab Doulton's mouth on Tuesday morning, and I want that written down in a yard with a hundred and twenty people standing in it, because a thing that has been swallowed for a month is a thing nobody can be shown afterwards."

---

Then she put the slate round, and people read it, and she answered questions in the order they came, which was the order a yard asks them in, and none of the first four was the one she would have chosen.

The first was a woman asking whether her landlord would hear about it, and Yara Sen said that she did not know and that it depended entirely on whether the landlord read the district book, and the woman said that her landlord could not read, and the yard laughed, and the woman did not find it funny.

The second was a man asking whether they should go on selling the tank. Yara said yes, and the yard said *what*, and she said it again.

"Yes. Sell it. Three foot four inches of it, at sixpence, to anybody with a pail. I know what I have just said. I am telling a yard that has nine children in it to go on selling the only water in it to strangers, and I would like somebody in this yard to write down the hour so that in four weeks when somebody asks me why, there is an hour on a slate.

"And here is why. Because the alternative is that a hundred and twenty-six people drink it, and I do not know what is in it in a form I can measure, and I have had four children in a book for a fortnight and I am not going to feed it to nine of them for three days to avoid explaining myself to a man with a barrow. Sell it. Take the money. It is not ours."

The third was a man who wanted to know whether the city would do to this lane what it did in the spring, which was a different question wearing the same coat, and Marda Ollan answered it from the fourth step, and Hanne Roeg let her, and did not interrupt.

"They will put a form against it," Marda said. "They will put a form against it because there is now a thing they can put a form against, and before the ninth of this month there was not, and that is the first time in my life that being a fact has cost me anything."

The fourth question came from Old Marden, from his own step, in the voice of a man of eighty-one who has decided that if he is going to be asked a question in a yard he would like it to be the correct one.

"Then the supply did not stop," he said. "You are telling me the water has not stopped. I want to be sure I have it, because I have been in this lane fifty years and I have heard a supply stop and I have not heard a supply diverted, and those are not the same and one of them is a piece of luck."

"No," said Yara Sen. "It has been diverted. That is the whole of it and I am not being kind. There is a difference between a thing stopping and a thing being taken somewhere else, and the difference is that a thing that stops can start again when the man who stopped it feels like it, and a thing that has been taken somewhere else comes back when he feels like that too, and there is no way in the world for a lane three miles away with a plate under it to make a man in an office feel like it."

Marda Ollan wrote that on the second slate, in her own hand, without being asked.

---

Tarin Rusk told it after that, and he told it badly, because he had told it in his head for two days and in the yard it came out in the wrong order, and the yard was patient with him in a way that was worse than impatience.

Then the arithmetic, which he put on the slate himself, because it was his and because the last two times he had done arithmetic in a yard it had gone against him and he had decided that a man who has been wrong in public should keep doing it in public.

> *Tank, evening of the 2nd: 4,700 gal, at the lip.*
> *Sold, 3rd to 9th: 340, 340, 300, 280, 240, 220, 190 = 1,910.*
> *Lane drawing, 3rd to 9th, 70 a day: 490.*
> *Evaporation, 50 a day: 350.*
> *Out: 2,750. In: nothing.*
> *Standing, evening of the 9th: 1,950 gallons, which is three feet four inches in a tank that holds four thousand seven hundred.*

"Somebody is going to want to do that sum out loud," said Yara Sen, "so I have done it. A thousand nine hundred and fifty gallons, in a district of a hundred and twenty-six people, is twenty-seven days at seventy a day. It is twenty-seven days of *water*. It is not twenty-seven days of what is in it, and the difference between those two things is four children in my book, and it is not a thing a tank can fix, and I would like it understood that I have not been waiting for a tank to fix it, because it cannot."

"Two hundred and forty gallons," said Marda Ollan.

Everyone looked at her.

"Eight pounds, at eightpence the gallon, which is what clean water is at now, is two hundred and forty gallons. That is three days and ten hours of the seventy. I have had the money in a bucket since the thirtieth and I have been the person holding it, and I am not going to stand on this step on the ninth of the month and pretend that nobody in this yard has done that sum." She had the ledger in her hand and did not open it. "The fourth step is going to decide what to do with it on Saturday, in daylight, with the whole of the lane in it, and not before, and I am not going to have a physician do it and I am not going to have a man with a lamp do it, and if either of you want to say something about it you can say it on Saturday."

---

Then the dispensary, and it was at the end because Bel Crale made it be at the end, and because Yara Sen had a book and forty-one names in it and had been putting the fourth step's water in it since the thirty-first in a line under a rule in small writing.

"Seven of the nine are holding," she said. "Seven of the nine are not worse than they were on the sixth. That is not good news, that is *stationary*, and stationary in nine children in this weather is a finding and not a comfort.

"The fourth is not holding." She had the book shut, and she opened it, and she did not read from it. "Sab Doulton is seven and she has been at three points on the twenty-eighth, on the sixth, and again this morning, which is three times in a fortnight, and each time has been for longer, and this morning the skin of her palms is cracked and she has been sick twice since Tuesday and she has asked her mother twice this week where the front door is.

"Her mother is in this yard. I have said her name out loud because I have said nine names out loud in this lane this month and I have been called a great deal of things for it, and I am going to say the tenth, and I am going to say the rest of it, because there is nobody here to leave the room.

"She is at the beginning of the fourth stage. I have never had a fourth stage in this district. There is a fourth stage and I have read about it and I have had a man in the Reach die of it and I have not had one here. It is not a burn. It is a person losing the place they are standing in, and then the water going wrong in the blood, and then it is not water going wrong in the blood, it is the person.

"And the seventy gallons a day has stopped being a number I can give you." She put her hand flat on the closed book. "It was four children at four gallons, and five at two, and a hundred and seventeen at three pints, and that is sixteen and ten and forty-four, and that is seventy, and I have had that in my head since the twenty-seventh and it is the only sum in this district that anybody could check with a stick and a string.

"It is not a sum any more. A child in the fourth stage does not take four gallons. I do not know what it takes. There is no figure for it in this basin because there has not been one here, and I am telling you that in a yard because in four weeks somebody is going to ask a physician why the water in the blue steps was not given out according to a formula, and the answer is going to be that the formula was right until the ninth of this month and then it was not, and I did not have a second one."

Nobody said anything for about a minute. Somewhere down the lane a man was calling for a pail.

"Eleven gallons in a stone jar," said Yara Sen, "and a butt at the top end that I am going to have to tell somebody about this evening for cleaning water, and twenty-three gallons of money, and one thousand nine hundred and fifty gallons of the other thing."

She shut the book and put it under her arm.

"And I would like to say the last thing now while I am here and about it, because I have been not saying it for two days and it is a physician's job and not a favour. There are thirty-four people two miles up this hill whose water comes out of the ground at about a hundred gallons an hour, out of a hole in a kitchen floor, out of the thing you have all just heard about, and it has never been tested by anybody, and if you asked me this morning as a physician whether the nine children in this lane should drink it I would have to say yes and I would have to say that I have a right to that answer only if somebody who is not a physician asks me for it and not if I go and take it. I am putting that in this yard in daylight so that nobody can say afterwards that a physician kept it back."

A man near the arch said, quietly, to nobody: "I would ask you for it."

"I know you would," said Yara Sen, "and I have said it where he can hear me, and that is what daylight is for."
