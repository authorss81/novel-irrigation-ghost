# Chapter 0029 — What A Man Can Still Do

A cart is two wheels, a shaft and no grip, and Wick Venn found that out on the morning of the thirteenth at four in the morning, on a cattle track, in the dark, with a bad hand, and it was the single most useful thing anybody had said to him in a month and nobody had said it to him at all.

He had been managing his left hand by keeping it out of the way for ten days, which is a thing a man can do with a hand that has no feeling in two fingers as long as the hand is not asked to hold anything. He had been doing it in the dark and on the wall and in a yard, and it had worked, and it stopped working at about a mile and a half up the cattle track when the road went from a rutted track to a made-up stretch with a crown on it and the cart began to work, and Wick discovered that steering a cart with your left rein in a bad hand is not a matter of grip.

It is a matter of *feel*, and he could not feel it.

So he swapped them, and drove with his right hand on the near rein and his left holding nothing, and got a stone off the crown at about a mile and three-quarters, and stopped, and put both feet down, and got out in the dark and put his shoulder against the back of the cart and pushed it the last three hundred yards to the fenner turn, and arrived at the dispensary gate at about a quarter to six with a shaft rubbing his hip and his hand throbbing in time with his own pulse, which is a thing a bound hand does when it is not being looked at.

He had eight shillings' worth of a cart-load of nothing, four pounds, and a place he had not been in nine years: the lodge of the Reach waterworks, at the fenner turn, which is where the pipe work is let out and where Wick Venn had laid more lead than anybody alive, and where a man called Osgar Pike had said to him once, in a doorway, in a way that had seemed to him at the time like nothing at all, *when you have done another year I will put you in for a journeyman's paper, and the paper is worth more than your hands, and I will say so again in front of the steward.*

Nine years. Six days a week. And Wick had never once been in the lodge, and had never once been asked to be, and had known exactly why, and it was not the door frame, though the door frame was the reason it started.

It was that Ondra's door frame came down on the first day of the month off a wall that Ondra had paid for out of a wage he no longer had, and Wick had taken it down because it was in his way and he had not been asked, and the row had held it against him ever since, and Osgar Pike had heard about it from a carter, and a man whose sheet a carter has had a look at is a man with a sheet.

He stood at the lodge gate in the dark and thought about all of that for about two minutes and then went and knocked on the door, because there was a whole other thing, and it was more urgent.

---

The other thing was the water cart.

Wick knew the man. Everybody in the fenner district knew him, because he had been the one who used to come twice a week up the terrace road with clean water at twopence and had stopped on the thirtieth of the month, and everybody in four counties knew why he had stopped, because he was a man with a name and a row and a wife, and the blue steps had begun selling to the fenner district out of a cistern at threepence the gallon, and a man with a name cannot be seen buying from a place that is selling to his neighbours out of its own tank.

"Errol Slee," said Wick, at the door, in the dark, with a bad hand and a cart. "I am Wick Venn, out of the blue steps, and I have got four pounds and a cart."

"You have not got a cart," said a voice. "You have got a barrow's worth of trouble."

"I have got a cart," said Wick, "and I have not come to sell you anything, and I have not come to argue with you, and I am not going to say one word about the thirtieth unless you open the gate, because I have been told about the thirtieth by four people and I have come the last half mile working out what to say about it and I have decided that whatever I say will be a knife in one direction or the other and I would rather say nothing badly than something well."

The gate opened.

Errol Slee was fifty-odd and he had a lantern and a face that had not slept, and he looked at Wick's hand and at the cart and at the money, and he said:

"Where is it going."

"Seventy gallons a day into a dispensary in a lane that has got a jar and a butt and a plate under the floor and nine children in it with the marks, and a room that was opened in the spring on forty-one pounds and has fifteen shillings and fourpence in it tonight, and a seven-year-old at the third point for the third time in a fortnight." Wick Venn said it the way a man reads a list he has been given. "I have four pounds. I want eighty gallons at a shilling and I want you to bring it and I want to drive the second load, because I have not got another man in this lane with a cart and no one else in this lane with a cart is free."

"That is a shilling the gallon."

"That is a shilling the gallon and I have it counted out in my coat and I am not going to argue about the price with you in your own doorway at six in the morning, and if it is wrong you can have it for what it is worth and I will not say a word about it afterwards either."

The man took the money. He counted it, and he put it in a box, and he closed the box, and then he stood in his own doorway for a while with his hand on it, and said:

"I did not send you away on the thirtieth."

"I know."

"Somebody's man came up the road in front of me on the Wednesday with two carts and told me the price was going to three and that I would not get it at two, and he said it in the road, in front of me, on my own road, and I am not going to tell you what I said to him, and if I had gone up that lane and sold to you at twopence on the Friday I would have had men in four counties saying that I was the man who supplied the row that was selling them its own drinking water, and I have got a row of my own, and I have got nine years of it, and I have not got a basin of water in the yard this month either, which nobody has noticed and which I would like you to notice now and then say nothing about."

Wick Venn said nothing about it.

That was the whole of the transaction and it took about a minute and a half and neither man raised his voice again, and it was, in Wick's own judgement nineteen years later, the most decent piece of work he ever did, and it was done by two men not saying anything to each other on a cattle track before dawn.

---

The eightieth gallon was in the dispensary in the blue steps by nine o'clock on the evening of the thirteenth.

It came in a churn and four pails, and Wick carried the churn in with his right hand and set it down and put his weight on his hip to get it round the door frame, and Bel Crale counted forty gallons in the churn and forty in the pails and did not sleep that night, and the eighty gallons went in the book in four lines with the dates on them and the prices, and Bel Crale wrote the fourth line and then wrote in the column she had made for it, in the small writing, the thing she had asked to have written:

> *Entered in this book. Eighty gallons, at one shilling the gallon, the thirteenth day of this month, from the fenner turn. £4 0s 0d. Offered to the dispensary. Not drawn from the tank of this district, and no worse for it.*

Yara Sen came in at about eight and looked at eighty gallons of clean water in a town in a lane that had seven, and she did the thing she did, which was to sit down.

"Well," she said.

"Will it do?"

"It will do for four days, at seventy a day, and I will not draw it for four days, and it will be gone by the seventeenth of this month, and by the eighteenth the tank in the yard will be at about four hundred gallons and the day after that it will be at two hundred." She put her hand flat on the churn. "It buys four days. It is four days that I have not got, and I could not tell you in front of a child that it was a week."

Then she did the part of the evening she had been dreading, which was the ninth, and it was a boy of twenty-two who came into the dispensary at about half past eight and stood in the doorway and did not come in.

Niko Venn had not been in the blue steps for four days. He had been up at Ashgate and then he had been nowhere, and he had slept on the wall of the dispensary awning for two nights, which Bel Crale had permitted and Yara had pretended not to know about, and he had been down at the water cart all afternoon with the pails because a man with a bad hand cannot lift a churn and a boy can.

"Doctor," he said. "Can I look at the Doulton child."

"You may look at her. You may not hold her hand, and you may not say anything to her mother, and if you say her name to her in a tone you have got in three days' sleep I will put you out of my room."

He looked at her for about four minutes. He counted, on his own hand, with his own fingers, the way a boy counts; the pads of the index and middle fingers and the web between.

"Three," he said.

"Three."

"And she asked her mother twice this week where the front door is."

"She did."

"That is the fourth one." Niko Venn put his hand down. "My aunt kept a low pump for nine years and there was nothing to hand on when she died because there was nothing to hand on. That is what I have been telling people about since the seventh and I have said it like a thing I was proud of, and I have been up a hill and down a hole and under a street this month, and Doctor Sen, the reason there was nothing to hand on is that there was no *name* anywhere. The pump worked. Nine years. Nobody ever wrote it down, so the day my aunt died the pump stopped being a pump and it was a hole in a wall with a handle on it, and the row got four hundred feet of lead out of it in about a year and put a cistern in the top end and forgot it happened."

Nobody said anything.

"So that is what I am going to be," he said. "Not an apprentice in the Water Office. I have been saying that out loud for a fortnight and I did not know that I was saying it until about an hour ago, standing at a cart with a man from the fenner turn who did not want to be here, and it came out of me and it was true and it did not feel like anything."

Wick Venn, who was sitting on a bench outside with a bound hand and a coat over his knees, said: "You will want it writing down."

"I do not want it writing down. That is the whole difference between me and you and it is why you are the man on the record and I am not, and I have known that since I was eleven and I have been furious about it since I was eleven." He turned round. "But I will want somebody to know I said it, and there is a woman in here with a book, and she says things go in it, and I am not going to ask her to put a boy's ambition in a physician's ledger because it will look like an event. I will say it to you, and you will remember it, and if it turns out to be a load of nonsense then I will be twenty-three and able to be embarrassed about it myself."

"I will remember it," said Wick Venn, "and I am not a man anybody writes things down about, and if you want it kept I have got a slate and chalk and I will cut it in the yard tomorrow and you can put your own mark under it, and it will be yours and not mine and not the lane's."

Niko Venn thought about that for a moment.

"No," he said. "Not the yard. That is a rule in this lane and I have watched it get written and I am not breaking it with a thing nobody asked for. You remember it. That is the whole of what I have got and it is not very much, and I have had it since the fifth, and I have not told a soul because I could not think of one that was not the wrong one."

He went back out into the dark.

---

Yara Sen wrote eighty gallons and four days in her book and then, on the same line of the same page, because it was the same fact, the other thing.

> *13th day. Eighty gal of clean water, at a shilling, from the fenner turn, by two men who both said nothing about the thirtieth and neither of whom will ever say it again. Entered above. To be drawn from the 14th, 70 gal a day, and to be gone on the 18th.*
> *And on the same day, in a yard, on the evening of the twelfth, before I had said anything about either of those things, a boy of twenty-two told me and a man with a bad hand that he was going to give up an office he cannot get and take up a thing that has no name in this city and no paper and no wage, and that nobody was going to write it down for him, and that he had asked for it to be remembered by one man and not by a yard.*
> *I have entered it because this book is the only thing in this district that will not be lost, and because a thing that is only remembered by one man is a thing that dies with him, and I would like there to be a place where somebody can find out that it was said, in case it turns out to have been true.*

She shut the book.

On the evening of the fourteenth the tank in the yard was at nine hundred and fifty gallons, which is one foot seven inches in a tank that holds four thousand seven hundred, and there were two slates on the low house step with figures on them, and the words *discharge* and *one hundred and nineteen, so entered at the applicant's correction* were in three different buildings in a city of six thousand people, and a sheet of paper with a name and a question and a refusal in it was in a bag on a cart going up the cattle track toward a warden's office, and nobody in the blue steps had slept more than two hours together since the ninth.
