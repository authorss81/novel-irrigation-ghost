# Chapter 0018 — What The Ground Did

Yara Sen came down the lane at half past six in the morning of the thirty-first with a spade over her shoulder, because she had put a spade in the lane the day before and promised on the dispensary step that she would put it in properly, and a woman who says a thing in front of forty people gets it done before the light is good.

She put it in four places: at the wall where the pails stood, in the centre of the lane opposite the third step, in the ruts by the throat, and under the flags at the corner of the lowest room, which is where Tarin Rusk had been on his knees the previous afternoon with a bar and a face she did not care about.

At every one of the four places the spade came up wet at a hand's depth, and at the throat it came up wet at two, and she got down on her knees in the lane in a good wool skirt and put her hand flat on the surface of what was standing in it and left it there for a moment out of professional habit and then took it away, because it was not cold.

"Spade," she called. "Spade, get me the third jar and the lid and the pan, and get the Silver from the drawer in the dispensary, the spoon, not the fork."

Bel Crale brought them, and brought a bucket as well without being asked, because Bel Crale had been running a room on forty-one pounds for a season and knew what a woman doing this needed before she asked for it.

---

She did the whole of it in the yard in front of the row and she did it in the order that made the argument, and she did it with a spade and a jar and a lid, and not with an opinion.

First the jar. She filled it from the lane, and set it on the low house step, and put the pan under it, and lit nothing, and said: "That is going to stand until noon and I am going to come back at noon, and I want every person in this lane who has an opinion to have looked at the lid of that jar at noon and to have their own opinion about what they can see on it. I am not going to tell you what I think is in it. I am going to let chemistry be in charge for one morning."

Second the spoon. She took the silver spoon out of the dispensary drawer, which had been her mother's and which she had brought from Vellum in the spring because a silver spoon that has been in boiled water for nine years does not tarnish, and she put it in a pint of the lane water and set it on a plate in the sun, and turned it over, and said: "That is a hundred-year-old test and it is still the test. If the water is soft, the silver stays bright. If it has salts in it that want to be salts, the silver goes dull and cloudy at the bowl, and by the end of the day you cannot see your face in it. Look at it now and look at it at dusk. That is the whole experiment and it costs nothing."

Third the spade, and this was the one she had promised and this was the one she had brought it for, and she did it kneeling, with the four holes open in a row, and a yard of mud on her knees.

"Six inches," she said. "That is where the ground is wet, at the wall, at the centre, at the ruts, and under the corner of that room. It was ground that you could not get a wet trowel into a fortnight ago. It was a lane that drained nothing for thirty years and the reason it drained nothing is a plate of iron over the throat, and the reason the plate went on is the year this row came off the schedule, and the reason the row came off the schedule is on a piece of paper in a registry in this city that I have never read and do not expect to read."

"So the plate took it," said Bel Crale.

"The plate took it for thirty years and the ground got used to it, the way ground does." Yara wiped her hands down her skirt, which achieved nothing. "Ground under a street that cannot drain does two things. It learns to be a sponge. And it keeps the water. The water table under this lane has been standing at whatever height a plate over an outfall makes it stand for three decades, and it has been standing there quietly, and nobody has ever once had to notice, because the district's only clean water for three decades was a bucket off a well in the awning, and the well went down through the sponge and into something below the sponge, and the something below the sponge was above the water table."

"And now the water table is at the top of the sponge."

"Now the water table is at the surface of the street, and it has been since about eleven o'clock yesterday morning, and it is going to stay there for two days whatever I do, and the water standing in this lane is not going down today." She stood up and looked at the yard. "That is the finding. Not a mystery. A foot of water in a lane cannot go anywhere because there is nowhere for it to go, and it goes down, and the ground fills, and then the ground has to give it back somewhere, and the somewhere it gave it back to yesterday was a floor."

---

The tap on the terrace road main ran brackish by eleven o'clock, and by noon on the thirty-first there were nineteen people standing in front of it in a queue, and it was the last municipal water any of them had.

Yara Sen had put a pint of it in the pan with the lane water beside it, and they had stood at noon and looked at the two lids together, and the crust on the lane jar was a finger thick and grey-white and the crust on the tap jar was a third of that and yellow, and neither of them was a thing you could put in a child.

"Both of them," she said. "Both of those are the same water from different ends of the same problem. The lane water is what you put in the ground. The tap is what the city put in the ground, thirty years ago, in a lead pipe in a trench not six feet from where I am standing, and when the ground under a street comes up to the level of the pipe, the pipe stops being a pipe and becomes a length of lead with a hole in it that is the whole length of it, and the water outside it comes in, and the water outside it is now the water off a lane in the blue steps."

"How long?" said Marda Ollan, in a voice with no expression in it whatsoever.

"Your tap is clean now because it is running, and it will stay clean while it runs, and it will go off the moment the pressure drops, so keep it running." Yara Sen wrote something on the slate and did not read it out. "The pipe is not the problem. The pipe is forty years old and every lead main in this city is forty years old. The problem is a foot of water in a lane that has nowhere to go, and it will not go away until the ground under the lane can hold it or somebody unbolts a plate."

Nobody said the second part out loud, and everybody heard it.

---

She did the round at two o'clock and the four names in her book had gone the wrong way, and two of them had gone the wrong way a long way.

The Tall boy was at two points, which was where he had been on the twenty-sixth, and he had been sick in the night. The second child, the one under the arch, was at three points for the second time in nine days and had burning skin on the palms and was nine years old and could not stop putting his hands in his mouth, which is the third stage, which is the stage that comes next, and she had four days of clean water in the world and a fortnight of it to give him.

Then the room.

The lowest room on the left of the arch had water standing a hand deep in it and had had it since yesterday morning, and the flag at the corner was up about half an inch, and the boy who slept on the bed frame had slept in a chair in the low house, and Ondra had taken his frame apart and leaned it against the outside wall because there was nowhere else for it to be, and the whole of that household's belongings were stacked on the flags of the top end under a tarpaulin that had not been a tarpaulin in its life and had come from Wick's cart.

"Do not tell me it will go down," said Ondra, who had not asked her anything.

"It will go down. Tomorrow, or the day after. Two days, and the room will be wet and then it will be damp and then it will be a room with a damp corner in it, and you can have it back."

"Will it."

"I am going to be straight with you, because you have been straight with me all month and because you have lost a frame and a chest of clothes." She crouched down in the doorway, which she did not do to patients, and which she had decided to do to him. "There is something in it. Not much. It is not the fourth stage and it is not fever, and if the boy had stood in it he would have been at the second point in a week. But it is not nothing, and it is not water, and I would not have him back in that room for a month if I were you, and I have not got a better offer than the top step and a tarpaulin."

"Then he is on the top step until the end of the month," said Ondra.

---

The money came in on the afternoon of the thirty-first, and it was the single worst thing that happened to the blue steps in the month of the ration, and nobody had any warning of it at all.

It came in on a barrow, and then on two more barrows, and it came in because on the afternoon of the twenty-seventh a man from the Fenner district had walked four miles down a dry road and been given a pail of water he had not paid for, and had come back on the twenty-eighth with four men and two carts, and had been told, by a physician standing in a yard, in plain words, what was in it.

He had bought it anyway. Of course he had bought it. He had four miles of dry track and a cistern with a hole in the bottom and a family in it, and a woman in the blue steps had told him in front of a yard full of people that it would not be drinking water, and he had weighed that against eight children at home, and he had weighed it and had bought it, and he had not come and asked anybody's blessing, and he was not a villain, and there was nothing anybody could have done.

By the afternoon of the thirty-first the blue steps was selling water.

Bel Crale had stopped it on the second day, for about an hour, by standing in the lane with her arms out, and then had been asked to move by a woman from the third step whose own barrel was low, and had moved, because Bel Crale was a woman who could not stand in a lane being told by a person with a barrel when a person without a barrel was thirsty. It was the correct decision and she did not sleep that night.

They sold it at a penny the gallon on the twenty-eighth and twopence on the twenty-ninth, because that is what a thing nobody can use is worth when somebody has just been told in a yard that it cannot be used, and it went to threepence on the thirtieth and stayed there, out of a cistern that a physician had certified in front of a hundred and twenty people as unfit for human consumption.

Forty gallons went on the twenty-eighth and forty more on the twenty-ninth, and then it came in earnest — three hundred on the thirtieth and three hundred on the thirty-first — and by the evening of the thirty-first six hundred and eighty gallons had gone up the terrace road in somebody else's pails.

Eighty gallons at a penny and then at twopence, which is ten shillings. Six hundred at threepence, which is seven pounds and ten. **Eight pounds** in the lane in coin and small silver, in a bucket that had held chalk that morning.

And that was where the water for the clinic came apart.

The cart that had been coming up from the Sallow road twice a week with clean water at twopence the gallon stopped coming on the morning of the thirtieth, because the driver was a man with a name and a row of his own, and a man with a name cannot be seen selling water to the blue steps. Nobody had told him to stop. Nobody had threatened him. He simply had a row, and his row had begun to hear about a man selling to the Fenner district out of a cistern on the terrace road, and he had a wife.

The second cart came on the thirtieth, from a different direction, and charged ninepence, and would not come into the lane at all — would only come as far as the cattle track, three hundred yards short, and would not have it known that it was the dispensary.

The third came on the thirty-first at eightpence and would not come into the lane.

So: seventy gallons a day of clean water, of which the dispensary had eleven in a stone jar under the bench and a butt in the top end, and the dispensary had held fourteen pounds and sixpence on the first of the month, which is what was left of forty-one after the room and the kettle and the bin and Bel Crale's wages, and by the evening of the thirty-first it had fifteen shillings and fourpence.

Fifteen shillings and fourpence at the price of clean water on the third of the month buys twenty-three gallons. Twenty-three gallons is a third of one day, and the day after that is the first of the next month, and a child in the second stage needs his four gallons whether the first of the month has arrived or not.

And the money to fix it was in the lane, in a bucket, in silver, and the silver could not buy it, because everything for sale in the blue steps on the evening of the thirty-first was the one thing in the basin that nobody could use.

---

Yara Sen found Hanne Roeg on the fourth step at four o'clock in the afternoon and did the whole of it in about four minutes, because she had done this before, in the Reach, eleven times.

"A butt in the fourth step," she said. "How much."

"You know how much."

"Two hundred gallons if it is the big one. I want two hundred gallons, in pails, into the dispensary, this evening, and I will put it in my book against the four names in it, and I will put the amounts and the dates in the book, and I will not put your name in it if you would rather I did not."

Hanne Roeg did not answer that for a moment.

"You know what you are asking me for."

"I am asking you for water. I am not asking you for anything else. And I am going to tell you the price anyway, because you have paid every price this month and I would rather you knew this one on my mouth." Yara crouched down beside her, which she had not planned to do. "Your household is on a stamped form in a registry. If a dispensary in the blue steps is supplied from the fourth step, then any man who comes down here with a form in a fortnight can stand in this yard and look at a bucket coming out of a door, and write two lines about it. That is the whole of the risk. I am not able to make it smaller, and I am not going to pretend that the smaller risks are free."

Hanne Roeg sat on her own step and looked at the lane, where a man was loading a pail into a barrow for the fourth time that day.

"Then write it down," she said. "Write the two hundred gallons down in your book, and put the fourth step against it, because I have been un-findable for seventy-one years and I signed a form on Saturday and I am findable now, and I would rather be found with nothing than not found at all. And write this beside it, and I want it in your own hand, not anybody's: *offered freely by the fourth step, and the water is not fit to drink, and Doctor Sen has said so in this yard in front of a hundred and twenty people, and we are giving it away anyway.*"

Yara Sen wrote it down, and read it back, and got it right, and sat on the step for a moment afterwards with the book shut on her knees.

"There is one more thing and I am not going to be kind about it," she said, "because you are seventy-one and you can take it. From Saturday you have wanted to be countable, and you have been, and it has cost you the whole of what you had, and I want you to hear from a physician that the two things you have wanted are not both available. You cannot be findable and unfindable at once. A district that is on a schedule has a schedule, and a schedule has columns, and the columns get filled, and the filling is done by men with forms who are going to fill in a box about this lane for the rest of my life, and some of them will be fair and some of them will not."

"I priced it on Saturday," said Hanne Roeg. "I priced it on Saturday evening with a cup of Bel's water and I knew exactly what it cost. Write the two hundred gallons down."

Yara Sen wrote the two hundred gallons down, and it went in at two hundred, and at nine o'clock in the evening she boiled the first of it in the dispensary and gave it to a nine-year-old child in three doses over an hour, sitting on a stool with his feet off the floor, and she held the cup herself, and she did not go out of the room.

---

At the end of the day she made the entry in the book, and the book has three columns and the entry went in the third, which is *hands*, and then she went back and put one line in under the date, above the ruled line, in the small writing she used for things that were not clinical.

> *31st day. Six hundred and eighty gallons sold out of a cistern of which I certify the water is unfit for human consumption, at one, two and three pence the gallon on successive days, to persons not of this district, two days after that fact was stated in this yard in the presence of a hundred and twenty people. Eight pounds taken: ten shillings on the first eighty gallons, and seven pounds ten on the remaining six hundred.*
> *The price of clean water in this district has gone from twopence the gallon to ninepence on the thirtieth and eightpence on the thirty-first.*
> *There is now more money in this lane than there has been since the flood year, and not one of it can be spent on a child, because everything they have to sell is the one thing nobody is able to buy a use for.*
> *A sale is a fact. A fact with a price on it is a record. Nobody has to believe me about it. I have put it here where the next physician can find it, and I have put it here because the book is the only thing in this district that is not going to be lost, and because the next physician is going to arrive in this lane in about four weeks and find a well, a pump, a plate and a cistern, and is going to be asked by somebody in an office what this place is, and is going to want to know why there is money in it.*

She shut the book and put it under the bench, and there were nine children in the blue steps with the marks, and four of them were in a book with a fortnight on it that ran out on the fourth day of the next month, and in the yard the water in the lane was an inch and a quarter deep and was not going down, and on the step outside the low house a boy of nine was sitting with a bit of chalk in his hand, marking a cistern coping that he was not going to have to mark again until somebody filled it.
