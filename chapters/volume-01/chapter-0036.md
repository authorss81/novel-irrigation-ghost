# Chapter 0036 — Nine Years Of Tuesday Mornings

The filter-bed store at the top of the works had a cold hearth in it and a table that was two packing cases and a board across them, and Iven Marrow had put a lamp on the table and a small book beside it, and he had asked Tarin Rusk to come up at eight in the evening on the twenty-third, and the other two of the Guild team had gone down the cattle track at six without being asked to, and were by that time four miles off and would be at the counting-house by dark.

"You have a room to sleep in," said Marrow. "There is a straw pallet in the corner and it is not clean and it is dry, and it is two miles nearer a chamber than anything you will sleep in, and I am not going to be able to sleep tonight and I would rather have somebody in the room while I do not."

He did not offer it at the beginning. He made tea in a can, which tasted of iron and nothing else at all, and he put the can down and said:

"Three things and then you will go down the hill and I will not follow you down the hill, because there is nothing in it for me either way and I have come to this hill for a chamber and not for a fight.

"Work. I have a place. Grade two, inspector of high gates, thirty-one pounds a year and four shillings a day of finding expenses when I send you out, against your forty at the Office, which I have looked up, and I want you to hear the number and not be surprised by it, because a man who says *I will pay you more* is buying something and I am not buying you. It comes with six men and a tools chest and a mule and a boat for the tarn work in the spring.

"Access. That is the part I would have you think about for a week before you answer. The counting-house at Vellum holds eleven thousand four hundred leaves of maintenance record for every gate in this basin, from the first of them. I will put your name on the reading card for the whole of it. No sluicewright in the Guild has that card. Three men have held it, two of them are seventy, and one of those is dead, and I have had it for six years and I have not got time to read it, and I will not pretend that is the Guild's generosity, it is my guilt.

"And inside the eleven thousand is a thing you want more than you know you want yet. Every plate specification. Bore, date, gate, and the man who set it. Two hundred and thirty-nine plates in this basin, and I could have a clerk write you the list in a fortnight. With that list you can go into a store, find a brass ring, and put a thumb in it and know what it should read. I have been in your store. There is a plate in it, and the board is a hand's width off what that plate should give on a five-and-nine head."

"Which is a plate of the wrong bore."

"Which is a plate of the wrong bore," said Iven Marrow, "and that is where the part of your head goes, and I am going to tell you where it goes in about a minute and you are not going to like it."

He turned the can round on the table.

"The third thing is a bench. The Guild trains at Bexhill, in a shed, on Tuesday mornings, and it is not a secret and there is no cipher to it. A boy comes at seventeen and does not miss a Tuesday, and he is a sluicewright at twenty-six. It is nine years of Tuesday mornings, and that is all it is, and that is the whole of what I have that you have not got, because you have a head reading and a string and a chalk and a chamber, and I have four men who can go down to a gate bed in cold water in the dark and set it, and three of them could not do what you did at a shop in Vellum on the eleventh of this month with a lamp and a press-glass.

"Eleven weeks at Bexhill. You will be given work nobody wants. You will be the fourth man on a bench and you will be the one they leave to do the ones that do not come out. And in a year I will be asking you to check a setting I have made myself and I will be annoyed about it and I will do it in front of people, and that is how it is done, and that is the only part of it that matters."

"You have offered me a great deal," said Tarin Rusk.

"I have offered you less money, all the records in the basin, and eleven weeks of a shed," said Iven Marrow. "And they are in that order of what I would want if I were thirty-three. Now the price. I have made this offer four times in nine years and have never dressed it up, and none of the four before this one got written down, and two of those men are still working for me, and one of them has a district's water supply in a report because I wrote it in."

"Go on."

"The report goes in. It will be the most detailed document about that chamber that has ever existed and it will be in four buildings, and it will say what is in the store and what is on the board and what is under the flagstones and what came up the well in a kitchen floor, and it will say the name of the works, and the name of the settlement, and the number of persons in it, and the year the well was gauged and by whom.

"And the blue steps will not be in it.

"That is the price and there is no way to pay it in instalments, and I have thought about whether there is, and there is not."

He said the reason then, and he said it in about four minutes, and it was the best argument Tarin Rusk had heard in a month and it was entirely true.

"Three reasons, and all three of them are good, and I have never pretended otherwise.

"One. The Guild maintains works. That is in our articles and it is what we are for, and a route is a liability. A report that puts a district's water supply in connection creates a service, and a service creates maintenance, and I have got four hundred works in this basin and no money, and if I take on a service to a place I cannot find I cannot pay, then in four years the first man to say that route to anybody is me, and the district will say *the Guild maintained it for four years and then let it go*, and that will be true, and I will have no answer to it.

"Two. A service is a bargain between two named parties, and one of you is not named. I have watched a district lose a fight in a room because the other party in the room was a firm and not a person. You are not named, and a party who cannot be named cannot be given anything, and I am not able to hand you what I have not got a counterparty for.

"And three, and this is the one I would like you to take seriously, because I have been wrong about the other two before.

"A district's water supply on a schedule is a thing that can be closed. I have put a road on a schedule. I have watched that road taken off it eight years later by a man in an office who had never walked it, on a piece of paper, in a morning, and nobody has walked it since. You have been in this basin four years and I have been in it thirty-one and I have never once in my life seen a thing go onto a schedule and stay there, and the things that stay there are the things nobody is asking about. You want to be on a schedule so that you cannot be removed. Every schedule I have ever seen is a removal waiting for a reason."

He stopped and drank the tea and made a face about it, and Tarin Rusk, who had had no taste in his own since the seventh of the month, watched him do that and said nothing about it and was not going to tell that to a stranger at eight in the evening for anything.

"So there is the price," said Iven Marrow. "The blue steps is not in the report. And I am not going to soften it by telling you the district would find out, because it would not, not for a year, and by then it would be in a schedule you cannot get off without asking. And I am not going to tell you I could not find it, because I could find it in eleven days and I have a man who could find it in four, and I have not sent him, and that is not loyalty, it is a piece of paper, and a piece of paper can be had for money."

---

The lamp guttered twice, and Marrow looked at the small book beside the lamp and did not touch it, and then he said:

"Say no, and go down the hill, and do not write to me about it, because I have four men who have written to me about it and all four of them wanted something afterwards. Just say it and go."

Tarin Rusk sat on a packing case in a store above a chamber of water with eleven houses on top of it and had about ninety seconds to say it, and what he had was not the four reasons.

"The first three are good," he said. "I have been arguing with all three of them for two days and I have not beaten one of them, and I am not going to insult you by pretending otherwise, because a man who tells me that being put on a schedule is how you stop being removable, after thirty-one years in this basin, would be talking, and I have known what I would say to a man who was talking.

"Here is why, and it is one sentence and it is not mine.

"On the twelfth day of this month a woman of seventy-one at the top of a lane in my district wrote a letter and put a question in it, and the question is whether a district with no row may ask a settlement with no row for water. It went up the cattle track in a bag on the night of the fourteenth and it is in a warden's office now and nobody has answered it, and I do not know the answer, and I have an idea what my answer would be, and I am not going to have my idea recorded in a piece of paper by a stranger before the woman has had it.

"And that is the whole of it, and it comes down to this. If the report names Ashgate and not the blue steps, then in four years the only people in this basin who can ask that settlement for anything are the men who own it, and there is a settlement up that hill of thirty-four people with no row and no keeper and a well that a woman has kept for sixteen years out of her own kitchen floor, and it will never be able to ask anybody for anything, because asking needs a row, and a row is exactly what a document like yours does not give it.

"And I have a rule and I have had it since the sixth of the month before and I am going to say it out loud, because I say rules out loud, which is one of the faults I have and Doctor Sen has one as well.

"Nobody's memory goes on a piece of paper without their answer. And a report is a piece of paper. And so is a lease, and so is a register, and so is a plate, and so is a schedule entry, and I have a stone at the top of that lane with a name cut on it on the seventh of the month before and a face that has stayed blank because my hand will not seat, and a second stone a hundred and eighty-one years old under a chamber with the same blank face on it, and I am not going to be the man who writes a district out of a document and calls it procedure."

"Would you be on the other side of the table," said Iven Marrow. "If the district had a row."

"Yes."

"Then you are not refusing me on a rule," said Iven Marrow. "You are refusing me on a schedule entry, and that is worth more to me than a rule, and I am going to write it down, and I am going to write it down correctly, which is: *applicant declines the exchange on the ground that a named route is the condition of a service, and asks that a name be put to a district before any instrument is drawn.*"

"That is what I asked for on the twenty-second of the month before and I was refused in four grounds and one of the grounds was correct."

"Then it is a good ground," said Iven Marrow, "and I would like to know which of the four it was, and I am not asking now, and I will ask when it stops being a wound."

---

He did not press. He picked up the lamp and stood up, and then he sat down again, because there was one thing left and he was going to do it whether Tarin Rusk wanted it or not.

"I am going to be wrong about one of those three things and I am not going to argue it with you tonight," he said, "because you have come up that hill twice in four days and you have not slept properly in any of them and you are not able to hear a thing said to you at nine at night by a man you met on Tuesday, and I have not got an evening to spare in the next month, and I am not going to spend it losing.

"Which one. Second. I am wrong that a party who cannot be named cannot be given anything. I have believed that for twenty years and I have watched it be the reason a village got nothing, twice, and both times a man with a loud mouth and a good lawyer did it, and the village got nothing, and I wrote the letter afterwards, and the letter did not put water in a bucket. So I hold that rule and I have been on the wrong side of it twice and I would like somebody in the world to be able to correct me on it, and there is nobody in the Guild who can, and I have been waiting a long time.

"And I am not going to say the rest of it, because the rest of it is *and then I will change my own practice, and I have three hundred men who will want to know why a sluicewright from a terrace in the lower fold has changed the articles*, and there is no version of that sentence where it does not sound like a man asking for something, and you would be right to hear it as one.

"The offer does not expire. It is not going to be held against you and it is not going to be mentioned to your office, because your office has not asked me a question and I have not gone looking for one. There is a Guild seal on it and I have made it once and I will make it again, and the man who made the first one is me, and he will not enjoy making the second one, and he will make it."

He put his hand on the small book on the table and pushed it four inches across the wood towards Tarin Rusk.

"That is not the counting-house," he said. That is the bench book out of the shed at Bexhill. It is a working book. It is meant to be written in by whoever is at the bench, which is the whole of how the Guild teaches and the reason the Guild is worth anything at all. Nothing in it is restricted and nothing in it is a record and nothing in it is for sale, and I am not asking you for anything for it, and if I put it in a report you can take it back and I would deserve to be told about it.

"Anything I know, I will teach you in a shed. Anything I hold, I will sell you. That is the difference and I have never pretended it is a small one, and you have just spent a fortnight refusing to take the second half off a man, and you were right, and I would be a poor teacher if I could not see that you were right."

Tarin Rusk said thank you, and said he would think about it, which was not true, and Marrow said goodnight to him in a way that had no expectation in it whatever.

He took it down the mill road at about half past nine and put it inside his coat against the bad shoulder, and he was half a mile down the slope when he stopped, because a man who has been given something for nothing by a stranger at eight in the evening has to decide on the hill whether he is being given it for nothing, and there is no way of deciding that on a hill, and so he went back up.

Marrow had the door of the store open and the lamp still on the table, and he looked up and said, "Which part."

"The part where you are wrong," said Tarin Rusk. "The second one. I have thought about it for half a mile and I am not able to argue it and I am not able to accept it, and I am not going to be able to for some weeks, and I would like to say so while it is still a thing that is bothering me rather than something I have decided."

"That," said Iven Marrow, "is the first useful thing anybody in this basin has said about it in nine years, and I am not going to write it down, because if I write it down then next year a man in a counting-house can point at it and say the Guild has conceded a principle, and you did not say it for me. You said it because you could not get it out from under your own feet, and that is the only way anybody ever says a true thing, and I have watched thirty-one years of men say it the other way."

Tarin Rusk took the book off the table.

They walked to the head of the mill road and watched the man with the level pack his things. The Guild team went down the hill at ten o'clock with a full satchel of ledger and an empty one of anything else, and there was a bench book in a coat inside a bad shoulder on the same road.
