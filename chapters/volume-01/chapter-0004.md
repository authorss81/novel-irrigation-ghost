# Chapter 0004 — The Weight of a Leaf

Vellum was dry the way a bone is dry, and that was the entire reason the record city sat on that terrace instead of anywhere a person would rather live. Two hundred years of people had moved their lawsuits and their land claims and their dead into this shed of stone vaults, because the stone sweats in the wet season and the paper does not, and paper is the only thing in the basin that cannot be argued with.

Mara Quill had a bench in the second room of the Municipal Memory Office, under the north light, and the north light in Vellum was the good light, and the good light was the only thing in the building that was free.

The volume in front of her was the Ashgate maintenance volume, folio eleven of a series of nineteen, and it had been re-bound in the year before last, and the Office's own practice was to re-bind each folio once a generation, and to certify it, and to enter the certification in the requisition book, and to send the certified copy out to whatever body of people had a lawful reason to ask a question about Ashgate. Which, since the works had been sealed, was nobody at all, and the copy went to the upper shelf of the third press, where it had been since she started.

She had forty-one volumes to clear before the quarter turned, and the quarter had a review attached to it, and the review had an arithmetic attached to it, and the arithmetic was somebody else's problem. Hers was forty-one.

The work was: unback the spine, lift the leaves, clean them in their order with a soft brush and no water, lay them in a press under weight, rebind with a new spine of tawed hide, and certify that the sequence and the contents of the volume had been examined and were as they appeared to be.

That last clause was the entire content of her job, and Mara Quill had never once in eleven years signed it on a book she had not personally turned every leaf of.

She turned every leaf of folio eleven. It took her the morning, because she did not rush the middle, where the pressure readings were, and because the volume was written on both sides and the older half of it in a hand she had to keep adjusting to.

The pressure logs began in the year the works were new. They were in columns: date, gate, state, head in metres to two places, keeper's mark. She read the first eight leaves of logs the way you read a footpath, automatically, because after the first hundred lines a pressure log stops being numbers and becomes a picture of somebody's patience.

*head 5·71. gate shut. keeper's mark.* *head 5·74. gate shut.* *head 5·70.* *head 5·77.* *head 5·69.* *head 5·72.* *head 5·73. gate shut.* *head 5·74.*

A hundred and eighty years of a reservoir sitting at five and seven-tenths of a metre, and the number never once changed enough to matter, and a man writing it down in a shed at the top of a mountain, in the middle of the afternoon, for a hundred and eighty years.

The gate was shut for all of it. That was the thing that made the volume dull. An intake gate that stays shut for a hundred and eighty years and the head never moves is not a mystery, it is a mountain with a lid on it.

Then the leaves stopped agreeing with each other.

Mara felt it before she had any explanation for it, the way you feel a stair that is not where you left it. She had the leaf up on the palm of her left hand and her thumb under the edge, and her thumb said no, and she looked at the leaf and the leaf looked back at her, being a leaf.

It was a hair's difference. She would not have caught it in a stack of unrelated documents. She caught it in a bound volume in sequence, because the leaves in folio eleven were all the same stock: laid, cream, a chain line every twenty-two millimetres, and a watermark in the shape of a stepped tower that the Vellum mill had stopped cutting into its paper some time in the previous century.

The leaf in her hand had no watermark at all.

She set it down. She did not touch it again for a moment, which was a discipline she had learned the hard way, and then she picked up the balance from the corner of the bench, which was a small brass thing with a pan on each end and a needle that could be read to a half-grain, and she weighed it against the leaves on either side of it.

It was lighter by rather more than a leaf of the same size should be. It was lighter by about the amount you would expect from a sheet that had been made for a different purpose, or a different year, or by a different hand with a different idea of how much pulp a page should contain.

She wrote nothing. She measured it with the rule: the ruled width was a sixteenth under. She wrote that down, in pencil, in the margin of her own working sheet, with the time beside it, because the first rule of the work is that you may write anything you like on your own working sheet and nothing at all on a volume.

Then she turned the leaf over.

The back of it was blank. The back of it had always been blank; she had turned it herself twenty minutes ago. And it was not blank, because the light coming across the bench at that hour came in under the leaf from the side, and something on the far face threw a shadow onto this one.

She reached for the raking lamp.

What came up out of the paper was not ink. It was the shape of writing, pressed into the fibre hard enough to have gone through: a row of short strokes, a longer stroke, a dot, and after it a second column, and after that the unmistakable shape of a date written the way every clerk in the basin wrote dates, which was the year first and then the month, because they had all been taught the same in the same kind of room.

It was mirrored. She turned the leaf over again and read it the right way round on the underside, which is how a pressure shadow has to be read, and she wrote out what she could make of it in her own hand on the working sheet, and her hand was not steady, and she let it not be steady, and did not pretend to anybody that a clean hand had anything to do with a right one.

> *ASHGATE INTAKE, MAIN WHEEL, LEVEL FOUR GALLERY*
> *GATE SHUT.*
> *HEAD 5·8_*
> *BEFORE THE NINEFOLD. KEEPER'S MARK — [illegible]*

And then, on the same line, at the far right, in a different pressure and a different hand: *maintained.* One word, hard, twice underlined, driven into the paper hard enough to be the strongest thing on the leaf.

She sat quite still for a while.

A gate, shut. A head on it, five point eight and something. A hundred and eighty-one years ago or thereabouts, the year before the flood, the year when men were writing down the readings they were taking *because* they had a reason to write them down, and then somebody had cut the leaf out and put a different leaf in, and the different leaf was lighter by a measurable amount and came from a mill that had given up its watermark and had no chain lines at all.

And underneath the original writing, on the replacement, the word *maintained* — written by somebody who wanted a later reader to know that this leaf was not an oversight.

She held the two leaves side by side and thought about what she actually knew, which was: a page had been taken out of a bound volume and a different page had been put in its place, and the taking and the putting had both been done by a person who had known how to do them properly, and had then written *maintained* underneath so that the next conservator would understand that the substitution was authorised.

There was no authorisation here. That was the part she could not get past. The Office did not do this. The Office had a method, which was to leave the leaf in and add a marginal note, and the method existed because in one hundred and ninety years four volumes had been found with leaves gone and no note, and the note had been introduced afterward, and the four had never been explained, and the note is the only reason the Office can still say it keeps a record.

So somebody had not used the method. Somebody had known the shape of the method well enough to counterfeit its authority, and had done it in a folio that had sat on an upper shelf in a dry stone building for a year and a half, in a building with two hundred people in it, and had got away with it.

And the head on the shadow was 5·8 something, and she had eight pages in front of her saying that the reservoir had never been above 5·7 since the year it was built, and the gate had been shut the whole time, and now the Water Office had asked for a *defined* head on a gate the record says was shut, and the answer to that question was on a piece of paper that no longer existed except as a bruise.

She went and got the certification stamp, and looked at it, and put it back in the drawer.

She wrote the entry in the requisition book in her own hand instead, in the column reserved for volumes not certified, which she had signed perhaps nine times in eleven years.

> *Vol. XI, Ashgate maintenance. Sequence examined. One leaf substituted: different stock, no watermark, reduced width by 1/16 in., underweight against neighbours. Pressure shadow of removed writing on the reverse, showing an intake head at the main wheel, gate state shut, and the word "maintained" in a second hand. Volume NOT certified. Discrepancy entered under my hand, M. Quill, this date. Request: production of the removed leaf, or a copy of the entry authorising its removal.*

Then she signed it a second time, below, because the form wanted the certifier's mark and she was not certifying, and the second signature said *M. Quill, not certifying, on the grounds stated.*

The day clerk came at two with the cart for the volumes she had finished, and there were none on the cart, because she had finished nothing.

"You've done one," the clerk said.

"I've done one."

"Nine leaves to the next press and you haven't started." He looked at the requisition book, and then, with the particular carelessness of a man who has never read a provenance note in his life, he said, "Kreft's going to want a word about your hours. He's been saying the second room isn't a workshop."

"Tell Mr Kreft that the second room is where the quarter's backlog is," Mara said, "and that a volume which cannot be certified is not a volume finished."

He took the empty cart away.

She worked until the lamps came on, and then she worked for another hour after that, because her own rule — the one she had written down herself eleven years ago and had never been able to improve on — was that an unverified record must never decide a public route, and she wanted to know which way this one failed before somebody with a bigger budget and worse manners decided for her.

She got a better reading on the shadow by laying the leaf face down on the bench and holding the lamp almost flat, and then she tried it again with a small mirror set under the press-glass, and at the second reading she had the missing figure.

*5·89.*

She wrote it on her working sheet and looked at it for a long time, because it was not a difficult number and it was not an important number, and it was the number that was going to be the whole of the trouble.

In the morning she would be asked to certify, and she would not certify. And then the discrepancy would go up to Hollis Kreft, and Kreft would be quiet about it, because Kreft was a careful man and careful men do not shout, and then the requisition would go out to the Water Office in Cresset with the exhibited figure attached, and Cresset would look at it and try to decide whose fault it was to explain a head of 5·89 metres at a gate that their own book says has been shut since before the flood.

Somebody in Cresset was going to have to answer, and Cresset had sixty engineers, and it always picked the one who had been nearest.

The leaf lay on the bench under the lamp with its ghost still in it, five point eight nine, written by a dead man, pressed into a page by a dead man, and the page itself gone.
