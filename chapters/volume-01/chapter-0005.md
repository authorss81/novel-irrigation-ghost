# Chapter 0005 — Two Professionals, One File

The Memory Office in Vellum had sent for him, which was a phrase Mara Quill had always liked. Nobody in Vellum sent for anybody. Vellum received, catalogued, and declined to be hurried, and it was a considerable event when it reached out across a day and a night of mountain road to a man it had never met and told him to be in a particular room at a particular hour.

Tarin came in with a blister on his heel, an overnight bag with a bar in it, and a day and a night of not sleeping, and he had decided on the road that he was going to lie by omission and get out.

He got as far as the second room.

"You're late," said the woman at the bench.

"Three hours. The coach at Ferrow had no water and then it had the wrong water."

"Sit there. Not at the bench, at the table, the bench is a bench and not a table." She did not look up. She was reading a leaf by raking light with one hand flat on the press-glass, and she had the stillness of somebody who spends eleven hours a day making a single judgement and does not care to be interrupted between two of them. "Tarin Rusk, senior flow engineer, Cresset Water Office, four years in the lower terrace district, appointed to answer requisition eighteen of this month against folio eleven. You have an objection to any part of that?"

"No."

"You have one anyway. Sit down and have it after you have read this, because I want you to read it before you start managing me."

He sat. He read it. It took him a long time, longer than the folio, because the woman let him get to the end twice.

The requisition carried a certified discrepancy, and the discrepancy carried a leaf.

He had known on the road, from the way the notice was worded, that somebody had found something physical. What he had not known was that it was a leaf, and that the leaf had been cut out and replaced, and that the pressure shadow underneath it held a head of 5·89 metres at a gate that the same page recorded as shut.

He came to the last line of the shadow, the one word driven hard and underlined, and he read it twice.

"Maintained," he said.

"By a second hand," said Mara Quill, "on the same page, in a heavier pressure, at the right-hand margin, and it is the strongest impression on the leaf. Whoever replaced this page wanted a later reader to know the substitution was authorised. There is no authorisation in this building. Our method for a withdrawn leaf is to leave it in and add a marginal note, and we adopted that method after four volumes were found with leaves gone and no note, and the four have never been explained to anybody's satisfaction including mine."

"Was the note the method, or was the note the explanation?"

"Both," she said. "Which is the problem with method."

He set the requisition down. "The head is 5·89."

"5·89, metres, to two places, which is this Office's notation and was the intake's notation in the year before the flood. The shadow is clear on the gate state and the date and the last two figures. The figure before the decimal I took twice on two lamps and I will not swear to a second attempt under a better lamp, and I will swear to five and eight."

"Five and eight nine," Tarin said.

The woman looked up for the first time.

"You have a notebook," she said.

He had a waxed field book with a blue cover and a bar of chalk in the loop and a page he had not shown anybody. He had carried it from the intake to the arch to the warden's hut to the coach at Ferrow with his thumb worn white on the corner, and the number was on the second page, written at eleven minutes past six in the morning with a hand that had not been thinking about anything but a float.

He opened it on the table and turned it round so it faced her and put his finger on the line.

*06:06. Ashgate intake, main wheel shut and chained, six years. Stilling tube live, pressure from below, source not gate. Head above gate seat 5·89 m. Floats pin at top stop, pulse on the tube ~2 a minute.*

Mara Quill read it twice as well. She read it the way she read leaves, which was slowly, and with a place kept clear in her attention for the possibility that she was being shown something chosen.

"You took this on the fourth," she said.

"The first. Six in the morning. Day one of the ration, the day it rained."

She put her hand flat on the table on either side of the notebook, which was a thing he recognised later as the beginning of an argument she intended to have properly.

"Then explain to me how a man who was on a sealed intake at six in the morning on the first has in his notebook the figure that was pressed into a leaf in this building before either of us was born."

"I can't."

"That," said Mara Quill, "is the first sentence anyone has said to me in six years that I have believed, and I want you to understand that it has cost you nothing and me the entire ground I had prepared to stand on."

The room was very quiet. Down the corridor somebody was calling for a second cart. Through the north window there was the low blue-white light off the terrace and the sound of Vellum being a place where nothing had ever once run downhill.

Tarin put his hands flat on the table too, because he was about to do the thing he had come here not to do.

"Then here is the rest of it," he said. "I did not put a number on that page. I did not know the page was there. There has been a head of five point eight nine on that gate for a hundred and eighty-one years, and on the first I went up to recalibrate a decommissioned float gauge and I found it and I unsealed the inspection gate with a bar at eleven minutes past six in the morning before the warden check, and the water came through the gallery and went uphill down the terrace overflow run, and I have not been able to shut it from the intake end because the head is above the gate seating and there is no crew up there and no authority."

He stopped there, because that was all of it, and because he had no idea what it was for.

Mara Quill sat back in her chair and put two fingers on the requisition and two fingers on his notebook and looked at the four inches between them.

"You have told the warden," she said.

"I gave a condition. I gave the head and the direction and the state of the gate. I did not write the cause in the box, and I want you to understand that I knew that was a choice, and that I made it, and that I would do it again in the same morning."

"You posted the notice."

"I posted it. Blank cause. It went on the warden's board addressed to no district, and Pell sent it up asking for a row to serve, and the commissioner's office returned it with a line saying no such row exists in the district schedule." He turned the notebook over and showed her the back of the last page. "I have been down to the end of that run. There is water in a courtyard and a row of houses and a cistern at a third, and the outfall that should take that water away has a plate of iron bolted over it with a bolt the size of my fist on it and no key in the city."

"That is not a route question," she said. "That is a complaint."

"That is a complaint *with* a route in it," he said. "I have a courtyard under four inches of water and a plate across a drain and fifteen days of cistern on forty thousand people, and I have a number in my book that matches a ghost on a page in your building, and I would like to know which of those is the lie, and I have a day and a night's travel and no authority and one bar."

Mara Quill was quiet for a moment. Then she stood, and unlocked the press, and took out the volume, and set it on the table between them, and turned to the substituted leaf without finding the page number, which told him she had done it a great many times.

"Look at the page that replaced it," she said. "Not the ghost. The page. Read it to me."

Tarin read it.

> *ASHGATE INTAKE, MAIN WHEEL, LEVEL FOUR GALLERY. Gate shut and dogged. Chamber void. No residual head. Maintenance not required. Superintendent's entry, 1st day of the dry season.*

"It is a clean leaf," he said.

"It is a leaf that says a shut gate has no head on it. It is on different stock, in a different sizing, from no mill this office has ever bought from, and there is a real head of 5·89 under it, at the same gate, in the same gallery, in the same units, to the same two places." She turned the book round and put her finger under the shadow. "That is not a lie about a number. A forger writes a number that suits him; this is the opposite. Somebody took a *true* number off the record and set a clean leaf in its place so the record could say the gate stood dry, and did it so well that the only surviving copy of the truth is an embossment in a piece of paper nobody would look at unless the paper itself were wrong."

"And the page above the shadow," he said. "Word for word."

"Word for word, and the date is the same, and the keeper's mark is the same, and *maintained* is in a second hand." Mara Quill took her fingers off the book. "So whoever put the false page in did not invent a chamber. He removed a true reading and left a page saying there was nothing there, and then he wrote *maintained* underneath in his own hand, which is a thing a man writes when he expects to be caught and wants somebody to have to answer for it."

"He wanted a question asked by somebody who could not answer it."

"Or," she said, "he wanted it asked by somebody who could."

The two of them looked at each other across the table for the first time, and it was not friendly, and neither of them was pretending it was.

"You think I did it," Tarin said.

"I think a new man at a sealed works, with a notebook of measurements he made himself, has produced a figure identical to one that was in this building a hundred and eighty-one years ago. There are three possible men in that story, engineer. The man who invented the figure and came a very long way to have it printed in my paper. The man who took it out. And the man who is the only one of the three who can be asked to explain, and who came here this morning, and who told me the first true thing anybody has said to me since the discrepancy went up."

"And which do you think I am?"

"I think you are the third," said Mara Quill, "and I think you are going to be treated as the first, and I have watched this building spend eleven years being correct about paper and useless about people, and I would like to help you and I do not yet know that I can."

She closed the book, and then, oddly, she looked past him at the wall.

"Wait," she said.

He followed her look. There was nothing on the wall. She got up and went through the door at the back, which was the stair down to the lower room, and he followed her because there was nothing else to do.

The lower room held stone. Not much of it: a rack of record stones, a shelf of sluice keys that ought to have been returned years ago, a long box of bell wires, and the smell of a dry cellar that had never in its life been wet. The rack was made for eight. It had nine stones in it and a paper slip over the front of the lowest shelf that said *pending disposal* in a clerk's hand with a date five and a half years old.

"The works were sealed six years back," Mara said. "When a works is sealed, the record stone comes off the wall and comes here, because we do not leave a name in stone outside a structure that has been closed. Six years is a long time to leave nine stones on a pending slip."

She crouched and pulled the forward-most stone out from the row, and set it on the floor, and it was the size of a bread loaf and grey-green with lichen that had never had a chance to grow.

"It came down the mountain on a cart," she said. "It is cracked along the diagonal. There is no wet mark on it anywhere. It is the dullest object in this building and I have looked at it once, when it arrived, and wrote *inscribed, undamaged save transport fracture* and moved on."

Tarin crouched in front of it.

The name on it was cut twice.

That was the first thing. A name on a record stone is cut once, and the chisel is set, and the letters are cut and left, because a stone is a slow document and a slow document is not improved by being redone. This stone said ASHGATE MAIN INTAKE, and the letters had been cut once, and then the same letters had been cut again, shallower, in front of the first set, so that the stone read two names a half-inch out of register, one behind the other, the way a person reads a thing through a dirty window.

Underneath the name were two lines, and the second of the two was not a rule, it was a date, and there was a third short line with a witness hand at the foot of it, and the whole of the lower half of the stone was worn to a soft grey nothing, because a cart wheel had gone over it once on a mountain road.

Tarin put his hand flat on it.

He had put his hand on ten thousand record stones in eleven years of service and had never once got anything out of any of them that was not in the stone.

He got something out of this one.

It came up through the palm of his hand and up the inside of his forearm, and it was not pain and it was not a voice, and the nearest thing to a description he could have given anybody afterwards was that the stone had a depth that the eye could not get at, because the eye sees a face and the hand was being shown the inside of the face. There was a line in it. There was a line running the whole length of the stone from the cut name down into the worn grey, and the line was wet, and it was not wet the way stone gets wet, and it was not going anywhere. It was there, and it had been there, and it was going the way a road goes, which is to say that it had a far end that somebody had to be responsible for.

And then the thing said:

> **The Gauge:** One route remembers. It asks for a witness.

Tarin Rusk took his hand off the stone and sat back on his heels on the floor of a stone cellar in Vellum and did not say anything for some time.

Underneath the words there was something else. Not more words. A pressure, like a room with more air in it than the room was built for, or a hold on a rope with somebody's weight at the far end of it. He could not tell, sitting there on a cellar floor, whether that was a second thing saying something or the first thing going on behind his own ears, and he had been a day and a night on a mountain road with no water and no sleep, and there was a perfectly good mundane explanation available for a man in his condition. He reached for it and held on to it.

*One route*, he thought. The stone has two sets of letters and three lines and a witness hand and he had counted, in the ordinary way, at least two things that wanted saying to him, and the thing had said *one*.

And it had not told the water to ask. It had told the *water*. Water does not need a witness. What was in that stone was not a channel asking for a person; it was a piece of paper with a keeper's mark on it, in a dry cellar, five and a half years past a pending slip, and it was not the water that had been waiting, it was the *record* — a thing that had been waiting a hundred and eighty-one years for somebody to be in a room with it.

Or he was a man who had not slept, in a cellar, in Vellum, at the end of a day and a night of mountain road, feeling the inside of a stone because his hand was cold and the stone was not.

That was the whole of what he had. He could not make the water go anywhere with it. He could not open the gate from a cellar in Vellum, and there was nothing whatever in that information about a courtyard in Cresset where a cistern was filling to a third and a plate of iron was taking the weight of a mountain.

Mara Quill was watching him from the other side of the stone.

"What did you do?" she said.

"I put my hand on it."

"That is not an answer to the question I asked, and you know it, and I am going to ask it again in a moment, and I would rather you had a moment to decide." She sat down on the floor across from him, which was not a thing she had been doing on this bench for eleven years. "In this building's older usage, when somebody can make a route answer, that is called a reader, and what they are given is called the Gauge. It is in the register of a practice that we no longer have anybody for. I have never seen one work. I have read four accounts of one working and three of the accounts are worthless and the fourth is a story told by a man who wanted a pension."

"Those four accounts," Tarin said. "Do they say what the Gauge says?"

"No," said Mara Quill. "They say the reader was told something. I have never in my life been told anything by a piece of paper, and I have signed a great many pieces of paper."

She let that sit, and then she said, in a completely different voice: "You have read a stone. Say the number out loud and tell me what it is doing, and then we will know what you are, because I have run out of categories."

"It is not doing anything," Tarin said. "There is a line in that stone and I can feel where it goes, and it is wet, and it is real, and there is nothing in it that will open a gate or move a foot of water. I have a courtyard in Cresset with four inches in it and a plate across the drain and fifteen days of cistern, and this thing under my hand in this cellar cannot put a bucket down that has gone dry." He put his hands on his knees. "And it asked for a witness. Whatever it was asking, it was not asking me. I have no standing here, no office, no crew, and a day and a night of travel already behind me. I am the wrong man for a witness and I know it."

"Then who is the right one?"

That was the question, and he had been carrying it down from the mountain since the first, and he had been carrying it down for a day and a night with a growing certainty that he already knew the answer and did not like it.

"You have said the volume is the only durable record of that works, and that a route has to be legible to be moved. I have known that since I was nineteen without ever once putting it in a sentence." He stood up and put the stone back in the rack in its place, because he had not asked to move it and would not. "The only people in this basin who have used that route, kept it, swept it, and argued about it for a hundred and eighty years live at the end of the run. And they are not in your catalogue, and not in the district schedule, and the warden's board says their outfall is plated and out of service with no keeper and no key, and my notice came back from the commissioner's office because there is no such row in the district schedule."

Mara Quill was looking at the slip that said *pending disposal*.

"The only witnesses to Ashgate live in a district that does not legally exist," he said. "How do I produce one of those to a register?"
