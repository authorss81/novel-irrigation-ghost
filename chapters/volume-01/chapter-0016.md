# Chapter 0016 — A Hundred And Four Names

The warrant was a printed sheet with five boxes and it was worth more to Mara Quill than anything she had held in eleven years of service, because it was the only document in the Marrow Basin that permitted her to stand in a yard in the lower terrace with a lamp and a good sheet of paper and be certain that she was there lawfully.

*Collation of fair copy against district original. Volume: Glassstep district survey, field book, with redaction folio tied in at the back. Fair copy: Vellum survey press, open access, sheet 214. District: the blue steps, lower terrace. Date: the twenty-eighth day of the month. Purpose: to establish whether the district original agrees with the fair copy as to household names and the schedule entry of their removal.*

Eleven words in the purpose box, because eleven words was what a purpose could hold, and a purpose that ran over eleven words in this building was a purpose that had begun to want something.

The coach put her down on the terrace road at half past eight in the morning with a lamp, a roll of good stock, a press-glass, a lens, a prepared sheet, and a warrant, and she walked the last mile and a half into a lane she had never walked and had known since she was eleven years old.

She had thought about this for four days and had got as far as three rules, and she wrote them on the back of her hand in the road with a pencil, because she was a woman who wrote things on the back of her hand.

*One. Do not ask for the book. Ask to look at it with them.*
*Two. Do not read a name into a public room unless the person it belongs to says it first.*
*Three. Do not leave this lane with anything in your hand that only you can read.*

The third rule was four days old and it was the reason she was here.

---

The fourth step of the blue steps was a door with a blue door, and Hanne Roeg opened it before Mara had finished knocking, which told her that the warrant was known, which told her that a woman with a stamped envelope had come up the hill on the twenty-second and told somebody in this lane what she had written in box five, and that the somebody had been the right person.

"You are the paper woman," said Hanne Roeg.

"I am a conservator with a warrant to collate a field book against a fair copy. It is one sheet and it takes most of a day and I do it here, in this yard, with the book on a table and anybody in the lane who wants to watch."

"Sit down on that step, because you are going to want to sit down before you finish that sentence." The old woman put her hand on the frame. "That book is not mine. I will tell you that first, because you have come to me expecting to have to argue and I would rather you did not waste the morning. The survey book goes to the house the surveyor lodged in, and the surveyor lodged in this house, and so it has been in this house since before the register, and it is *this lane's*, and there is a difference between the two of those, and you will not get it from a day here."

"I know the difference. It is why I did not ask you for the book. I asked you for the table."

---

That was how it went, and it took four hours, and the four hours were the argument.

Mara put the fair copy on the table under the awning, unrolled and weighted, and put the warrant beside it, and then did the thing that eleven years of a conservator's training is for, which was to stop talking and let a room look at a document. There were forty people in that yard by ten o'clock and eleven at eleven, and they stood round a trestle table and looked at two pieces of paper about themselves, and being allowed to look was the only thing Mara had that nobody else in this story had been given.

Then she read the difference aloud, both sides, in plain words, in the way she read things — the fair copy on the right, the field book on the left, and between them the whole of what the city had done to a lane in a single winter.

The fair copy said: *Sheet 214. Terrace row, blue steps, lower fold. Two hundred and thirty properties, of which two hundred and thirty retained. Schedule entry of the following date: consequential correction, terrace row removed from distribution schedule.*

The field book said: *Surveyed, the eleventh day of the fourth month. Two hundred and thirty properties numbered, of which one hundred and nine returned for closure, thirty-one returned for exclusion, and ninety retained. The list of the one hundred and forty so returned is begun in the folio tied at the back.*

The folio tied at the back, with a bootlace, after twenty-six years under a floor, was a single sheet with a hundred and four short horizontal strokes on it, which is thirty-six short of the hundred and forty the field book promises, and which is the first thing in twenty-six years that anybody in this lane has been in a position to notice.

---

The trouble began with the strokes, and it was a trouble Mara had predicted in the road and still could not have prepared for.

She got the lamp low and the light across the sheet and she looked at the ink for eleven minutes before she said anything, because in eleven years nobody had ever lied to her about a thing she could see and she did not intend to start the day by accusing.

"The sheet is foxed," she said. "The whole of it. Twenty-six years under a floor in a row that is damp in the winter, and the fibres have gone, and the ink has gone *into* the fibres — it has not sat on the paper, it has spread, so that the edge of a stroke at this end is not the edge of a stroke that was made. You cannot compare these to a hand. Not honestly. I would be doing a thing in front of you that I would spend the rest of my life being asked to defend, and I would rather not."

"So you cannot tell," said Marda Ollan, "whether it is the same man."

"I can tell you what I can tell you and I will not dress it up. I can tell you that it is the same *ink*, and the ink is court-supply, and the court-supply is issued in dated batches with the year and the batch stamped on the lid of the tin, and if the lid has gone the way the paper has gone then I am wrong about the date as well." She put the lens down. "I can tell you that the marginal reason is in the same abbreviated form the registry uses — *consequential correction* — and that the abbreviation is the same abbreviation, because it is the same office and the same century. I can tell you that it is one hand and not a hundred and four, and that the strokes were entered in a list without lifting the nib, and that a man being paid by the sheet makes them the same length, which is what we have here. I can tell you that the reason given is a *schedule* reason, and that it names a schedule, and that the schedule it names is the same schedule that took this row off the distribution list, and that a document on a table in a yard can now show that a district was removed from a water schedule by an entry that refers to itself."

"And you cannot tell us whether it is the same man who did the other thing."

"No."

"Well." Marda Ollan folded her arms. "Then what have you come down here for."

---

What Mara Quill had come down here for, and what she said next, was the truest thing she said in four days, and it took her a while to get to because she had to put it past the fact that a woman with a form on the twenty-second had told a warden's office where this lane's water came from, and had not known that the same name was in a ledger in Vellum.

"I have a pattern that only I can read," she said. "It is a drawing in my own ink in my own drawer and there is no copy of it anywhere and if I am hit under a cart on the Vellum road it is gone. That is not modesty. That is the fact about every piece of evidence in the world, and it is the reason this office is afraid of me, and it is the reason it published a finding without my name on it last Thursday, and I could not stop it and would not have wanted to."

She put her hand flat on the field book, which she had not done all morning, and which she had been told twice to ask before she touched.

"What I need is a *second* copy of the same fact, made by somebody else, out of the same book, in front of witnesses. Not a copy I make. A second copy, in a second hand, held by a second custodian, in a second building. If the Ashgate volume is ever locked in a room for twenty years and comes out into a hearing, and a man says *a conservator once said so*, then a hundred and twenty-six people in this lane holding a thing in their own hand is the difference between a rumour and a pattern, and the difference is the only thing I have ever wanted in my life."

"You want us to copy it," said Marda Ollan. "You want a hundred and twenty-six people in this lane to hold a piece of paper about a hundred and four names that they cannot now be found under."

"No," said Mara Quill. "I want two people to copy it, in their own hands, in their own ink, out of that book, in front of witnesses, and I want to certify both copies as made from the same original in my presence by the two named hands. And then the fact exists in this lane in a way it can be checked in this lane, by a person who is not me."

Hanne Roeg had come out and was sitting on the step.

"That is the whole trick, is it not," she said. "The second copy. You have taught me the whole trick in three sentences and I have been living inside it my whole life without knowing it had a name." She looked at the bootlace and the folio under it. "There is a problem and you have not noticed it, and I am going to point it out, and then you are going to have to decide what to do about it in front of a yard."

"Go on."

"That list has a hundred and four names on it," said Hanne Roeg, "and every one of them is a house on this lane or a house that was a house on this lane, and one of them is the house you are standing in. I have known since I was forty-five that my mother's name is on that sheet. I have never seen it. I know it the way you know a thing that everybody in a lane knows and nobody will say in a lane." The old woman's hands were flat on her knees. "You have come down here to ask a yard to agree that a hundred and four of them were struck out. And a yard cannot agree to that, because a yard is not a person, and there is nobody in this lane you can go and ask who has the standing to say yes on behalf of a house that has been struck off the sheet for twenty-six years. So what happens? Somebody stands up. Somebody says *that is mine*. And then you have a name, in a yard, in daylight, in front of forty people, on the record — a household's name, in a city where that household is on a stamped form in a registry as of Saturday."

"Yes," said Mara Quill.

"And that is not a thing you are asking me to be careful about. That is a thing I am going to have to decide about."

---

They argued for an hour and forty minutes, in the yard, in the sun, and the argument was not about principle. It was about arithmetic of the cruelest kind.

The children. There were nine with the marks and four in Yara Sen's book and the marks come back in three to five weeks in this weather, and there was no version of this lane's water supply that was not, at this moment, a single unanswered question with a plate under it.

Marda Ollan: "You can have the copy without the names. Count them, date it, put the margin on it, put the hand on it. You can certify a pattern. You cannot certify a house."

Mara Quill: "I can certify a claim about a house. I cannot check it. And an uncheckable claim about a hundred and four houses is what I have just spent four days refusing to be."

Old Marden, from the step: "Then make it checkable with one."

Hanne Roeg said it before anybody had argued for it, in the voice of somebody who has decided:

"The fourth step."

"Hanne—"

"The fourth step is mine. It is this house and it has been mine since my mother, and if the lane is going to have a name on the paper then it is going to be *a* name and not a hundred, because a hundred names in a yard is a bonfire and one name is a signature. I do not care that it is mine. I have been un-findable for seventy-one years and I signed a form on Saturday and I cannot be un-findable again, so being findable twice is not a catastrophe, it is arithmetic." She got up off the step, which took a while, and she was not shaky, she was precise. "You will read out one name. I will tell you it is mine if it is mine and I will not tell you if it is not, and you will stop, and you will write down that you stopped, and that is the record. And in fifty years a woman in this office with a lamp will be able to say: the fourth step was named aloud, in a yard, by the fourth step herself, in front of witnesses, on a date. That is checkable by me. Not by you. By me. And that is the only checkability that was ever any use to anybody on this lane."

Mara Quill wrote it down as it was said, which is the only thing a conservator is for.

---

She read the hundred and four strokes out of the folio, in order, in the yard, at twenty past one in the afternoon, with a witness on each side and the sun behind the paper, and it took about six minutes.

She read the count and the date and the schedule reference and the margin first, at length, and then she read the list.

On the eleventh stroke an old man who had been standing at the back of the yard for half an hour said, "Stop."

Mara Quill stopped.

"That one's mine," he said. "It is the second house. My mother's name. It has been my mother's name on a list for twenty-six years and I have never been able to say so out loud in this lane because saying so out loud is a thing you do when you have got something to lose, and I have had it for about four minutes now and I have got nothing left to lose, so."

"Thank you," said Mara Quill. "That is an identification made by a person present. I am not going to write the name down and I am going to write that down."

She wrote it on the certificate, in the terms she had been thinking about since the second of June, and read it aloud afterwards to the yard because she had learned that afternoon that a document nobody has heard read is a document half the lane believes is a lie.

> *One (1) person present, at the reading, identified a struck entry as the name of a household of this district. The conservator did not transcribe the name. The conservator cannot confirm the identification and has not attempted to. The identification is recorded because it occurred, and is not offered as evidence of the contents of the folio.*

The old man nodded at that and Hanne Roeg said, "That is the first thing a person from an office has ever put in writing in this lane that was about us and was not against us," and Mara Quill felt something go out of her that she had been carrying up the hill since the seventeenth.

At the fourteenth stroke the fourth step's name came, and it was the twelfth house from the top, and Hanne Roeg said "mine" in a voice like a door shutting, and Mara Quill stopped, and wrote it down, and there were forty people in a yard watching an old woman pay for a piece of paper.

After the twenty-seventh stroke nobody was able to identify anything, because a yard can only do so much in the sun, and the rest was read silently and quickly to the end, and it was one hundred and four.

---

The work took until four o'clock: the collation against the fair copy, sheet by sheet, property by property, ninety retained against ninety retained, which is nine thousand separate little agreements between a fair copy and an original made in a winter a century and a quarter ago by men who expected to be right. Mara Quill checked all of it in the open, in a yard, and twice somebody in the crowd told her she had a house number wrong and she was wrong both times.

Then she made the two tracings.

She made hers first, at one to one, in her own ink, on good stock, with the batch and the date and the margin and the stroke characteristics and the schedule reference, and no names beyond the two that had been identified in the yard by the people they belonged to, which she entered as identifications and not as transcriptions.

Then she gave the second sheet to Hanne Roeg and the second pen and said: "You do it. I will read the sheet out and you write it, and if you cannot see the sheet I will not tell you what is on it, because the point is not that you copy my copy."

The old woman wrote the hundred and four strokes as a list of numbers, because a list of numbers is a list and a list of names is a different kind of object, and the numbers were the house numbers in the lane's own sequence, and she took four hours over it, and her hand got worse toward the end and did not get worse in a way that made it a different hand.

At the foot of it she wrote, in the space Mara left her: *Copied by the fourth step, of this lane, out of the survey book of this lane, on the twenty-eighth day of the month, in the presence of M. Quill, conservator, and of the persons of this lane.*

Mara Quill certified both, in one hand, in one sentence, in front of the yard, and the certification said that both tracings were made from one original, in one room, in one sitting, by two named hands, and that where the two tracings agreed they were two independent attestations of the same thing, and that where they did not agree the original was the authority and not either of them.

"Well," said Marda Ollan, looking at the two sheets. "There is a thing in this lane nobody can take."

"There is," said Mara Quill, "and I want to be careful, because I have watched people do exactly what I am about to do. That is a second attestation. It is *not* proof. Two hands in one room in one afternoon can be two hands in one room in one afternoon because one of us is lying, and the only thing that makes it better than a rumour is that it is checkable *here*, by somebody in this lane, out of the book, at any time. It is not worth more than that. It is worth exactly that, and that is more than I had on Friday."

---

The Tarin Rusk business happened at half past five, on the dispensary step, in front of about nine people, and neither of them asked for privacy, and he only understood afterwards that this was the whole of what the day had been teaching her.

He had been in the yard since two o'clock and had said almost nothing, and she had known he was there the entire time, and when the certificate was signed she came down off the step and he came off the wall and they stood in a lane with the lamps being lit in it.

"You have made a mess of my month," she said.

"I have made an argument for a channel off a sealed gate in a yard full of people with two charges against me."

"Yes. You have." She had the roll of stock under her arm and she did not put it down. "Listen to me before you say anything generous, because you are about to. Two copies of a pattern, and neither of them is you, and I still cannot get the Ashgate volume out of a locked room to put them beside the only other document that matters. I have made this lane *checkable* and I have not made it *countable*, and those are different things and one of them is mine to give and one of them is not."

"Then tell me what you need and I will do it."

"You cannot do it. That is what I want to say next, and I am saying it because if I do not say it now then in a month you will have been let into this and it will have been the worst thing either of us has done." She turned and looked at him properly. "What I need is the source. Not the head. Not the flow. The *source* — the gate, the works, the document, the thing that says a head of five point eight nine metres is standing behind a wheel that this city has recorded as shut for six years. That thing exists. It is in the strong room in Vellum with my initials on the marker, and it is restricted under rule nine, which says that an unverified record may not decide a public route."

"Which means that the district's legal existence and the one document that would support it are the same object."

"I did not say that."

"You did not say it out loud. You said it the other way round, twice, which is the same sentence, and I am an engineer, and it is a very old mistake to think that saying a thing in the right words makes it a different thing."

"Then do not write it down."

"I am not going to write it down without you seeing it."

"Write it and do not file it," she said, "and I will not pretend that a sentence in your book is a record, and I have spent this afternoon teaching a yard the difference between the two, so you will understand that I am not being cold, I am being consistent, and you are welcome to find that irritating."

---

They made it on the step, in about ten minutes, and it was not a reconciliation, and it was not warmth, and it was the most adult thing that had happened between them.

"Right," she said. "If you are going to be a man who tells people the source and the head and the branch and the plate, then I am going to be a woman who tells people the ink and the batch and the margin, and neither of us can do the other's job, and both of us are going to be tempted to do the other's job at about four in the morning. So."

"So."

"Five things, and if you agree to them I will give you the pattern in a form you can hand to a man who is not me, and I will not do it in my own shorthand, I will do it in a way a clerk could check, which you will hate, because it will be slower than being right."

"Agreed."

"One. Neither of us uses the other's method in public without telling the other first. You do not turn a pressure figure into an accusation. I do not turn a nib cut into a verdict."

"Agreed."

"Two. Nothing goes on paper with one name on it. Either of us." She said it flatly. "That one is mine and you are going to work out in about four seconds why, and when you have worked it out you are going to say so, because I would rather be caught than let it stand."

"Four seconds," said Tarin Rusk. "You have one copy of everything, in your hand, in your drawer, and no custodian, because you decided on purpose on the ninth of the month that nobody should be able to check you, and that was the right decision on the ninth of the month and it has just become the single largest hole in this entire business. I am not scolding you. I am saying that you fixed it today, in a yard, in front of forty people, and I want it on the record that I noticed."

"Thank you," said Mara Quill, "and that is the closest thing to a compliment anybody has given me in six years, and I am going to pretend it was a professional observation and I am going to be right to."

"Three. Neither of us takes a private memory off anybody without asking that person, in words, and getting an answer that is not silence. Including our own. I have already refused to do it once this month and I want it written down so that the second time I am tempted there is a piece of paper in a conversation rather than a good argument in my own head at one in the morning."

"Agreed. Agreed, and it is not a small thing, and I have watched a man work out that a private memory is not a public record in the dark."

"Four. If one of us is charged, the other of us does not produce the other's evidence without telling them first. That is the one that matters and I am putting it in the middle where it is inconvenient."

"Agreed."

"Five. We disagree in public when we have to, and the disagreement goes into the record, and neither of us is allowed to be the man who is only ever reasonable."

"That one is not going to be difficult for either of us," said Tarin.

---

They did not shake on it, and she was packing her roll on the step when he said the last thing.

"You have not got what you wanted either."

"No."

"And I have not got what I wanted. I have a stone with one name on it, four witnesses who will not stop a route, and a channel with a head on it, and none of those are the same as a district."

"No," said Mara Quill. "They are not. And I want it understood that this is not friendship and I am not going to be one of the women who gets fond of a man and then goes soft on a provenance, and if you tell anybody in Vellum that I said that I will deny it in writing."

"I will not tell anybody in Vellum."

"You will tell the warden's office on Saturday, because the warden is going to ask you on Saturday how a restricted folio is going to help a district that has no row, and you are going to be the man who is standing in front of a warden's office, and you are going to lie to him, or you are going to do something worse and be honest at a length. Neither of those is a thing I can help you with." She shouldered the roll. "Good night, Rusk. Mind the plate."

---

She walked the mile and a half back up the terrace road in the dark with a roll of good stock under her arm and four and a half miles of thinking in front of her, and by the time she got to the crown of the road she had done what she always did, which was to establish the provenance of a decision before she had committed to it.

The certificate was signed at four o'clock in a yard in front of sixty people, and it was in her bag, and it said four things: that a redaction folio tied into the field book of the Glassstep survey had been written in court-supply ink of a dated issue; that the same issue, on the same abbreviated form, with the same institutional correction wording, was recorded against the Ashgate maintenance series, and a hand in the second of those bore a downstroke characteristic that a reader of long standing would recognise; and that the two tracings at the foot of it were made in one room in one sitting by two named hands from one original, in a conservator's presence.

Three of those four things were about a document. The fourth was about a hand.

A hand characteristic is the most dangerous sentence a conservator can write, because it is the only kind of sentence she writes that is not falsifiable by anybody who can read. The batch is falsifiable — one tin lid. The margin is falsifiable — one fair copy. The two tracings are falsifiable by anybody in the blue steps with the book. And the downstroke of a *g* that leans a degree and a half is only falsifiable by a person who has read that hand, and there is precisely one such person in the commissioner's registry, and he is forty-one years old, and he has been in service since before the register, and he was nineteen years old at a kitchen table on the north side of this city and was paid a half-crown a sheet to copy out a docket, and made a game of it with a nine-year-old girl who was supposed to be practising her letters.

The moment that sentence went up a stair in this building it stopped being a provenance note about two documents and became an examination of a man, and the examination would be conducted by his own office, and the result would be that the man either corrected a record or did not, and either way a person would know that Mara Quill had looked at him.

There was a version of the certificate that did not have the fourth sentence in it. It would still be a good certificate. It would be a weaker one by a measurable amount and it would be true. And nobody would ever know that a hand had been left out, because nobody in the basin knew about the leaning *c* except the family it belonged to and one nine-year-old girl who could no longer remember the room.

She could file the weaker one. She had until Friday. After Friday the certificate went up with the requisition and there was no editing it afterwards, because Mara Quill had taught the Office that year that a document which is edited after it is filed has no provenance at all, and she had taught it that herself, and she was not going to be the exception.

At the crown of the road she stopped, and stood in the dark, and said out loud, to nobody, the sentence that Rusk had asked her to write down and had not written down, and then she said it a second time because she wanted to hear whether it sounded any better the second time, and it did not, and she left it in the air where it was, because a sentence in a conservator's hand is not a record and she had spent the afternoon teaching a yard the difference.

She carried the certificate down to the coach and put it in her box with the other papers and did not sleep, and wrote a letter to her mother that began *I have found out who it was in nineteen years of not asking*, and did not send it, and then sent it.

Friday was the fourth of the next month, and the certificate went up on the fourth, and there is a copy of it in a district file at Vellum, and a marginal note in the commissioner's registry in a neat official hand that says the matter has been noted and no further action is proposed at present, and a date against the margin, and that date is a Tuesday in the second month of the next year, and nothing whatever happens on it.
