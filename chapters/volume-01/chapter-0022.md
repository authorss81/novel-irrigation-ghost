# Chapter 0022 — Past The Staging

The switchback was cut in three pitches and Niko Venn went down all three without touching the wall once, which he was aware of, and which he was not going to mention to anybody for at least a year.

He had gone down it at two in the morning on the second with a lamp in his teeth and Wick on the rope above him, and he had spent three days since deciding what he was going to be when he came back up, and the answer he had arrived at was *an apprentice with a master who is under two charges*, which is a thing there is no clause for, and which had the merit of being the least flattering version available.

He had also worked out, in those three days, that he was not going to be that.

---

The spoil at the mouth of the cut had come up again since the second and the rill mark was deeper, four fingers now instead of two, and the standing water was still coming out of the hole in the floor of the cut and going down the fall of the floor and away, which meant whatever was behind it was still putting out more than a bucket a day.

"Twenty feet is the staging," he said, to nobody, because he was alone at the front, which was the arrangement. "Tarin. Twenty feet, and it is boards on two ledgers, and the ledgers are wedged not pegged, so it is a thing that can be moved by a man who is not thinking about it. And there is a ladder."

There was a ladder.

It was a timber ladder, eight or nine feet, made out of a length of scaffold board with the rounds left on one side and the string cut in two to make them, and it was standing from the staging up to the ledge with its foot propped against the staging rail and its head over the lip, and it was a good ladder, and it was not twenty-six years old, and the wood was pale and the rungs were clean.

Niko Venn put the lamp down and looked at it for a while.

"They sent for it," he said.

Nobody had said anything on the way up the mill road, and now Wick said: "Aye."

"On the second he said *get the ladder*, to somebody, and he said it loud enough for three men on a ledge to hear him, and he is a man who has been underground a long time and is not surprised by anything. And now there is a ladder." Niko crouched over it. "That is not a kindness. A kindness would have been no ladder and a locked ring. That is a man who has been told three engineers are coming and has decided to make it easy for us, and I want somebody to write down that I said that before we are on it, because in a week one of us is going to remember it as a rope-eye left for somebody who might be a friend."

"Write it on your hand," said Wick.

"I am going to write it on my hand," said Niko Venn, and wrote on his hand, and went down.

---

The staging was twenty feet down and it was a good place to stand, which was the first thing he understood about why men worked off it. Four boards, well trodden, with a rail on two sides and nothing on the other two, and a coil of new hemp on a peg, and a tin reflector hanging on a hook with a flame in it that had been burning about an hour and had a hood over it against the draught, which was a thing a man had bothered to do.

The man was not there.

The lamp was, and the tin, and a fold of oiled rag, and a boy of about fifteen asleep on the boards with his boots in a basket, and Niko Venn stood on the edge of a staging two miles above a road with a boy asleep on it and had a moment in which he could not think of a single thing that was not a trespass, and it took him about two seconds, and then the boy snored, and Niko went on.

"Tarin," he said, low. "I am not going to be clever about this. I am going to say the whole of it once and then we do the work.

"That boy is paid. He is paid weekly, by somebody who can afford a boy's wages out of a works that has been shut six years, and he is asleep on a staging at midnight because he has been on it every night since the second, which means he is doing our nights for us and he is fourteen and he is being paid to do it. He is not evidence and he is not a man on a staging. He is a boy.

"Whatever is down there, nobody put it there this month. That is the whole of what I can tell you at the top of this ladder and I would like it written down somewhere before we go on."

---

The filter house was a long low building with the gable end open to the quarry and a door of vertical boards in the other, and inside it there was the smell of every filter house in the world, which is wet stone and cold iron and something sweet underneath it that never entirely goes away.

There were four filter beds in a row down the middle, stone-lined, with sand and gravel in them and a layer of lime on the top of the gravel that had gone hard and white as cheese. There was a settling tank at the far end, and a pipe off it going up into the wall, and the pipe was the suction, and it went down.

It went down through a floor of flags on the quarry side into a cast gland about the size of a man's thigh, and it was a copper pipe a hand wide, and it was the only thing in the building that had a direction in it, and Niko Venn put his hand on it and knew at once that it went into a chamber and not into a well, because a pipe that goes down out of a filter house is a pipe that goes to the thing the filter house is filtering for.

The pump was at the open end, where the gable was, and it was not what Niko had expected and not what Tarin would have expected either, and it took him a minute to work out what he was looking at, and then about four minutes after that to stop feeling pleased about having worked it out in a minute.

It was a beam pump. A single cylinder, a walking beam overhead pivoted on a frame, the pump rod hanging from the far end of it into the bore, and at the near end a horse gin: a frame of squared timber, a vertical spindle, a four-bar sweep about eleven feet across, and a pinion. It was the single ugliest and most ordinary machine Niko Venn had ever stood in front of.

The sweep was still.

The walking beam was down on its heel, and under the heel there was a block of oak about the size of a man's boot, and it was pale, and it had been cut with a saw, and the saw strokes were recent enough that the sap was still wet in the weather.

"Tarin," Niko called, and then, because Tarin was thirty feet away with his arm up a shaft trying to see something and did not turn round, he said it louder. "It is chocked. Somebody has put a block of oak under the beam and taken the load off it and stopped the horse."

He looked round the gable for the horse and there was no horse, and the mangers were down, and the trough was scraped out and dry, and there were no cart tracks on the quarry floor going anywhere near the gable, and there was one set of a horse's hooves printed in the dust of the quarry and it was going out and it was a week old at least.

"So it did not break," said Niko Venn. "It was stopped, and it was stopped by a man with a saw, and then the horse went away somewhere with a cart, because you do not leave a horse on a works with nothing for it to do and a drought coming. So: somebody was told, and somebody sent the horse out of the basin, and there is a boy on a staging two nights running because there is still a lamp to keep and a plant to be seen to be standing."

He wrote that on the other hand.

---

The board was nailed by the beam at eye height, and it was a tally board, and it was the single most interesting object in the building.

> *3rd day, 4th day, 5th day, and so on down the board, going back — Niko did not count how far, it was thirty or forty columns and the lamp would not reach the end of them.*
> *Col. 1 — date.*
> *Col. 2 — cans, filled and run off.*
> *Col. 3 — head at wheel.*

And the head at wheel was 5·7 on every line he could reach, and had been 5·7 for as far back as the lamp went, and there was a place about a third of the way up the board where the 7 became a 9 and everything above that place said **5·9**.

Niko Venn did not know what a head was in metres. He knew it was a number that went on a board, and that a number that changes on a board is a number somebody has to be able to say out loud, and he had spent the last month watching a man be charged with tampering over a figure, and so he got out the stub of pencil he carried behind his ear for marking ladder rungs and copied the whole column onto the back of his left hand, date by date, as far as the lamp would let him, including the line where the 7 became the 9.

Then he put the pencil behind his ear again and stood there with his hand open in front of him, reading his own handwriting upside down in a lamp, and thought: *that is a figure that somebody upstairs has been paid not to write down.*

He did not have a name for that thought and he did not need one. He was twenty-two, he had a hand with a column on it, and there was a pipe in the floor of a building that went down into a chamber, and the man who had said *get the ladder* had sent for a ladder, and if Niko Venn stood on this floor for one more hour he would learn who had been paid not to write down 5·9, and he had been told by a man on a wall in the lower terrace that his whole life he had put a dangerous place in front of himself and called it a job.

He took the second lamp, the one with the tin-lid hood his aunt's tin had made, and went to the door at the side of the settling tank.

---

Wick held the ladder.

That was what he was doing and he had been doing it for about forty minutes and he had not been asked to, because Tarin was at the far wall of the filter house with his face on a crack and his right arm up a joint he could not reach, and Niko had gone off with the second lamp saying *I am going to have a look at where the pipe goes* in a voice that was not a question, and Wick had not answered it, because Wick had been on this staging four times in his life and had learned that the correct thing to do with a young man who has decided to go and look is to hold something.

The staging boards were wet. They were wet because a boy had been sitting on them with a lamp for two nights and a tin of oil and the dew comes off that quarry face at midnight like a roof coming down in slow motion. Wick's boots were wet through, because he had carried a bar down a forty-foot face and been in and out of a cutting for nine years, and his left hand was on the ladder rail at about shoulder height because the ladder was standing at an angle and if the ladder went he would be the one it went on.

He could see, in the light of the lamp on the hook, that the foot of the ladder was propped against the staging rail, and that the staging rail was a two-by-four wedged into the face with a wedge, and that the wedge had been driven from the quarry side and was not going to be knocked out by anything as small as a boot.

He was thinking about that, and about how a ladder is not a rope, and about the difference between holding a thing that pulls on you and holding a thing that slides, and about how he had told a man on a step in the dark that he was a man holding a rope in the dark and could be wrong about that in a way he could not be wrong about on paper.

And he thought: *I am not holding a rope. I am holding a ladder. And the only man who could have pointed that out to me is in a building with his arm up a joint.*

The ladder went.

It did not go far. It went about a foot, and the foot came off the rail, and Wick Venn — thirty-eight years old, of this lane, who could hold a bar, a lever, a lead pipe, a cistern lid and a boy of nine in the dark and had done all five — put his left hand flat on the face to stop himself and put his right hand on the ladder and did both of those things in the wrong order.

The left hand found rock first. The rock at the edge of a staging, at the height of a kneeling man, with a lamp on a hook two feet away, is not round. It is a quarry face and it has taken the tool marks of two hundred years of men cutting stone out of it, and it has small square teeth in it about the size of a thumbnail.

He put the flat of his hand on it and slid it eight inches before it stopped.

Wick Venn sat down on the boards of a staging on a wet night with his boots in a basket beside him and his left hand turned over in front of his face, and he could not feel any of it at all.
