# Chapter 0031 — A Form With Six Printed Lines

The tank in the yard of the blue steps was at nine hundred and fifty gallons on the evening of the fourteenth, which is one foot seven inches in a tank of seventy-eight and a half square feet with four thousand seven hundred gallons of room in it, and on the evening of the fifteenth it was at eight hundred and thirty.

The difference was one hundred and twenty gallons, and the hundred and twenty was two numbers. Seventy went out of the door of the dispensary. Fifty went off the open surface of the tank into four hundred yards of dry air, because the sun does not ask a district's permission and has never once been entered on a schedule.

That was the whole of the outflow. Nothing came in, and there had been nothing coming in since the night of the second, and the reason there was nothing coming in was that the water in this lane was not found, it was delivered, and the delivery had stopped.

Niko Venn wrote the two numbers on the coping in chalk, in a level hand, and wrote the date under them, and then cut the notch he had left half-cut in the fifth of five notched pebbles, which took him forty minutes and which was the fifth part-day he had spent on a hill nobody was going to fund.

"You are not going down there," Tarin Rusk said.

"I am not going down there," said Niko Venn. "I said it out loud in the dispensary on the thirteenth and I have not gone back on it and I am not going to go back on it tonight."

"Then what are you doing with the pebble."

"I am doing the counting." He turned the stone over. "You are the one with a book. I have got a stick and a bit of chalk and a tank, and the tank is the only thing in this lane that tells the truth twice a day, and nobody in this yard is going to do it after tonight because they have all got something else to be frightened of. So I am doing it, for nothing, and if a man comes down that track in five weeks with a form about the water supply in connection I would like there to be a hand on this coping going back to the fourteenth that is not mine."

"You have never said you were a keeper."

"I have never said I was anything," said Niko Venn, "and a pebble is not a keeper, and there is no clause anywhere in this city for a boy with a stick and a chalk, and I have stopped trying to find one."

---

The answer came down the cattle track on the afternoon of the sixteenth, on the same horse that had gone up with the warden's condition on the morning of the sixteenth, and the reason it came back on the same horse and in one day was not speed. It was that a form is a quick thing. Nobody had to think about it.

Sluice Warden Pell came down at about four in the afternoon with a folded sheet of good paper and a look on his face that a hundred and forty people in a yard could read in about a second and a half, and Tarin Rusk, who was standing at the wall, thought that he had seen that look on a man in Vellum once and had not known then what it was.

Pell did not open it, which was the thing he had thought about on the road down.

"Three things before I read this," he said, "and the third is the one I would have given you time to think about, and I have not given anybody time, and I am sorry for it and I am not going to do it differently.

"One. This is a reply and not an answer. It is on the paper of the commission's office and it carries the number of my condition, so the office has read my condition, and I want that said before anybody speaks, because for nineteen years I have written things in a book that went up and I have never once known whether anybody read them.

"Two. It does not mention the engineer's statement. Six paragraphs, signed on the fifteenth, and not one word in this sheet about a head, or a chamber, or a plant, or a settlement, or a man. I have had it in the satchel for two days. I am not calling that a conspiracy. I am calling it a form.

"And three. This sheet asks this district a question. Not a warden. Not a physician. The district, in a printed line, and the answer is yes or no, and I have been on this road nineteen years and have never had to explain to a yard what a yes means."

He unfolded it.

> *Office of the Acting Commissioner, water and channels. Sixteenth day of this month. In the matter of a condition entered by the sluice warden of the lower terrace road on the fifteenth day of this month, reference as follows.*
>
> *District. No row is held. No row appears on any schedule in this jurisdiction. The office has no power to enter one and has not attempted to.*
> *Water supply in connection. A contractor's discharge from a works at Ashgate, delivered to the district by gravity under a works order. This is not a supply of this office and is not a supply of this city. It is not entered on any return and no part of it is charged to any district rating.*
> *Condition. Noted. Referred.*
> *Cause. The works at Ashgate are under contract for the maintenance of pumping plant. The contractor was instructed on the second day of this month to suspend. The plant is not therefore running, and no abstraction is taking place, and the condition reported is not a condition of this office.*
> *Date of examination. It is noted that the discharge is the only supply the district has had since the first day of this month. The district is invited to advise this office whether it wishes the discharge to be continued, or suspended, or permitted to continue without comment, and any advice will be entered on this sheet and on the return for the week in which it is received.*

There is no box headed *time* on a warden's form, and Pell said once in a yard on Tuesday that it was the only box he had ever wanted in one, and there is no box headed *cause* on a warden's form either, and the cause had been on this one since about half past four in the afternoon of the sixteenth day of this month, printed on the paper of a man's office four miles away, in a hand that did not hurry, and it said that the plant was not running.

Nobody in the yard said anything for a while. A hundred and forty people and a pump that had not run for thirteen days and a well that had never been tested and thirty-four people on top of a chamber, and the cause of it was a shut plant, and the shut plant was correct, and somebody was right, and it was not any of them.

Then Old Marden, from his own step, in the voice of a man of eighty-one:

"It is stopped. You have told a yard it is stopped. That is the first thing any office has said to this lane since the first day of this month that has been true, and I would like somebody to write down that I said so, because I have watched four men in this month go silent rather than say that out loud, and I have said it."

"It is stopped," said Pell. "And I would like it written down, and I am a warden and I write on Tuesdays."

---

Yara Sen took the sheet from him, and read it standing in the yard, and read it a second time, and then asked for the yard to let her put one thing on a slate before she would answer anything anybody asked her, and the yard let her.

She wrote the heading first.

> *Water supply in connection.*

"That is the box," she said. "I want everybody in this yard to be able to point at it tomorrow, because a sheet like this is a thing you argue with in about nine days, when the ink is dry and the horse has gone back up the track, and by then it will be a rumour that came down a hill. It is a box with six words in it, in a hand, on good paper, and it says that the only water this district has had for sixteen days belongs to a man under contract to a works this office has sealed, and that the city will not pay for a drop of it, and that the city has noticed all of that and has written it down."

She put the chalk down and picked it up again.

"And then it asks us a question. It does not ask us a question about the water. It asks us a question about the pump. Would the district like the discharge continued, or suspended, or permitted to continue without comment. Three answers, and I am going to say all three out loud so that they are in a yard in daylight.

"Suspended: from the seventeenth this district has no water at all. Not bad water. None. A tank going down at a hundred and twenty gallons a day, eighty gallons of clean water held in a dispensary against a day I have not named out loud, a jar of eleven gallons from the ninth, a butt at the top end, and a plate under the yard with four bolts on it and no key in this city. And a tank at that rate is at the bottom of its draw-off on the twenty-second of this month."

"Continued: the discharge comes back. It is the water that came out of a works pump two miles up the mill road into a cistern and through a hundred and twenty-six people's mouths and into nine children, and it is the water I have been putting in a book since the twenty-eighth of the month before. Four of them at three points. One at the beginning of the fourth stage, and I will not soften that in a yard.

"Without comment: the pump does whatever it was doing on its own, and this office has entered on a piece of paper that the district did not ask the question. I would take that in exchange for a good deal at the moment."

Somebody said, at the back, "Then it is the second one."

"I know which one it is," said Yara Sen. "I have known since the ninth, and I have told a yard to go on selling the tank to strangers for a week and a half, and that sentence is going in a book and I would like somebody to remind me of it in a month. I am not going to tell this yard which one it is. That is not a physician's sentence, and I am not going to stand in front of nine children and make it look like a professional opinion. It is a household's decision about what it drinks, and I am the one who has to write down what happens afterwards."

---

She came back to it at about half past five with the sun off the coping, and it was the part of the afternoon that mattered.

"Right. Now the other thing, and I am giving you this before Thursday, because on Thursday somebody in an office is going to take the only sentence out of that sheet that can be repeated without anybody having to think, and that sentence is *the discharge*, and nobody is going to look at the third box at all.

"Here is what happens when a stopped pump is started again. I have been asked by three people this week what happens if that plant starts up. I have the answer to two thirds of it, I have a gap on the third, and I am going to give you the two thirds and say plainly where the gap is.

"First. A plant stopped under a head for thirteen days is not an empty pipe. Every inch of that rising main and of the covered channel that comes down this terrace has been standing full and still since the night of the second, and the first water out of it is the water that has been lying in the pipe, and that water is the worst of it. Not a different water. The same water, standing, in an iron main, in a heat like this. Everything that has come off the inside of a pipe in thirteen days comes off it at once when you start moving it again.

"Second, and this is the one that matters for the ninth and the sixteenth and the twenty-second. I am going to say it as arithmetic and not as a feeling, because I have no instrument for it and I am not going to pretend I have one.

> *The tank in this yard loses a hundred and twenty gallons a day and every drop of that hundred and twenty is water. Seventy goes out of the door and fifty goes off the surface. The seventy carries with it whatever is dissolved in it, because a child drinking it drinks the whole of it. The fifty carries nothing, because the sun takes the water and leaves the salt where it is standing.*

"So the salt in that tank is not being used up, it is being left, and thirteen days of that is not thirteen days of the same water. It is a tank getting stronger, day on day, out of the same hole. On the ninth it was one thing. On the sixteenth it is a different thing. And nobody in this lane has ever tasted them side by side, because there has never been a second sample of anything in this district in thirty years."

She stopped, and picked up the slate, and put it down again without writing on it, which was a thing Tarin Rusk noticed and Hanne Roeg noticed and nobody else did.

"And the third part, which I have not got. If that plant is drawing out of a body of water that has been standing, then that body is being drawn from, and a body that is drawn from does not give up what it was giving up. I know the water under a settlement two miles up this hill comes up out of a kitchen floor at about a hundred gallons an hour and has never been tested, and I know the same water is under this lane, and I cannot tell you in what order that body will be affected, because I have not got an instrument and neither has anybody in this basin, and I have said so in this yard twice this month and I am saying it a third time.

So: two thirds and a gap. And if somebody in this city writes a cause on a piece of paper tomorrow, this is what they will be writing about, and I have put it where a hundred and forty people can hear it, which is the only place a physician can put anything."

---

She was in the yard again on the sixteenth with a slate and her book, doing the count, because she had done it on the ninth and on the sixth and on the twenty-eighth of the month before and had it in front of her on the seventeenth, and the yard had come to watch her do it and had stopped watching by the second row because it was a woman with a book.

It was a woman at the top end who stopped her.

"Mrs Olask," said the woman. "I have listened to that woman for an hour and I want to say one thing and then I will go home."

"Say it."

"Petra is not one of yours." The woman was about sixty and she had her sleeves turned up. "Petra Olask, in the top room, nine years old, and she has had the cracks in her hands since the spring before last, when the water went off and has not come on since. On and off every summer since she was six, and the man at the lodge said it was the lime, and it is the lime, and it has always been the lime. She has never in her life had your tank or anybody's tank, and she is in your column at two points, and she has been in it on the twenty-eighth of the month before and on the sixth and the ninth and the sixteenth, and she is not stationary and she is not improving and she has not moved, and she is a child with bad hands in a hard spring."

Yara Sen had the chalk in her hand and did not put it down for about four seconds.

"Say the rest of it," she said.

"That is the rest of it."

"What is her name."

"Petra Olask. Ninth house past the top step on the left."

Yara Sen looked at the fourth column of the book, and then went back three lines with her thumb, and then went back three lines again from the other end, and then shut it, and held it against her chest with both arms, and stood in a yard in a lane of a hundred and twenty-six people and did not say anything for long enough that somebody at the back said *well?*

"Then I have had five in that column at two points," she said. "Since the twenty-eighth. The sixth day. The ninth. The sixteenth. Four times, in my hand, on four days, with the same four children and me, and one of them has been in it since the spring with lime in the water of a hard spring, and the reason she is in it is that she is a child with bad hands and I have been counting bad hands."

Somebody said, "It is only one."

"It is one out of five at two points," said Yara Sen, "which is a fifth of a column I have been reading out of a yard for three weeks, and a fifth of a column is not a fifth of a child. And I want it said in daylight, in this yard, by me, before Thursday, because on Thursday a man in an office is going to write a cause and I am going to be asked whether the number of children in this district with the marks is rising, and it is not rising, and it has not risen, and *that has been the good news I have been giving this yard for three weeks and it was partly a number I invented about a spring.*

"Do not soften it for me. I have had three weeks of being right in public and I have earned about four days of it. Write it down that I was told by a patient's mother in a yard on the sixteenth day of this month and I have taken it out of my book and I have not put anything else in its place, because there is nothing else I can measure."

Hanne Roeg was four steps up with the mat across her knees and had heard all of it, and she came down two steps, which she did perhaps twice a year.

"Doctor," she said. "You will want to be careful what you say to that woman on the step, because she has a grandson at a school in the fenner district and she will hear the word *made up* and she will hear it in a yard, and she is seventy-one and she will carry it up four steps and she will not put it down again."

"I know which of us is seventy-one," said Yara Sen.

That night a woman from the top end put a slate on the low house step in Yara Sen's own hand, in the small writing, under Petra Olask's name. It said: *Entered 28th, 6th, 9th, 16th. In error from the 28th. Not a case of this district. Lime, of the spring. The mother told me.*

And Marda Ollan came down at nine with the ledger and stood over it, and then said, to nobody:

"Nine hundred and fifty on Tuesday night. Eight hundred and thirty tonight." She wrote it in her own book and turned it round and showed it to Tarin Rusk, because he was the only person in this lane who would know what it was.

> *15th: 950 − (70 + 50) = 830. 16th: 830 − (70 + 50) = 710. 17th: 590. 18th: 470. 19th: 350.*
> *And on the twentieth there is no figure, because if nothing comes in there will be 230 in that tank, and if that plant on the hill comes back there will be four thousand seven hundred, and I have written both of those numbers down and I do not know which one is going to happen and neither does anybody else in this lane.*

"Six days from tonight," said Marda Ollan, "on that arithmetic, and no municipal supply in any form coming down this road, and I have written it in a book because I hold the book and not because anybody asked me to. The eighty gallons of clean water the dispensary is holding was bought on the thirteenth at a shilling the gallon and has been held since the fourteenth so that it can be spent in one day. They draw it on the eighteenth. On the nineteenth there are ten gallons and a jar of eleven and a butt on a stand. On the twenty-second, on that arithmetic, there is nothing in that tank at all."
