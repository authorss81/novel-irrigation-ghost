# Chapter 0007 — What the Water Brought With It

The dispensary on the terrace road was one room above a grain store with a deal table pushed against one wall and a lamp over the table, and it had been opened by Bel Crale in the spring with forty-one pounds and a bucket, and Bel Crale had kept it going since by being the sort of woman who can be trusted with a key.

Tarin came in at half past nine with two stoppered jars and a third full of branch water and did not get to say anything at all.

"Sit on the stool," said Yara Sen, without turning round. "Not the table. I have a child on the table."

She was forty-one and had been down this road since the spring, and the road knew her, and the row did not entirely trust her, and both of those facts were the result of a three-year argument in which she had never once been wrong about the water and had never once been able to prove where the water came from.

The child on the table was about six. He had a bowl of water and a spoon and he was being given sips of it by a woman standing at his head with both hands on the bowl, and he was throwing it back up at the rate of about one mouthful in three, and every time he did it his face changed in a way that Tarin could not stop looking at, which was not pain exactly. It was a sort of startlement, as though the water had gone somewhere unexpected.

"Bel," said Yara Sen. "Where did this child's water come from yesterday morning?"

"From the pump. From the pump by the low house."

"Yesterday morning. And the day before."

Bel Crale looked at the floor. "The pump."

"How long has that boy been drinking from the low pump?"

"Six days." Then, before anybody else said anything: "He is not the only one."

"How many?"

"Three. I have three and I have written down three." Bel Crale's voice was very steady and she put her hands flat on the bowl. "The Sweets, the two under the arch, and the Tall boy who found his way back from the pump this morning by the long road. He does that every day. He did it this morning the long way round because he came out at the top of the lane and he did not know the top of the lane, and I have taught that boy every short cut on this terrace."

Yara Sen straightened up and looked at Tarin Rusk for the first time since he had come in the room.

"You did that," she said.

"I opened a branch to the low pump at ten past six this morning."

"You opened a *channel* to a low pump at ten past six this morning and you did not see whether anybody was drinking from it first, and no, I am not finished, and you will want to be, so let us get that out of the way now and then you can be and I will be standing here while you are."

"I did not open it to make the row ill."

"No," said Yara Sen. "You opened it to make the row not thirsty. I have read your requisition. It is a beautiful document. It is the best-written thing anybody has sent me about this drought and it is technically impeccable and it does not contain the word *where* anywhere in it, and I want you to know that I have had three hundred of those documents in six years and I have never once been able to smell the water out of the paper." She uncorked one of his jars and held it up to the lamp. "This is not silt. Silt settles. This is in solution. This water has been somewhere with a great deal of salt in it and it has come back."

"Where," Tarin said, before he could stop himself.

"That," said Yara Sen, "is the question, and you are the only man in this room who could answer it, and I have been asking the city for three years and the city cannot answer it either, and I have learned something about how this works, which is that nobody is withholding it. Nobody has a copy of it in a drawer. It has simply never occurred to anybody that it is knowable, because it is not in the places where water is supposed to be."

She set the jar down and went to a shelf and took down a stoppered jar of her own, which was the same water, and she had a slate and a stick of chalk, and she was quick and she was not slow down for anybody.

"Give me your two hands," she said to him.

"My hands are all I have got, Doctor."

"Your hands are the only honest instrument in this building and I mean that as a criticism of the rest of us." She turned his right hand over and pressed the pads of two fingers against the pad of his thumb, hard, and then let go, and did it again, on the other hand, and then again, and then wrote a line on the slate without looking at it. "Now the same on the boy's hand." She did it to the child on the table, and to the woman holding him, and to her own. "The skin under the pad of the index finger, and the pad of the middle finger, and the web between them. Those are the three places. It shows about a day before a person starts complaining, and the first complaint is always the taste, and by the time I can see it in the hand the fever has been going four hours."

"What is it."

"It is called salt fever in the Reach and the scaling in the upper markets and nothing at all in the office language, because the office language will not admit there is a name for it." She put the chalk down. "It is not a miasma. It is not a curse. It is not bad water in the way that dirty water is bad water, and I want that said clearly in this room, because the moment anybody in this row hears *bad water* they will decide the answer is a closed pump and a smaller district, and the answer is not a closed pump, because the water in the jar came out of a channel that was dry on Friday and there is no dirty water in this district to have got into it."

"Then what is it," Tarin said.

"Salt," said Yara Sen, "in a quantity that comes from rock, dissolved into water that has been moved through a place it is not supposed to be in." She sat down on the stool opposite him. "Water that goes where it is sent does not do this. Water that is *argued with* does. I have been trying to get that sentence into a report for three years and I will tell you why I have not: because I have never once been able to show anybody a mechanism. I have had a hundred and forty cases between here and the Reach and every one of them in a household on a route somebody has a name for, and the one thing they all have in common is that the name came afterwards."

The room was quiet. Out on the road somebody was running a barrow over the flags, and it went on and stopped, and the three of them listened to it.

"The branch has no name," Tarin said.

"Your branch has a stone in it with my colleague's crooked mark on it, and a route name, and a date, and that is more than most of the Reach has. What it has not got is anybody's agreement." She leaned forward. "You have got a trace, a record, and a man who put his hand on it. You have not got one single person in this district who agreed to anything. There are three hundred people on this terrace and you have a witness and a crowd, and a crowd is not a thing you can take water on, because a crowd is not somebody — they are all somebodies, separately, each one of whom can be asked."

He had not thought it in those terms. He had thought about a plate and a head and a cistern, and about a number in a book, and he had thought about a boy of nine at the top of a lane who was about to be ill, and he had not thought about a crowd at all, because he had not had to until six o'clock this morning and he had been awake since three.

"You named a branch that ran past three hundred people who were not told," said Yara Sen, "and now there are three children ill in the room you are standing in, and the water is salt because it had to go somewhere it was not allowed to be. I have been trying to write the mechanism of that sentence into a report for three years and I cannot, and I am not going to pretend to a man on a morning when I have patients. That is not a mystery. That is a sink."

"The Gauge was right," Tarin said.

She looked up. "What?"

"In Vellum. On the fifth. I put my hand on a record stone of the Ashgate intake and something said: one route remembers. It asks for a witness. Those are the words, and that is all it said." He heard himself and understood that he had said it out loud, and that he had said it the way a man says a thing he has been turning over for a day. "It was right that a route remembered. It said nothing whatever about what it would cost, and it asked for a witness and I found one, and I got a branch of water out of it, and I did not ask the three hundred."

Yara Sen looked at him for a moment with an expression he could not read, and then she said, "I have no use at all for that and I am not going to pretend to you that I have. Get out of my room."

"No."

"Then get out of the *way* of my room, because I have to wash that child in clean water and I have about four gallons of it in this building, and three more children to look at before the light goes, and one kettle." She was already moving. "You may help if you can carry."

They washed the boy. He screamed when the water touched the soles of his feet, and Tarin held him, which he had not expected, and the child hung on to his coat and shook.

When it was done and the boy was asleep on Bel Crale's coat, and the two other children had been seen, and the slate had four names on it, Yara Sen came out into the lane with the jar in her hand.

"Close it," she said.

"I know."

"Close it this afternoon, and I want you to do it in that yard, in front of those people, and I want you to say out loud why."

"That will empty the pump."

"That will empty the pump." She turned the jar over. "You have given that row four hundred gallons of water that has made three children vomit, and if you leave it running I will have nine, and then it will be children who cannot be carried. That is not a moral question. It is arithmetic and it is a fever curve and I have watched it go on a Reach family in two days." She was quiet for a second. "And you do not get to keep it because you feel good about it. That is the whole of my objection. I have had four men in six years bring me water they were proud of, and every single one of them wanted to keep it longer than the children could stand it, and not one of them ever did it on purpose. They did it because closing it meant admitting what they had done."

"Two days," Tarin said. "That is what is in the pump tank. Two days for the row, and then the cistern in the yard, and then the plate."

"Yes."

"Then I will close it."

He walked back up the lane and got the log out and the wedge, and he did it at eleven in the morning, in the yard, with the whole row standing in it, and he told them what Doctor Sen had found and what salt fever was and what he had done to cause it, and he used the word *crosscurrent* because there was no plainer word in the world and then he said that there was no plainer word, which made it worse.

Hanne Roeg asked him one question.

"The man who put his mark on the stone," she said. "Tam. Does he know?"

"He was there when you were all drinking it."

"Then you will tell him it was your doing and not the water's," she said, "because if you do not, he will spend the rest of his life telling people the water turned, and men like that lose the pump, and I have seen it."

He said it to Tam Roke at the pump, alone, with his hand on the bar of the gate, and Roke looked at the pump tank for a long time and then said: "Right," and went to work.

Then Tarin Rusk went and found the blue book.

He sat on the step of the low house with it open on his knees, in the shade, with the noise of a disappointed row behind him, and he got the grease pencil out, and he did the thing he had done perhaps ten thousand times, which was to lay his left hand along the spine of the book and press the pads of the first two fingers of his right hand flat against the fore-edge to square the leaf, and then to set the pencil down and write.

The hand did not do it.

Not slowly, and not clumsily. The two fingers came down, and there was nothing in them, and his hand went on past where the press should have been and lay flat on the paper, and lay there, quite obediently, waiting for an instruction it was not going to get.

He looked at it. It was the same hand. He could see the old lime burn across the second knuckle, and the nail of the index finger was split the way it had been split since Ferrow, and the hand was his.

He got the pencil and wrote the date. He wrote the day and the month, and he wrote the hour, and he wrote the entry: *branch closed 11:20, three children early salt fever, crosscurrent, cause — route opened without a legitimacy condition, my hand.*

His writing was his own. It was legible, it was in his hand, and it was not the writing he had made ten thousand times, and he could not tell you in what particular respect it was different, and he sat on a step in a lane and turned a grease pencil over in his fingers for a long time and could not make himself begin the next line, which was supposed to be the pressure at the junction, and which he could not have got wrong if he had been drunk.

He knew what had gone. It was not a number and it was not a fact; he had lost numbers before, in the flood, and had written them down twice. This was a motion. He had used this motion every day since he was thirteen years old, on gates and floats and spindles and page edges, the two-finger press to feel whether a thing was seated, and it had been so entirely part of his hands that he had never once known it was there, and now he reached for it and it was gone, and what was in its place was a hand that would do what it was told and nothing else.

He tried it once more, on the edge of the book, deliberately, the way you press a bruise.

Nothing.

He had a flask in his bag, and in the flask was the tin pot, and in the tin pot was the bitter leaf he had carried for eleven years and ground for himself because it was the only thing that tasted of anything at all. He poured a cup and drank it standing up.

It was hot. It went down the way water goes down. It had no taste of any kind, and it had no bitterness, and he had drunk it every working day for eleven years, and he stood against a wall in a lane and drank a cup of it and felt the inside of his mouth and could not find anything there to miss.

He put the cup away.

It was going to be a long dry stretch, and he had fourteen days of it before the cisterns went, and he had a stone at a pump with one cut face and one blank one, and he had a notice in his coat with an empty box in it, and he had a requisition answer in a coach driver's hands with a number in it that matched a page a hundred and eighty-one years old, and he had the same fourteen days of nothing but his own arithmetic.

And there was exactly one person in the whole of the Marrow Basin who had put a mark on a piece of stone in the last four years and would do it again, and she was asleep in a room at the end of the row with a basin of water that was going to have to be boiled four times before anybody could put it near a child's skin.
