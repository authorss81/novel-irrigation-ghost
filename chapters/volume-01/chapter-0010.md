# Chapter 0010 — One Name, One Window

The ten days were the worst of his life, and he spent almost none of them doing anything, which was somehow worse.

On the eleventh he went down the terrace road to the warden's station and asked Sluice Warden Pell to post a service window for a district that was not in the schedule. Pell looked at him for a while and said that he had no row, no cause, no keeper, no key, and no engineer, and that five of those were requirements and the sixth was a courtesy, and that the only one of them Tarin had was the courtesy.

On the twelfth he wrote to the Municipal Memory Office in Vellum, four lines, asking for the certified discrepancy to be released so that the number in his log had a document behind it that was not a hundred and eighty-one years old. The answer came on the fifteenth and was two lines and was correct, and told him that a certified discrepancy is the property of the Office and not of the person who certified it. He read it four times and understood that she had not written it, that somebody above her had, and that she had let him know which of the two it was.

On the sixteenth he asked twenty-three people to stand on a stone.

Nineteen said no, and the nineteen were not cowards. Two would not put their name near a plate the warden's board said was out of service. Four had drunk from the low pump on the seventh and watched three children be carried off it. Six said they would do it if there was a window, a date and a warden present, and then said, one after another, that there was no window and no date and no warden, and he had not been able to make those three things out of anything he had.

On the fourteenth he was sitting on the mill wall with Niko Venn, who had been asking what was at the other end of that arch since the seventh, and who told him there was a way down into the old filter house under the works, off the quarry side, in a cut no survey in the city recorded, because his own father had run a load of water down it in a wet year when Tarin was small enough to be somebody else's problem. Tarin wrote it into the blue book with the arm that still worked and put a line under it, and sat looking at the line, because he had no authority to send anybody down a cut and no money to pay anybody who went, and neither of those facts was going to improve.

Tam Roke never said no. He said nothing at all for nine days, and on the nineteenth he came past the gatehouse where Tarin was sleeping and left a length of oak at the wall, planed, tapered, sixteen inches long, with a groove cut down one face that took his chisel cleanly, and a hole drilled at the head for a pin, and no note.

Yara Sen came every day. The three children were improving. The boy who could not find the top of the lane could find it now, and the boy who threw up his water had kept nothing down for four days and had begun to want to eat, which she said was the only clinical fact that had ever made her cry, and then denied flatly when he said so. On the eleventh day of the ration there was a fourth child in the row above with the same illness, and it had come out of the courtyard cistern, which the district drank from on the days the pump was dry and which had been filling since the first with a head off a sealed gate.

They all knew, then. He had said it on the seventh in a yard, and it had not been believed, and by the twentieth it had been drank.

---

On the twentieth day of the ration, Tarin Rusk came down the cattle track at half past three in the afternoon with a bar over one shoulder and a length of planed oak and a chisel in his coat, and the sun was coming in flat off the ridge onto a row of blue doors that had been blue for as long as anyone could remember and had been shut for eleven days because there was nothing to open them for.

The courtyard cistern was at a third and had been at a third for nineteen days.

He had the arithmetic on the back of a ward notice, in a hand that was getting worse. He was, at half past three on the twentieth day of a ration, the only person in the Marrow Basin who had done it, and there was no office anywhere that would check it for him, and that was the situation.

There were about eighty people in the yard when he came over the wall, which was more than the yard had held in his lifetime. Bel Crale had left the dispensary open. There were four children on the flags and somebody's mother on the step with a pail on her hip, and Wick had a bar and no idea what he was for, and Hanne Roeg was on the fourth step with her cane across her knees and the mat over her shoulder, because it was afternoon and it was what she did.

Niko Venn was at the stop-gate with a gauging stick in his hands, because there was nothing else he had been useful for in nine days and Tarin had told him on the fifteenth to stand there and not to speak, and he had stood there every afternoon since and had not once spoken, and the not-speaking had cost him more than anything the Office had ever asked him for.

Yara Sen was at the pump with a slate under her arm.

Sluice Warden Pell came through the arch at twenty to four with a clerk, and the clerk had a board on his arm, and the board was the warden's entry book, and Pell had a stopwatch on a chain and a pencil behind his ear, and he came into that yard and looked at the run coming down the wall and at the eighty people and at Tarin Rusk, and did not say one word for perhaps ten seconds.

"You did not tell me," said Pell.

"No."

"Notice on my board on the first, cause box empty, and a channel condition given to me in the voice of a man reading a shipping list, and I sent it up and they sent it back with a line saying no such row." Pell unbuttoned his coat. "Nineteen days. I have thought about very little else, and I would have come down here on the second if I had had a row to come to. Do you know how a warden works?"

"No."

"A warden works from a sheet. If it is not on the sheet it does not happen, and the sheet is all the law there is." He took the pencil from behind his ear. "I have no sheet for this. I have a condition, a date, a clock, a man who says he opened a gate, and a crowd. So I am going to write all of it, and I am going to write down what I see and who is standing in this yard while I see it, and then it is on a board in an office and it cannot be taken off that board by anybody who was not standing in this yard. That is all a warden has got."

He opened the book on the flank of the pump housing, wrote his own arrival at 15:40 in the left-hand column, and left the right-hand column blank, because the right-hand column wanted the time a condition began and no condition had begun yet. Tarin read the page upside down from four feet away, understood what the blank was for, and went to work.

He had set the oak in the stop-gate on the seventh and taken it out again and set it a half-inch in and out maybe forty times, and on the nineteenth he had found the setting that put six hundred gallons an hour down the branch, which was what the pin hole was for. He set it, drove the pin, wedged it, and stood back. The branch did not jump. The water went into the gap and down and away at the pace of something being allowed rather than something getting through, and came up to a hand's width and stopped, and did not tear the bed and did not fill the lane.

Yara Sen put her slate down on the flags.

"Before you say anything else," she said, in the voice she used to read out a dose. "You are stood down. You are charged with tampering with city records, that charge is open, and I will say so to any man in this yard who asks me in the next two years what happened on the twentieth day of the ration. And the water in that branch is the same water that made three children of mine vomit into a bowl on the seventh day of this month, and I have told this row what it is and I will tell it again. If somebody in this yard has a cleaner bucket I would like to hear about it now, and nobody has to be polite about it."

Nobody said anything. She waited the four seconds she had meant to wait.

"Fine," she said.

Tarin Rusk put the bar down, and took the length of planed oak out from under his arm, and put the chisel in his breast pocket, and walked to the plinth of the low house where the marker stone was set, and got it out.

One face of it was cut. It said *Glassstep low pump branch*, and the date, and under that a crooked mark cut by a man who said so out loud, and the letters of it were the cleanest stone work in the lower terrace, because he had known exactly what he was cutting and had not been frightened.

The other face was blank.

He got the chisel out and set it on the blank face and took the mallet in his left hand and knelt, and he had one name to put there, which was his own, because the record of a thing that had been done without authority ought at least to say whose authority it had been without.

He set the chisel and brought the mallet down.

Nothing happened. The blow landed. The chisel jumped, and the stone took a white bruise a quarter of an inch from where he had set it, and the mark of it would be there for a hundred years, and his hand had put it two lines below where he had decided to put it.

He tried again, and set it a hair lower, and the blow landed and the stone rang and did not take.

He tried a third time, and the hand went somewhere else entirely. He watched it go. The first two fingers of his right hand came down onto the fore-edge of the stone to feel whether it was seated, which is not a thing you do to a stone, and which he had done every day since he was thirteen, to gates and floats and spindles and the spines of field books, and which was not there.

That was the ninth of the ration, in a lane. It was the twentieth, in a yard, with a warden writing.

He put the chisel and the mallet down on the flags and let eighty people watch a municipal engineer fail to cut a letter, and said nothing about it, because nothing in a yard of eighty people is improved by a man of thirty-three explaining that he has lost something in his hand.

He put his palm flat on the blank face instead.

It was cold and gritty and it had never been written on, and there was nothing in it of any kind, and then there was:

> **The Gauge:** The stone is blank. Two hands are on it.

Beneath it was the other thing again. He had had it once, on the sixth, and not since, and here it was in a yard with eighty people in it: a weight under the sentence, a hold on a rope with somebody's weight at the far end of it, a room with more air in it than the room was built for. He had a perfectly good explanation for it this time and took it gratefully — a yard, a warden, a crowd, and his own name in the middle of it. He would rather have the crowd.

*Two hands*, he thought. He looked down at the stone. One hand was on it, and it was his, and the stone was blank, and there was nothing whatever on that face of it, not his name and not a witness and not a date.

So the statement was wrong, and it was wrong in a way that told him something. A stone with two hands on it is not a description. It is a demand. And the second hand is not there, and the second hand is not optional, and if he cut his own name and nothing else he would have a stone with one hand on it that meant a man had been here — which was not a route, and would not move a drop, and would not have kept three children out of a bowl on the seventh of this month.

He said it out loud. He turned round to the yard.

"Two hands," he said. "A route wants two. A name and a witness, and the witness has to be somebody who used it or kept it, and it has to be somebody who could have said no."

He had not planned to say the next part and it came out anyway.

"It has to be somebody who could have said no *today*. In this yard, while the warden is writing. A witness who cannot walk away is not a witness, it is a person standing next to a man who is asking. And if the only people who can be on this stone are people who are already here because they live here and have nowhere else to be, then the second hand is mine, and a stone with my hand on it twice is a man talking to himself. Doctor Sen will tell you in a week that a route named by one man runs crosscurrent and puts children in bowls, and she will be right, and I did that once already, on the seventh, with a full yard and nobody free to stop me."

The yard argued.

It argued the way a yard argues, which is all at once, and it argued for eleven minutes, and Pell wrote the whole of it down, and Tarin stood by the stone and let it happen because there was nothing else that a man in his position could do with a crowd except stand in it.

Some of it was for him. Some of it was against the Water Office and had been since the spring and had nowhere to go. One woman wanted to know who would be blamed. A man wanted to know why it took an engineer with a bar to tell them their own drain had been plated over. Old Marden from the top end asked where the water went that came down the run before it got to the pump, and Tarin told him: past him, to the yard, and the yard's drain was plated, and that plate is under more than it was ever built to take, and a lid under pressure comes off.

Nobody voted, because it was not that kind of yard, and nobody had ever asked it to be.

At ten to four, Tam Roke came out of the low house with his coat on.

"Right," he said.

Tarin turned round.

Roke came and stood in front of the stone with his hands in his pockets, and looked at the blank face, and did not touch it.

"I put my mark on the other side of that on the seventh," he said. "You told me the truth about what I was signing, which was that you did not know, and I marked it anyway because the row was out of water. I have had eleven days with that, and there has been water in this yard every day of it out of that cistern, and there is a boy of mine at the top end who is nine, and who thought for a week that the water was the Water Office's fault, and who now has a water physician telling him it is the water's fault, and both of those are true, and it does not make it simpler."

He took his hands out of his pockets.

"I am not going to be your second hand today," he said. "I am going to be the man who keeps that pump, and a man who keeps that pump is the man who has to be here at four in the morning when the branch has been running all night and nobody knows what the head has done. I marked it once. I would like to be a man who has a pump and a mark, and not a man who has a mark."

He looked round the yard.

"That is not me saying the branch is a bad idea," he said. "I am saying that if I stand on that stone then the day this stops, the row says the pump keeper stopped it, and that is what will be true, and I will not have it be true twice."

Nobody spoke.

Then the old woman on the fourth step said, "Roke," in a voice that meant about nine things, and he shook his head at her, and she let it go, which took something out of her that Tarin could see.

"It has to be somebody who can say no," he said, to Tarin. "So let somebody who can say no be in it. I have said it in front of a warden, which is better than a stone, because stones do not get argued with."

Pell made a small noise and did not look up.

Tarin Rusk stood in a courtyard on the lower terrace with eighty people in it and a warden writing and a man who had just refused him, and he took the two minutes he needed to lay out his terms, and he laid them out flat and slow, the way a man reads a risk aloud to a crew he has sent into a channel.

"There is no second hand on that stone," he said, "and I am not going to pretend to one. I am standing in a courtyard in front of a sluice warden who is entitled to write down whatever he hears, and I am doing it while I am stood down and charged with tampering with city records, and Doctor Sen has told you what that water did to three children on the seventh of this month, and I am telling you now that it will do it again. The district's only relief is a plate of iron under that yard that I have no authority to unbolt and no key for, and if the head comes up through it the yard goes under four feet and that plate comes off its bolts."

Somebody made a sound. He kept going.

"It runs from four o'clock this afternoon until three o'clock this morning. Eleven hours. The three tanks, the cistern, and every pail and pot in this row, and the moment those are level with the head in the branch it will stop of its own accord, the same way the cistern in this yard stopped on the first morning of this month, and it will not need me to stop it. After that there is nowhere for the rest of it to go but through that plate." He got the folded notice out of his coat and held it up where the yard could see the paper, which was good stock, and the box near the foot of it, which was empty. "That is the notice I filed with an empty box in it on the first, and I left the box empty on purpose, because filling it in would have been a report on a breach, and I did not want one before I had looked. I did not want to be caught before I had looked. I would do it again, and I would rather say it here than have it found in a file."

Pell's pencil stopped.

"Continue," said Pell.

"Eleven hours is arithmetic and not a favour, so here it is, and anybody who thinks the sum is wrong should say so now." He nodded at the cistern. "Ten feet across, twelve feet to the coping, standing at four, so it has eight feet of room in it, and eight feet in a tank that size is four thousand seven hundred gallons. The three tanks are fourteen hundred between them, cleaned. Every pail and pot in this row is six hundred, if they are all full. That is six thousand seven hundred gallons of vessel. The branch as I have set that log will carry six hundred and ten gallons an hour, and six thousand seven hundred into six hundred and ten is eleven hours, and it is not a guess. It is what the stick says and what the pail says, and I have had three days with a stick and a pail and nobody in the world to check me."

A woman at the back said, "It is eleven."

"Thank you," Tarin said, and did not ask who she was, and did not look for her, and let it be the yard's.

He put both hands on the stone.

"I am not giving this yard water," he said. "I have no authority to give anybody anything. I am putting a name on a stone in a courtyard and a warden is writing it down, and if anybody in this yard wants it stopped, say so now, and it will stop, and I will not argue with them, and I will not come back tomorrow and do it anyway."

Nobody said anything.

Then at the back of the yard a man put his pail down on the flags, turned round, and went out through the arch and up the track, and did not look back, and about forty people watched him go, and nobody stopped him, and that was the entirety of the only argument the route got.

"And after all of that," Tarin Rusk said, "here is what I actually came down here to do, and I have not done it yet. I am going to do it now, in front of a warden and in front of all of you, and one of you is walking out of the yard over it, which is correct, and I would have said exactly the same if you had gone before I started."

Nobody in the yard made a sound at all, and that was how Tarin Rusk knew that eighty people were listening. At the stop-gate, a boy of twenty-two with a gauging stick in both hands did not speak either, having been told not to, and Tarin found that he was grateful for that in a way he had not expected to be.

"Glassstep low pump branch."

The water in the branch changed.

Tarin had his hand on the stone and his forearm was not in the channel, and he felt it anyway, in the arm, the way he had felt it on the seventh: the water stopped fighting. The bed of the branch, which had cut a fresh groove in the limestone an inch deep in six hours on the seventh of the month, went quiet, and the sound of the run coming down the wall dropped by about a third, and the pump's handle went from heavy to light, and somebody at the back of the yard said *oh* in a small voice, the way you do when something you have been carrying turns out to be possible.

"It is not water you can see from a gatehouse," said Bel Crale. "It is water you can hear."

A boy of about nine was standing at the cistern with a bit of chalk and had been marking the stonework every ten minutes, because that is what his mother had told him to do with a stick when the water came, in a lane where a great many things had been done on the strength of a stick. He said, without turning round, that it had come up a finger's width since four o'clock.

They stood and watched a cistern in a yard that was not on any sheet fill at the rate of a finger's width every ten minutes, and it was not a miracle, and it was not nothing either. It was a head of five point eight nine metres on a shut gate, and a hand's width of branch, and a length of oak that a man had planed in his own kitchen, and a stone with one name on it and a crooked mark on the other side, and eighty people, and a warden.

Pell wrote for a long time. The right-hand column was the last thing he filled, and it said 16:00, and the twenty minutes he had stood there with the pencil off the page were the twenty minutes in which an engineer in a wet coat had laid out his terms and a yard had decided what to do about them. He wrote the names of the people who had spoken, and he wrote down that one person had left the yard during the naming, which Tarin had not expected and understood, a little later, to be the single most useful thing in the book.

Afterwards the woman at the back said the time out loud, four o'clock and three o'clock, and a boy said it too, and the two of them said it at slightly different moments and got the same figure, and nobody in the yard looked at Tarin when it happened, which he was grateful for in a way he had not expected to be grateful for anything that day.

At ten past six Pell closed the book and said, "Engineer. You will be escorted out of this yard at eighteen hundred, and you are not going to argue with me about it, because I have written the time on a board and a man in an office in this city will compare it with the time on a pump house log, and if I let you stay till dark then the only honest thing I could write is that I let a man stay past dark."

"Write it," Tarin said.

"There will be a second charge," Pell said. "It is not the same charge and it will not be joined to it, because they are about different things. The first is about a record. The second is about a structure, and about serving water out of a reservoir this city's own ledger records as closed, and it is dated this afternoon off this entry." He put the pencil behind his ear. "I am telling you now so that you do not spend a night deciding what I think of you. I think what is on the board."

"Right," said Tarin Rusk.

He picked up the bar. He said one more thing to the yard, because he had promised himself in the lane four days ago that if he ever stood in front of these people again he would say the true thing, and the true thing was the one he had not said to anybody.

"I do not know where it comes from," he said.

Eighty people looked at him.

"I know what it does, how much of it there is, how fast, and what it is carrying. I can tell you the head to two decimal places and I cannot tell you what is on the other side of that gate, and I have not found out in twenty days, and I would rather say that here than have anybody find out later that I knew and did not."

Hanne Roeg said, from the fourth step, "That is the first honest thing a man in a wet coat has said on this terrace since I was young."

Then she said, without any change of tone at all: "You have still not told me about your mother."

"I told you what the account says."

"You told me what the account says." The mat was across her knees. "I asked you on the first day what had happened to her, and you gave me a sentence, and a sentence is not an answer, and you have been down here nine days with a chisel and I have fed you twice. So: is she alive, or is she in the seventh slope with the rest of them, or is she somewhere else entirely, and if she is somewhere else entirely, then who has been keeping her there?"

"I do not know," Tarin said.

"That," said Hanne Roeg, "is the second honest thing a man in a wet coat has said on this terrace since I was young. Leave it at that and get your water, and come back and tell me when you know something else."

Pell's men came through the arch at eighteen hundred — a warden's attendant and two watchmen off the terrace road, who had clearly walked down for this and were clearly embarrassed about it — and Tarin Rusk picked up his bar and his tin pot and the flat brown sheet with his own name on the warden's board in his own hand behind it, and let them take him out through the arch and up the track.

The watchmen took him to the second gatehouse and left him there, because there was nowhere else to put a man under a stand-down, and one of them said, not unkindly, that he would be back at dawn, and Tarin said that was unnecessary, and the watchman said it was the regulation, and went and sat on a step.

The empty cistern house stood against the inside of the second gatehouse wall, and nobody had used it since the ration, and he went in and lay down in the dry corner of it with his coat under him, and did not sleep, and could not have told anybody afterwards what he had been thinking about, because it had not been one thing.

At ten past three in the morning, somebody shook his boot.

He had been not asleep. He came up out of the dark with the bar in his hand before he was properly awake, and the boy at the cistern-house door was nine years old and had a blanket round his shoulders and was standing well back from him.

"Sir," the boy said. "You should know. It stopped."

"What stopped."

"The pump. Three o'clock, near enough." The boy said it carefully, the way a child does when he has been told to be careful. "Dad was watching it. It went down and down and then the handle got heavy again, and the water in the channel went thin and then it went, and Dad said it was off."

"How long did it run?"

The boy worked it out, and got it wrong, and then got it right, because somebody had taught him the hours.

"From four in the afternoon. Eleven hours, near enough. Dad counted."

"How is it now?"

"Now it's nothing," the boy said. "The run's dry. The cistern's full, and the tanks are full, and Mum's got every pail in the row out, and Dad says it will be the best day this row has had since before he can remember, and the water physician is in the yard, and there is going to be an argument in the morning about whether any of it can be used." He shifted the blanket. "You said you would shut it yourself."

"I did."

"Did you not?"

"I was carried out at six," Tarin said.

The boy looked at him for a moment with the flat, unkind clarity of a child who has been told a plain fact by an adult and can tell that the adult has a problem with it.

"So it stopped by itself," he said.

"It stopped by itself."

"Then it wasn't yours," said the boy, and went home.

Tarin Rusk sat on the stones in the dark with a bar across his knees, and did the only arithmetic left in him, because he could not help it.

The municipal cistern rows for seven to twelve were finished; the return on the twentieth had said three days, which was the Office being generous, and on the twenty-first there would be nothing in them at all. Behind and above him the row had eleven hours of branch water in three tanks and sixty pails, and the yard cistern was up to its lip, and none of that was municipal, none of it would be on a return, and all of it was the same water.

He had cut a name into a stone once, on a lane, with a man who was free to refuse and did not refuse. He had said a name out loud in a yard, with a warden writing, and eighty people, and one man walking out, and one man who said no in front of everybody and was entirely right to. He had been carried out of it two hours before it ended.

It had run nine hours after he was gone.

It had stopped at three in the morning, in a lane on the lower terrace of a city that had been told its reservoir was dead, and it had stopped the way a thing stops when it has finished and not the way a thing stops when somebody tells it to, and there was nobody in it. Not him, and not Tam Roke, and not the man with the crooked mark on the other face of a stone whose blank face was still blank, and nobody in Vellum with a book, and nobody in the warden's station, and nobody in the whole of the Marrow Basin.

He got up and went out into the lane to look at it anyway, because he was an engineer and because it was dark and because there was nothing else to do.

The run was dry, and the branch bed was smooth where it had not been smooth on the seventh, and the water in the yard cistern was up to the lip and dark. The plate under the yard was making a sound he had not heard before: a very small ticking, every ten or twelve seconds. He stood over it and listened and could not tell whether it was iron cooling in the night or water finding a way round the bolts.

Then he went back and sat down on the step outside the cistern house and wrote nothing down, because he had tried, on the seventh, and his hand would not make the notation, and there was no longer any part of that day that he could have written with.

In the morning there would be a hearing, and after that the cisterns would be empty, and the district would be on a yard cistern full of bad water, and the plate would be the next thing that happened to the lower terrace, and he would have to find a way to stand in front of a room and say *I do not know where it comes from* and have it believed by somebody who does not believe him.

The boy had been right about that, at any rate. It had not been his.

And in the morning it was going to have to be somebody's again, and the only people in the basin with any business holding a name for it were a row of blue doors, a man with a crooked mark on a stone, a woman on a step who had said a name out loud once already in her life, and a district that had gone to bed wet for the first time in eleven days and would have to be got up in the morning to find out what that had cost.
