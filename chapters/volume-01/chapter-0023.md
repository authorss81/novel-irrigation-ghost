# Chapter 0023 — A Man Holding A Rope

The first thing Wick Venn established, sitting on the boards of a staging with his left hand turned over in front of a lamp on a hook, was that he was not going to be the man who made a noise about it.

He had worked the Reach six days a week for nineteen years and had put a cistern lid off its hinge in a way that put a white scar down one forearm, and he had once held a lead main on the terrace road while a boy of nineteen caulked the joint behind it and the main had a head on it and neither of them had told anybody they were doing it. He knew exactly what he looked like and he had a fairly clear idea of what it costs a man in a lane of a hundred and twenty-six people to be the one who is helped.

So he said: "Right," and then he said, "That is me then," and then he said nothing else about it for six hours.

---

Niko Venn came back up the adit at about twenty past one with the tin-lid hood in his hand and an expression on his face that Wick had seen on the boy once before, on the night of the thirtieth, at twenty-five past nine in the morning with both hands on a bar.

"Can you feel that," Wick said.

"What?"

"Your hand. Can you feel it."

Niko crouched down and took hold of it, and looked at the palm, and touched each finger in turn with the tip of his own, and Wick watched his own face while a boy of twenty-two did it, and after the fourth finger he stopped helping and let the boy do all five, which is the moment a man finds out what he has lost.

"Nothing," said Niko Venn. "Not the two outside. The other three I can make you feel if I press."

"Press."

He pressed. It came back not as a touch but as a sort of news, arriving a second late and about as informative as a letter from somebody who did not want to write.

"That is not feeling," said Wick. "That is being told."

"No," said Niko. "It is not."

And then the boy sat down on the boards beside him, which was the correct thing to do and which neither of them mentioned, and the two of them stayed there about a minute and a half in the light of a lamp on a hook with a sleeping boy in a basket at their feet and a plant that had been stopped for eight days forty feet below a cut.

---

Tarin Rusk did the only thing he had, which was to write it down, and he did it on a board because the page was in the book and the book was in a bag and the bag was in a cellar ninety feet below them with Niko's lamp in it.

He wrote on the flat of the staging boards with a stub of chalk, in the level hand of a man making an entry in a district book, and then he read it out, because Wick had asked him to read it out, which nobody had ever done for him in his life and which he found afterwards that he would remember for the rest of it.

> *6th day, about ten past one in the morning. Wick, W., left hand, palm, grazed across eight inches on the quarry face at the edge of the staging while holding a ladder. Full thickness of skin off the heel of the hand and the base of the thumb, the outer two fingers white and cold to the touch. Patient reports no sensation in the outer two fingers and reports sensation in the other three only when pressure is applied. Patient states he cannot close the hand and cannot hold a rope or a bar with it.*
> *Not treated by me. Nothing in this kit. To be seen by a physician or a bonesetter in daylight and the sooner the better, because a hand like that wants cleaning and a finger like that wants a splint and a man like that wants to be asked rather than looked at.*

He read it out and then he said: "That is all I have got. I am not a physician and I am not going to pretend that the fact that I have written it makes it better."

"That is more than the last one had," said Wick Venn. "Go on."

---

They tried to get him up the ladder first, which was a mistake, and they tried it for about nine minutes.

Tarin went first, and got up eight feet of it before the rail came under his hand, because he could not get four fingers round a square and could not get two either, and the two he had been using for forty years were not there. He came down and said nothing about it and Niko went up and got six feet and came down and said a great deal about it.

Wick sat at the bottom and watched two men who were better than him at this fail at it, and it was the single most useful thing that happened to him all night.

"Right," he said. "I am not going up that. I want that said out loud now, before we do anything else, so that nobody spends the night on it. I am not going up that ladder tonight or tomorrow morning, and it is not a matter of nerve, I have watched the two of you and neither of you can hold a rail, and a man who cannot hold a rail is a man on a rope, and a man on a rope wants both hands, and I have got one."

"Then you go on the rope," said Tarin.

"I go on the rope when there is a rope that a man may go on." Wick got up on his feet, which took a moment. "And there is not one, and we have known there is not one since the second, and I have been standing here for two nights telling myself the ring would do, and I am a man who thinks a thing is a thing because he wants it to be, and my hand is the bill for it."

---

The ring.

It was an iron ring set into the quarry face at the top of the cut, and a rope-eye in the ring, and the eye was a forged loop about the thickness of two fingers set through the ring and hammered flat and closed, and it was sound iron, and it was a hundred and fifty years old, and on the second night Wick had put a bight through it and taken the whole weight of Niko Venn and a rope and a lamp on it and it had held and he had thought no more about it.

On the sixth night he got up the ladder as far as he could, which was five feet, and put his right hand flat on the ring and pushed.

The ring moved.

Not much. About a quarter of an inch, in a socket of lime mortar that had gone to sand on the exposed side, and when he took his hand off there was a puff of it and a smear of rust-coloured dust on the back of his hand.

"That is not a ring in stone," he said. "That is a ring in a lump of lime, and I put four hundredweight of a man on it on the second and it has been sitting there in the weather since with nothing on it, and the weather has got into the joint where the stone has gone soft and it will come out with the stone round it the day somebody loads it properly."

"Then it is no good for you either," said Tarin.

"It is no good for anybody, and it is no good for me, and if I get twenty feet up that face on it in the dark then I come down it in the dark with one hand, and if I come down it in the dark then there is a boy of nine in a chair in this district who has nobody to bring him his milk." He wiped his hand on his thigh. "So the ring is out. Write that down too."

---

It took them from half past two until about four to work out what else there was, and the working-out was mostly Wick, because Wick had been building things with two hands since he was thirteen and the question of how to get three men up a face with one of them broken was a question about **knots**, and knots were the part of his trade that had never once been in his hands.

He could not grip. He could tie.

That was the thing he found out between two and four in the morning, sitting on the boards of a staging with his good hand and his bad one and a coil of eight-fathom hemp, and it was the only thing in the whole month that had gone right for him.

Tying a knot is not a matter of holding. A knot is made and then it is *dressed* and then it is *set*, and if you can see what you are doing you can do all three of those with a wrist and a forearm and the flat of two fingers, and Wick Venn could see perfectly well, and it was Tarin Rusk's right thumb and Niko Venn's two fingers and about an hour and a half of patience that got a **becket** and a **bowline** and a **half hitch** onto a ladder rail and out into a bight, and Wick Venn tied every one of the three with his knee holding the rope and his teeth holding the tail and the lamp on the hook throwing his shadow up the face forty feet.

"The trouble with this," he said, halfway through, "is that I can do it, and being able to do it is exactly the thing that will kill me. I can do knots to the end of my life with this hand. What I cannot do is carry a bar, hold a pipe, take a cistern lid off a hinge, or go down a ladder. And that is the whole of what I have done for nineteen years."

Nobody had anything to say to that and he did not want anything to say to it.

---

They rigged the bight up to the ledge and got it over the lip and made it fast to a hawthorn, and Tarin went up it and came down, and Niko went up it and came down, and it worked, and it was the answer to getting a rope to the top of the face, and it was not the answer to anything else in the world.

"Two of us can go up and down that," said Tarin. "On the ring, or on this, if somebody stands on the rail. Neither of those is three, and neither of those is you, and I am not putting you on a rope that goes through lime mortar with a wound on your hand that I cannot dress in a cellar with a lamp and a jar."

Wick Venn agreed with him, and sat down, and then said the thing that he had been building the last hour and a half for.

"The adit."

"The adit is not a way out."

"I have not said it is a way out. I have said it is the only piece of the hill that is not a way out, and that is why it is worth looking at." He put his good hand flat on the boards. "An adit is a drain. You do not drive one into a hill and leave it. You drive one from somewhere to somewhere, and the somewhere it comes out at is a door, or a shaft, or a mouth, because a man who drives an adit has to be able to get his barrow out of it. And Niko went down it ninety feet on his own at half past one in the morning with a lamp he made a hood out of a tin, and he has not been able to shout us down it since, and it has taken me two hours to work out that I do not know where that boy is."

They had not been able to shout him down it. That was true. The adit turned.

"I am not blaming him," said Wick, before Tarin said anything. "I want that on the record as well, because in a week somebody is going to try to make that the whole of it. I took a lamp off this staging because a boy of twenty-two went off down a hole and I am a man who holds things. He went because he is his age and it is his father's hill and there is a number on a board in there that somebody paid not to write down. Both of those are the ordinary way that ordinary men get into holes, and I have watched it four times and it has not once been stupidity."

"So it is not nobody's fault."

"It is nobody else's," said Wick Venn. "It is mine, and I have written it on a board, and that is the end of it and I am not going to be talking about it in a week."

---

The boy on the staging woke up at about six and looked at the three of them and got his boots out of the basket and said, to Wick, not unkindly:

"You've hurt your hand."

"Aye."

"There's a box under the settling tank with clean linen in it and a jar of oil, because Mrs. Drust sends it down on a Monday." He looked at Wick's palm. "You want me to get it."

"Yes," said Wick. "And I am going to say thank you, and I know what you are and I am not going to pretend you are not here, and I am not going to write your name down either, because you have not asked me to and because a boy on a staging is not a witness for anything and if anybody comes up here in a month and asks me who I met I will tell them a man I could not name, and that will be true, and it will be the only thing I have done in this business that I am sure of."

The boy got the box.

The day went. They slept in turn, badly, on the settling tank's stone rim, and Wick got his hand washed in a bowl of water the girl had carried down in a covered pail, and the water was cold and clean and came out of a butt in the store that she fills twice a day off the well-head with a pail and a windlass, and which she had been filling twice a day for a year without once appearing to think that was remarkable.

At about four in the afternoon on the sixth day, with his hand bound in clean linen and oiled, and the lamp re-trimmed, and eight fathoms of rope on a staging with nothing to tie it to, Wick Venn looked at the door at the side of the settling tank and said the sentence that the rest of it turned on.

"Right," he said. "Then we are going down after him. And before we do, somebody write down that we were not let out of here by anybody. We were not rescued. We walked in."
