# Chapter 0111 — The Bank View

On the Tuesday morning, the third of next month, the senior clerk of the Fair Copy set a plane table on the dry ground at the ford end of the lane at Cinder Ford, and by eleven o'clock there were a chain and four pegs and a level out on the drain bank, and Tarin Rusk was standing on a made-good bank with the silt of Sunday's work still in the seam of it, watching a magistrate measure what four people had done with a rope and a willow.

He had gone over the ford at eight because the clerk's notice of the bank view had come up on Monday's bundle and named the hour, and because Dorrin had asked for the four persons who made good the gap on the first of next month, and because a man who fills a butt at six can be on a bank at eleven and still be at a wall at six. He had told Nesta at the plank on Monday evening, and she had said the arrangement was the arrangement and not a schedule, and had asked him one question, which was whether he was going to say anything about the hand, and he had said no.

Cobb was the senior clerk's name. He was about forty and carried the level in a wooden case with a strap over his shoulder. He drove a peg at the ford end, ran the chain across the face of the made-good, read the height twice and wrote it, and then went along the top and did it again at four points, and the second reading agreed with the first, which Cobb noted on the form, because a clerk who records that his own figures agree has done the only honest thing available to him before breakfast.

"A yard," Dorrin said, when Cobb had the last peg in. "On the thirtieth it was a yard. What is it now."

"It is not a gap," Cobb said. "It is a face." He took the level out of its case and put it away again. "Whoever did this has laid about four feet of stone and run a clay foot under it and taken the top off the willow roots and put the top back on the roots and staked it. That is not nothing. That is a making-good by any definition I have got in my head."

"By the definition in the schedule," Dorrin said.

"By the definition in the schedule it is not a making-good. It is work. A making-good under the schedule is stone to the line, with a bond, a joint, and a date cut in the coping. That is a man in a shed with a contract. This is four people with a rope and a willow and two coats' worth of silt, and it will stand until a man comes at it in the wet with a bar." He looked at the clay seam. "It is good work. I would not want a better face on a bank I did not have to pay for."

Dorrin wrote for a while. Tarin could see the notebook from where he stood and could not read it at forty feet, and did not try, because a magistrate's notebook is not a thing a man with a pail reads at forty feet.

"Read it out," Dorrin said, "and I will correct it if it is wrong."

Cobb read it out in the voice of a man reading a form, and the lane heard it the way a lane hears everything, which is to say all of it and none of the punctuation. "Bank at lane end, Cinder Ford far-bank row. Second view, Tuesday the third of next month. Gap of one yard closed on the first of next month by four persons at their own request and without charge to any district. The foot let into the drain bed, the top held on the willow roots, the face of about four feet faced with stone from the ford bank, and the joint above the low water of the drain filled with clay from the ford. The works were performed by Mr C. Lille, Mrs S. Marr and Mrs J. Marr, of the row, and by Mr W. Venn of Nether Sallow. The works are sufficient for the present state of the drain. The works are not a making-good under the schedule of 1902, there being no bond, no joint and no date. The works are not sufficient against a season."

"Read the last sentence again," Sella Marr said.

She was sixty, and she had slept one night in the fourth door, on the second, and her own door had come back to her when the seal was lifted, and she came down the lane in waders somebody had lent her to hear a bank made out of her neighbours' Sunday.

"It is in the form," Cobb said.

"Read it again," Sella said, "and read the four names again, and let me hear my own name in it a second time while the lane is dry. There is a time coming when this will be a piece of paper in a town and not a voice in a lane, and I would like to have heard it twice."

So Cobb read the four names again. Sella Marr's went down a lane twice in one morning, and Jess Marr's beside it, and Corrie Lille's, and Wick Venn's of Nether Sallow, and nobody made a joke about the man from over the ford, because a man who put his shoulder under a bank on a Sunday morning is not a joke.

Then Dorrin took the notebook back and struck a line out with one stroke, and Cobb said nothing about that either.

"What did you strike out," Perdie Ammid said.

She had come over with a satchel and no book, and had not been asked to, because a clerk's notice of a view in a lane is a thing a valley hears about, and she heard it on Monday night and was on the ford stones at nine on Tuesday morning with thirty-three sheets in the bag and nowhere to put any of them. She stood at the ford end with a slate on her knee and her thumb along its edge.

"I struck out *is not sufficient against a season*," Dorrin said. "It is true. It is not mine to write in a bank view, and if I write it in a bank view it becomes the finding, and the finding goes on a form, and the form goes into a bundle, and then it is a thing somebody is going to be late about. I have written the four names, because they did it, and the four names are the finding." He shut the notebook. "Madam, you keep this valley's record and you have no book. Is that the whole of your condition this morning."

"That is the whole of it," Perdie said. "Thirty-three numbered sheets, and a satchel, and a figure of thirty-four and a half days in seven lines on one of them with nothing at the foot of it, and a number due at the foot of it in about a year."

"Then I will say what I came to say, and I will say it in front of these people, because a finding in a lane is worth exactly as much as the number of people who heard it."

He put his hand flat on the stone of the face, the way a man puts a hand on a thing that has been measured twice.

"This bank was made good on Sunday the first of next month by four people, three of them of this row and one of Nether Sallow, who were asked to do it and said yes. No magistrate was there. No clerk was there. No bar was there. The office of the Fair Copy has had nothing to do with that bank except what I write in this book between now and Friday." He took his hand off the stone. "I am going to write, truly, that it was made good by four residents of the row at their own request and without charge to any district, because it was, and because a record that leaves that out is a record that gets copied forward, and I have spent a week in this valley learning what a copied-forward record costs. I am also going to tell you, in the same breath, on the same page, why I am writing it down. A work that is not a making-good under the schedule is a work of improvement. A work of improvement is charged to the district it stands in. There is no other rule. It was printed in 1902 and it is not mine, and I would not have written it if I could have found a way round it."

"So the four of us are charged," Corrie Lille said, "for doing it for nothing."

He was an old man in waders with net twine in his pockets and he had been the record of that row on his own hook for eleven years.

"You are charged for doing it for nothing," Dorrin said, "and so is the row charged for a man from across the ford, and I have not decided whether that is fair and I have written all four of your names on it, which is what the form requires and not more than the form requires." "And I want you to hear the second thing better than the first. The charge will be small. And I want you to hear the first thing anyway, and it is that nothing in this is against you. It is a district that has never paid anything, paying something, for the first time."

"Then it is a punishment with a small number on it," Corrie said, and went and sat on the coping of the pump.

---

"What is a rate," Sella Marr said, and put her hands on the face of the bank and left them there, and asked Cobb once and in plain words, because she had spent nineteen years being asked nothing at all.

Cobb looked at Dorrin, and Dorrin nodded, because a magistrate who will not let his own clerk answer a question in a lane has decided what the lane may know.

"A rate is a sum on a door," Cobb said. "Not on a person. On the door. It is struck in the pound and collected once a year and it goes to the district, and the district pays for the works that are not on the schedule. That is all a rate is. It is a sum with a door under it."

"How much."

"That I cannot tell you, and I want to be exact about why, because I can see that you want a number, and a number is what you have gone without for nineteen years." He took a folded paper out of his coat and showed it rather than read it. "The sum for the works themselves I can give you on the Monday after they are done. The rate I cannot give you at all, because a rate is a fraction of what a door is valued at, and the valuation for this district has not been made, and there is nobody in this district who is able to make it. Not this office. Not me. Not the four of you. It will be made in a year by a man who comes through with a book and does not stay the night."

"So a door can be rated," Sella said, "and the rate is whatever a man with a book says the door is worth, divided by something."

"Yes."

"And on Tuesday the third of next month a door in this lane could not be rated, because there was no district for it to be in. And in nine days there will be a district, and the district will have a bill on it, and the bill will be on six doors." She took her hands off the stone. "That is the whole of what a month has done to us, and it took a flood and a magistrate and a court to do it, and it is not a good thing, and I am not going to stand in front of you and pretend I think it is. It is also the first paper anybody in nineteen years has wanted anything off me instead of nothing. Write that one down somewhere, because I have wanted it said and nobody has said it."

"It is written," Perdie Ammid said. "I have a sheet in the satchel and I write down what a room says and read it out, and I have put it on this one because I could not have got it into the front."

Dorrin looked at her for a moment. "You write things down and you read them out, and you have no book."

"I have thirty-three numbered sheets and no book, and a number due at the foot of a column in about a year," Perdie said. "So I know what it is to have a thing wanted off you that you never agreed to, magistrate, and I have not got the morning to talk about it in a lane."

"Neither have I." Dorrin turned back to the lane. "Saturday the seventh, six in the morning, at the lowest state of the drain. That is when the works are to be done in a form that will take a bond and a joint and a date, because I have been told the face will not last a wet winter and I believed the man who told me, on the evidence of my own boots on the thirtieth. Six in the morning, because the drain is lowest before the day's draw, and a making-good laid at ten o'clock is a making-good laid in water." He wrote it on the notice and Cobb initialled it. "The four of you are named on it. I would rather have you at six than not, and I would rather have the man who made the first face there as well, and I am told that man is not at six, and I am not going to ask him why, because he will not be there and I know he will not be there, and a magistrate who says a man's name at an hour the man cannot be at is asking a man to account for himself in front of a lane."

Tarin said nothing at all. He had a butt to fill at six and a magistrate had just declined, correctly and in front of nine people, to use his name as a thing to pull with, and he could not think what to do with a man being decent to him in daylight.

"Thursday, six," Wick said, from the coping. "Then I am there at five with a hod and a bar, and I will not tell him he is welcome and I will not tell him he is late."

---

At Nether Sallow that evening Tarin filled the butt at six o'clock, forty trips, an hour and sixteen minutes, with Nesta's pail and the roll of tarred twine wound twice on the handle. The boy counted out loud to forty and Nesta marked the shutter *full, Tuesday the third of next month, seventy-third day*. Above the plank the shutter carried the week's figures — a hundred and ninety, a hundred and ninety, a hundred and ninety, never divided — and beside them, in Wick's hand, the drain: down two feet since Friday the thirtieth and still falling, and the row's pump drawing, and the well clearing, and the frame on the culvert post ninety years old and empty.

The drain, Cobb had said on the lane, was the sole discharge of the Reach. It was true. Item one of the schedule of 1902 said it and item one stood as printed, and it had been printed a hundred and thirty years, and it had been true every day of that time, and on the third of next month it had a bank on it that four people had made good for nothing and that a clerk would charge them for.

He could feel the whole of it from the wall. Four miles two chains of drain from the plank down to the Sere, the outfall two miles below, the launder above it empty and chained, the tank above that standing at six and an eighth with no rate in it and none to come, and the row at the end of the drain with a stone face and a clay seam and six doors in a district that had not existed on Friday, and one pound of work to be valued, and a valuation that would be made in a year by a man who would not stay the night.

It was all true and it was all checkable, and there was not one part of it he could have told anybody on Tuesday morning that he had not found out by standing in a lane in daylight with his boots on.

That was the difference, and it was the whole of the difference, and it had been the whole of the difference since Monday the twenty-sixth, and a man could stand at a wall at six in the evening with his palm on cold stone and be exactly as useful as a man with a rule.

"Saturday," the boy said, with the slate under his arm. "Six in the morning. That is a working morning."

"That is a working morning," Tarin said, "and I will be forty minutes late to it, because the butt is filled at six and it is filled at six every evening, and there is no form in this basin in which a man may stop doing one of them to do the other."

"You could fill the butt at four."

"I could," Tarin said. "And then I would be filling it in the dark at four in the morning forty times instead of once at six in the evening in the light, and a butt filled in the dark by a man with one good shoulder takes nearer two hours than one hour and sixteen minutes, and at the end of it I would be no use to a bank in the light either." He set the pail down on the wall. "Write that on your slate. Not in a book. On your slate, for a year, and then take it off."

The boy wrote it, and did not read it out, because some things a man wants kept and does not want published, and the boy was fifteen and had worked that out on his own.
