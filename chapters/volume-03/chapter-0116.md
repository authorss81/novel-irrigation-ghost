# Chapter 0116 — What Sella Asked For

On the Sunday morning, the eighth of next month, the pump at Cinder Ford far bank was taken apart in the lane and the well at the end of it was bailed dry by hand, and neither job made anything anybody could put in a book, and both of them took the whole day.

Sella Marr had asked for six things on the Tuesday the twenty-seventh of the month before last, in her own kitchen, with a cup of broth in front of a man she had met twice. She did not have them written down and she did not need to be prompted.

"The pump is washed," she said, counting on her fingers with the handle of a barrow in the other hand. "The well is bailed. The hens are in the third door, and they have been since Sunday the first, and that one is done. My daughter's boy drinks butt water and not lane water, and that one is done because he has never once asked for anything else. Six doors on a form, and that is done, and it was done on Wednesday by a man with a soft pen, and I read it out of his mouth before he had finished and I made him read it again." She put her hand up. "And the sixth."

"You said the sixth was written down first whatever the number turned out to be," Tarin said. "Six doors stood wet and stayed."

"That is the sixth," Sella said, "and I have been carrying it round four doors for a week waiting for a form with a box in it, and there is not one, and there is never going to be one, because I asked a woman in a city office on the Wednesday the twenty-eighth of the month before last and she put a name in a box and did not put a count in a box, because she had not been given one, and she was right not to. So there is no box, and I have been asking the wrong question for a week and I am asking the right one now."

"What is the right question," Harker said. He had come over with a stick and a watch and no instruction, because a warden comes on a Sunday when a lane has asked him to.

"Whether a note can be got into a file that is not a count," Sella said. "I do not want to be counted. I want to be on a piece of paper that says we stood in water for eleven days and nobody came and told us to go, and I want it said in the same office that has the count in it, because in about four years the count will be in a column and there will be a clerk who reads the column, and a clerk who reads the column will not know there was any water at all."

Harker took out his book on the coping of the pump.

"I can do it as a note on the district file," he said, "and I will read it out. And I will tell you now what reading it out gets you, so that you are not disappointed on Friday. It will not be in the return. It will not be in the column. It will not be counted with anything, and in about four years a clerk laying out the district will pull the return and not the note, because the return is the return and that is the whole of what it is for. I have been doing this twenty-two years and the true thing about it has never once been popular."

"Then do it and tell me the true thing," Sella said, "and I will find a second place for it, because I have learned in a week that a thing in one place is a thing in one place."

Perdie Ammid came over at eleven with a sheet and no book, and wrote it out and read it back twice, and the reading back was the whole of why she had come. *Note entered on the district file of the lower Sere warden at the request of S. Marr, third door, resident nineteen years: the six doors of this row stood in water in the lane from the night of the twenty-second of the month before last to the morning of the first of next month, being eleven days by the count of the resident, and no person was removed and no person was told to leave, and the bread that came over the ford came without any form, and the water that came was out of a drain the office has not charged this row for. Six doors stood wet and stayed. The resident asks that it be written that they stayed.*

"That is me and not a clerk," Sella said, when it was read back. "The resident asks. Put that in. If it says the office notes, then it is the office being kind, and the office is not being kind, the office is doing a job."

"It says the resident asks," Perdie said, "in her words, and I have not changed a syllable and I have written *asked* and not *states*, because a woman who asks can be a woman who was refused, and I would rather the paper had that in it."

"Thirty-five," Perdie said, writing the number at the top, and then stopped, and looked at the sheet, and said, "Thirty-six. Thirty-five went down on Saturday with the works. Thirty-six is this. It is on a different paper from the tally and it is in a different book in a different building and it will not be counted with anything, and that is the sixth thing done and it is done in the worst possible way, and I am going to say that to the shop at Brant on Friday and I am not going to enjoy it."

"Do not enjoy it on my account," Sella said. "It is written."

---

The pump came apart at about eleven and went back together at four.

The head came off with two spanners and a cold wrench and a good deal of language from Wyn Marr, who had a barrow's worth of the language in him. The barrel came up four feet and the leather was gone to a ring of black slime, and the standing part was furred with six weeks of drain water and smelled of it, and Jess took the whole thing to the ford and left it there for the current, and Corrie scraped the barrel inside with a piece of tin until you could see daylight through the seam of the scrape, and Mrs Trew cut a new washer out of the heel of a boot that had not been wanted since the winter, and it was a bad washer and it would do for a year, and the handle was freed and greased with tallow off the top of the range, and the pump was put back and worked and gave brown water for a quarter of an hour and then water you could see the bottom of a pail in.

"It is not drinking," Corrie said. "Do not let anybody tell a child it is drinking."

"It is clean," Sella said, "and clean is what I asked for on the twenty-seventh and clean is what I have got, and I know the difference between clean and drinking at sixty years of age."

They bailed the well from noon. That is three hours of four people on a windlass with a rope and a bucket, and the water that came up the first hour was the colour of tea and had to go over the side onto the grass, and the second hour it was the colour of the Sere, and the third hour it came up nearly clear and they kept going anyway, because nobody in that lane had a reason to stop that anybody could explain, and then the bucket was coming up with air in it and the rope was slack and there was nothing left in the well but a hole.

Sella said the rest of it to nobody in particular.

"Nineteen years I have wanted that well empty once. Not dry — empty. You cannot see to the bottom of it and you cannot get a pail into it and you cannot find out what is wrong with it, and a man from the city once stood at the end of this lane in a coat and never came down it. That is what I wanted. A hole I could see the bottom of." She looked down the shaft, at the last of the daylight going in it. "There is no box on any form in the world that says *a woman got to see the bottom of her own well*."

Corrie put the bucket on the coping and wiped his forehead with his wrist. "Put it on the form I do not have," he said, "and put it on the slate at the shop, and if a man wants to argue with it he can come and see the well."

That was the whole of the Sunday. The Trews went home at three, Jess carried the boy up and down all afternoon with a hand on the windlass because the boy wanted the noise, and the water in the pump came up clean and the water in the well came up nearly clear, and both had to be boiled tomorrow, and neither of them was anywhere near a rating.

Tarin was there all day and was no use to anybody in it.

He had offered his hand to the well at about half past eleven, and Sella had refused it in front of the lane.

"No," she said. "Not that, and not on the pump. I have watched you put your hand on a wall and tell me what a lane four miles off is doing, and I do not doubt you, and I have no use for you down a hole. A well is a hole. Put your hand on the pump frame if you want to touch a thing, and be warned that a pump is a machine and I can argue with a machine."

He put his hand on the pump frame. It was iron, and cold, and wet, and it told him nothing whatever at all, not one foot, not one drop, not one thing. Four miles two chains of drain, two miles and four chains of empty launder, a tank standing at six and an eighth with no rate in it, and a hand on a cast-iron frame at the end of a lane four miles off, learning nothing.

"You cannot find your way in a pump," Corrie Lille said, from the windlass, not looking round, "whatever you can find in a wall, and I have been saying so since the twenty-seventh, and it is a Sunday and I am not going to be unkind about it. I would like to be permitted to say that I told you, and then let it lie."

"Permitted," Tarin said.

And that was the second time in three days that a lane had refused to be carried by him, and both refusals had been correct, and one of them had been made kindly and one of them not, and the difference between them was not in him.

At the plank at Nether Sallow that evening, seventy-eighth day, the butt took an hour and nine minutes because the shoulder was better than it had been and the water was warm.

"Near the notch, in the dark, at four in the morning," the boy said, "on Saturday."

"Near the notch, in the dark, at four in the morning, on Saturday," Tarin agreed. "Write it on your slate and do not put it in a book. And now write something else on it, which is the question I am going to ask you, and you are going to answer it badly."

"What question."

"Whether the figures should go in the frame."

The boy stopped counting. He had been counting for four hundred yards without being asked, because a boy of fifteen who counts trips has found the only arrangement in this valley that nobody argues with, and he did not like being asked a question in the middle of it.

"No," he said.

"Write *no* and read it out, and then tell me why, and if you have not got a why, put the slate down and think about it while you count, because you have forty trips in front of you."

The boy wrote *no*, and read it out, and then thought about it for about nine trips, and then said:

"Because a frame is a frame that somebody puts a notice in, and a notice is for doing something to somebody. And there is nothing anybody has to do about that plank, and the hundred and ninety is not an order, and I do not want to be the first one in ninety years to nail anything up there that a man could come and take a bar to."

"That is a whole answer," Tarin said. "Say it at the plank and Nesta will keep it."

"I am not saying it at the plank."

"No," Tarin said. "That is the second half of it and you worked it out on your own in a minute, and you will keep both."

Nesta was at the top of the bank with the chalk, and she had not been asked, and she said the one sentence that was in her to say about it, and she said it to the boy and not to the man, because a woman of forty-one has spent thirty-two years finding out which of the two is worth talking to.

"There has been nothing in that frame since the second of the month before last, when six of us took a card off it in daylight and turned it over and cut the tin out of it, and the frame went back up because a frame is ninety years old and a card is six. It said CLOSED. No water. No entry. Under penalty. And it named no office and named no person, and it was the reason this lane's water was a secret for six years." She wrote *full* on the shutter and the date under it and did not divide anything. "A frame is for a notice a person can be charged under. There is nothing in this lane anybody can be charged under yet, and I would not know how to word it if there were, and neither would you, and the day I can word it is the day somebody is going to want to be served out of it. So it stays empty. Ninety more years."

The boy wrote that on his slate too, and it stood on the slate for eleven days and came off the slate in the ordinary way, and the frame stood on the culvert post above the lane with nothing in it, ninety years old, and would still be standing there empty on Thursday the twelfth of next month, when a form was nailed to a bar at the far bank instead.
