# Chapter 0110 — Persons Accounted For

On the Monday morning, the second of next month, the flood court sat at Vellum in a room with tall windows, and Cael Dorrin laid *persons accounted for* before it, because the deadline was somebody else's and Monday was the day.

He laid them the way a magistrate lays things: in order, with dates. The warden's report of Monday the twenty-sixth. The certificate of Wednesday the twenty-eighth, by M. Quill, six households, return noted not reconciled. The sealing order of Thursday the twenty-ninth, two doors, S. Marr third door nineteen years. The carpenter's report of Friday the thirtieth, gap in the bank, coats holding, wave down the drain. The valley sheets of Saturday the thirty-first: shutter figures, staff, stick, drain falling. The flood book sheet: last gathering cut out, stubs dated, pencil line fourteen years old, no signature, no keeper, no return. The Sunday report: lane dry underfoot between the doors, bank foot eased into the drain by four persons with a rope and a willow, pump standing, well head standing, sixth door standing.

"Eleven persons, including one infant of nine months," Dorrin said to the court. "All accounted for. Two doors sealed Saturday evening per order. Lane dry underfoot Sunday. Bank made good in part by residents with shared risk." He paused. "The seal stands until the bank is viewed, but I ask the court to note that the residents held the bank themselves Sunday with a man from Nether Sallow, at their own request, and the ground holds this Monday morning."

The court noted it. The court lifted the seal on the two doors pending the bank view, ordered bread and cartage paid, ordered the tally of persons by name to proceed if willing, and ordered the flood book stubs and pencil line to the Memory Office for provenance: whose hand, what batch of ink, what date, and whether any return was ever recorded.

Dorrin bowed to the court, which sat in three persons with a clerk writing, and said the last thing he would say in that room for a long time: "The plan showed drain bank and foreshore, and the ground showed six doors. The plan was not a lie. Nobody went and looked. I went and looked Thursday, and Friday the bank gave way under my own seal, and Sunday four persons held it with a rope and a willow at their own request. The record is right about the water and wrong about the valley, and the valley is now on the plan. That is the whole of my report, on Monday the second of next month."

The presiding member, a woman of sixty with a chain of office, looked at him over her papers. "Magistrate. The deadline was mine, not yours. The flood court thanks you for persons accounted for. Eleven persons, one infant. The tally proceeds if willing. See that willing stays willing."

"It will," Dorrin said. "S. Marr third door nineteen years asked me to write her name right on a sealing order. Willing is not the difficulty. The difficulty is what countable costs. That is not my sentence. That is what the valley taught me Friday with two coats in a bank." That was the procedure. Dorrin had a sentence he believed — *what is recognised is real* — and Monday the court recognised eleven people, and the bundle would never say *no inhabitants* again without leaving a mark.

Afterwards, in the corridor, Mara Quill stopped him. She was forty-eight, with a drawer holding a sheet she had not published, and she had been competent in a flood the way she had never been competent in a drought, and the danger was in her drawer.

"Magistrate," she said. "Monday the second. The tally proceeds if willing. Willing is doing a great deal of work in that sentence."

"It is," Dorrin said. "A place that is countable can be sealed, and rated, and moved. I sealed two doors to protect a boundary and the boundary gave way under the seal. I am right about the record and wrong about the valley, and the valley held its own bank Sunday while I was writing *persons accounted for*." He held his plan case with both hands. The case was scuffed from the lane water Friday, and one of the clasps had rusted overnight. "Your certificate made the sealing possible, officer. That is not an accusation. That is what a correct paper does. It sends men with bars."

"I know," Mara said. "I signed it Wednesday. I publish the damage with it. That is the whole of my office." She hesitated. "Magistrate, the tally. If willing. The woman in the third door asked what willing costs. What do I tell her, when she asks me."

"Tell her a name in a box is a door and a bill at once," Dorrin said. "Tell her the court pays bread and cartage and the tally takes names only from mouths that offer them. Tell her—" He stopped, a man of fifty with a sentence he believed and a Friday in his boots. "Tell her the magistrate who sealed her door stood in water to the thigh holding a coat into her bank, and the seal is lifted, and the bank view comes with bread, not bars. That is not procedure. That is what I will say if she asks me, which she will, because she asked me to write her name right and I did."

"Thursday the twenty-ninth," Mara said. "S. Marr, third door, resident nineteen years. The first time in nineteen years. It was on a sealing order."

"The next time it will be on a tally, if willing," Dorrin said. "That is the difference between my office and a habit. A habit copies nobody nineteen years. My office writes Sella Marr until Sella Marr says otherwise." He bowed, a small precise movement with a rusted case in his hands. "Monday the second, officer. Persons accounted for. Eleven. That is the whole of what I carried out of a wet lane."

They parted in the corridor, a magistrate with a procedure and an officer with a drawer, and neither was a villain and neither was rescued.

The letter came down the road Monday afternoon on the cart, four days from Bexhill, in Iven Marrow's own hand, one side, five lines, and Perdie Ammid read it standing up in the shop at Brant with about nine people in the room, because a thing in a hand that is not read out is a thing in a hand.

> *Mrs Ammid. Your sheets 31-33 came inside the valley bundle and I have read them. The flood book is not mine and the pencil is not mine and fourteen years is not my bench. Keep your sheets. Price unchanged. Bench Tuesday. — I. Marrow.*

Nobody thanked him and nobody was asked to. He gained nothing. He was four days away. His price had not moved. Perdie put the letter in the chest with the three-line unfinished one begun on the sixteenth, and closed the lid.

"He may appear once in his own hand," she said to the room, "and he has appeared, and he is not thanked, and he is not the man who explains anything. The flood book is not his. Fourteen years is not his bench. That is the whole of him on Monday the second."

Bess Ilk, behind the counter, spoke up for the first time that afternoon. "Mrs Ammid. Thirty-three sheets. No book. The column in a cart with nothing at the foot of it and a number due in about a year. When does the number come."

"In about a year," Perdie said. "Thirty-four and a half days in seven lines, and the foot empty, and a counting-house at Vellum will look at it and ask for the foot." She closed the satchel. "Four and a half, five and a half, four and a half, one, twelve, five and a half, a day and a half. That is what exists anywhere. Everything else is in a cart. I have said that five times this month and I am counting, and Monday the second I say it a sixth time, because a figure with nothing at the foot of it is a debt, and debts do not go away for being carried in carts."

"And the letter," Bess said. "Three lines, unfinished, not sent. With the fortnight long past."

"The letter is three lines in a chest," Perdie said. "It was begun on the sixteenth and unfinished on the twenty-third with four days left, and the days are gone. It must not be resolved in any opening movement, and it will not be, because a woman of fifty-four who has written to a man four times in thirty-one years does not learn a new letter in a week, and does not finish an old one the day after a court sits." She picked up the satchel. "That is the whole of it. Thirty-three sheets go to Vellum in the morning. The chest stays. Good evening."

"Good evening," the room said, about nine voices, and nobody thanked her, and she did not ask.

At Nether Sallow that Monday evening, Tarin filled the butt at six o'clock, forty trips, about an hour and sixteen minutes, with Nesta's pail and the twine on the handle. It was Monday the second of next month. He was on his seventy-second day in the valley. The butt filled. The boy counted to forty. Nesta marked the shutter *full, Monday the second*.

The boy lingered after Nesta went up, with his slate under his arm, the way he lingered every evening now, because fifteen is an age for lingering where work is done.

"Mr Rusk," he said. "It is Monday the second. Seventy-two days. The court sat. The seal is lifted. The lane is dry. The book has stubs. What happens now."

"Now the tally proceeds if willing," Tarin said, tipping the thirty-ninth pail in. "The bank is viewed. The flood book goes to the Memory Office for provenance. Perdie's thirty-three sheets go to Vellum. The tank stands with no rate. The flume stands chained. The third rod stands empty." He lifted the fortieth pail. "And a man fills a butt every evening at six until the ground gives a crop or the winter does. That is what happens now. One trip at a time."

"And your hand," the boy said. "Sunday four people said *we want it* and it worked. Monday eleven people in a room would not act on it. Which is it."

Tarin tipped the last pail in and stood with his hands on the rim of the full butt, breathing through his mouth. Two hundred and forty gallons. A day and a half of drinking for three people.

"Both," he said. "That is the whole of Bend, and there is no name for it and I give it none. A hand that knows without showing is nothing anybody can act on, and a hand that works with three residents and a rope is everything. Monday's room was right. Sunday's bank was right. The difference was never in my hand." He looked at the boy. "About nine people in this valley spent a month telling me a name is negotiated and not given. They were right about that too. Water is negotiated. Forty trips is negotiated. Write that down and do not put it in a book for about a year."

The boy nodded, and did not take the slate out, because some things are counted out loud and not written, and this was one.

The lane at Cinder Ford lay dry underfoot between six doors. Two doors stood unsealed by court order pending the bank view. Eleven persons were accounted for. Sella Marr's name was on a sealing order with nineteen years beside it. The flood book sat in a record house with its last pages cut out and a pencil line fourteen years old with no name. Mara's drawer held a sheet about a closure with eleven names not given, unpublished, kept. The tank stood at two feet five and seven-eighths with no rate and none to be given. The flume sluice stood shut and chained on a new chain. The third rod stood empty with a padlock printed under it and a new line with a heading and nothing under it.

Wick came down to the wall at dusk with mud still under his nails from Sunday's bank. "Tuesday tomorrow," he said. "Bread Thursday. Stick Sunday. Bank Sunday. Court Monday the second. That is anybody's figures, five days in a row, and every one checkable: Tuesday the bread, Thursday the washer, Sunday the stick and the rope, Monday the persons. A week with five true things in it. That has not happened in this valley in six years."

"That is anybody's," Tarin said, tipping the fortieth pail in.

"Warden says the drain is down two feet since Friday. Sella's hens are back in the third door. The boy is nine months old and teething." Wick looked at the full butt. "Monday the second. Seventy-two days. Fourteen weeks run to the sixth of the month after next. That is the sixth, and the fourteenth week runs through it."

Tarin put the pail down. "A man is going to do that every evening now," he said quietly. "There is a day when somebody has to say his name."

"Tonight nobody has to," Wick said. "Tonight there is a butt and a pail and twine, and six doors dry where a paper said nobody, and a book with part of it gone and nobody writing down they took it." He picked up his coat. "Good night, Mr Rusk."

"Good night."

Wick went up the lane. Nesta went with the boy. Tarin stayed at the wall with his hand flat on the low stone, feeling two miles and four chains of empty launder and four miles two chains of falling drain and six doors dry at the end of it, with no way of giving any of it back, and no form for going anywhere, and a butt full behind him that would be empty by morning.

Below him the plank ran at a hundred and ninety in the dark, the way it had run six years under a notice that said no water. Above him the tank stood at five and seven-eighths with no rate. Between them the flume stood empty and chained on a new chain bought with a man's own money. At the culvert the card stood with the third rod empty and a padlock printed under it and a new line with a heading and nothing under it. In a record house the flood book stood with stubs and a pencil line. In a drawer at Vellum a sheet stood unpublished under brass weights. In a bundle eleven persons stood accounted for where nineteen years said nobody. Every paper held its place. The only thing that moved was a man with a pail, forty trips an evening, until the ground gives a crop or the winter does.

He took his hand off the wall, picked up the pail, and went down to the cottage at Brant, on Monday the second of next month, and he could not leave, and the water had gone somewhere he could not follow it to, and eleven people were accounted for where nineteen years said nobody.

The cottage at Brant stood at no rent with his coat on the hook and the folding rule in it and the watch in the pocket, and he set the pail by the door with the twine on the handle, wound twice and tucked under, where it had been since the Saturday morning it came off a stake at a culvert so long ago now. Seventy-two days. Fourteen weeks to the sixth of the month after next, the fourteenth week running through it. Five shillings a day, six days. A butt every evening at six. A name in two books four hundred yards apart. A hand that knew two miles and four chains with no way of giving it back. Six doors dry. A book with stubs. A sheet in a drawer. Eleven persons where nobody had been. That was the whole of Monday the second, and it was enough, and it was not finished.
