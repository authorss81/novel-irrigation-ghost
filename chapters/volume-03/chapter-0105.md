# Chapter 0105 — The Box That Wants a Name

On the Wednesday morning, the twenty-eighth of this month, Mara Quill sat at her desk in the Municipal Memory Office at Vellum and filled in a certificate about a flood, because a flood is the one thing a records office can act on, and she was good at it, and that was the danger.

She was forty-eight, an officer of a city office and not a neutral person, and she had not been one since she was twenty-one, when she first learned to read a hand by the lean of a downstroke and understood that every record in the basin was written by somebody with a reason. She had entered a row number for a district that had none, with her own name on the form, and a paragraph in four buildings said it was for a count and not for a supply. A row number makes a place countable, and countable is not the same as served. She knew that better than anybody, because she had written it.

The desk in front of her held three things that morning. The first was the quarterly return of the far-bank district at Cinder Ford, sent up by the tally office with the year's bundle, with nineteen years of the same line in the same column: *no inhabitants*. Nineteen years, in seven hands, the earliest faded brown and the latest fresh black, all saying the same thing about six doors and a pump that a woman had lived in nineteen years and was never in a return.

The second was a report from the lower Sere warden, dated Monday the twenty-sixth, two days coming up the road: the long drain running full to the bank, water standing in a lane on the far bank past Cinder Ford, six households affected, pump under water, well under water, two butts for six doors. The warden had done his job the way wardens do it, with a stick and a watch: lane water to the top of a boot, drain full four miles, no injury to persons, one hen-coop lost. He asked for instruction, because a warden records what he can see and asks what he cannot decide.

The third was a form. A Fair Copy certificate of inhabited place, printed, with boxes for district, parish, row number, households, persons, source of supply, and keeper, and a line at the foot for the certifying officer's name. The box headed *row number* was empty. The box headed *name* wanted a name for a place that had none, because the return said no inhabitants and the warden said six households and both were in front of her and they could not both be true.

Mara read all three twice, the way she had been taught: first for what was written, then for the pressure of what was under it. The return was a copy of a copy, nineteen years deep, in court-supply ink from dated batches, each year's hand copying the last. **Nobody had gone and looked. Every hand had done its job honestly, and the job was honest, and that was the whole of the defect. It was not a lie, and she had spent eleven years learning that the two are not the same thing. A lie can be caught. A habit can only be broken, and there is nobody in that district whose duty it is to break it, and that is why nineteen years is not a mistake anybody made and is still a thing that happened.**

She took up her pen and began the certificate, because that is what a records office does when water stands where a paper says nobody lives. She entered the district from the warden's report, and the parish from the tally office's index, and for the row number she entered the number she had entered before for a district that had none, with her own name beside it, because she had standing for a count and not for a supply and the paragraph in four buildings said so.

For households she entered *six, reported*. For persons she entered *unknown, estimated eleven including one infant of nine months, reported*. For source of supply she entered *pump and well, both under water at date of report, two butts*.

For the name she stopped, with the pen over the paper.

The place had no name in any return. The people in it called it the far-bank row, or Cinder Ford past the ford, or the third door. Sella Marr had lived nineteen years in the third door and kept hens. Jess Marr had been born in it. Corrie Lille mended nets in the sixth. A row with six doors and a pump and nineteen years of hens was not a place with no name. It was a place with no name anybody had written down.

Mara wrote, in the box headed *name*: *Cinder Ford far-bank row, so called by residents, reported*.

Then she wrote the certifying line, in her own hand, with the date under it: Wednesday the twenty-eighth, by M. Quill, officer of this office, on the report of the lower Sere warden dated the twenty-sixth, the return of the tally office being noted as saying *no inhabitants* for nineteen years and not reconciled, tally of persons by name to follow Monday the second of next month if willing.

She read it over once and blotted it. It was competent. It was exactly what the office was for: when water stands where a paper says nobody lives, somebody writes down that water stands there, with a date, so that no later hand can copy *no inhabitants* a twentieth year without leaving a mark.

You can leave a district at a door. You cannot leave a bill. Somebody in a valley had said that, in a room, and it had gone onto one of Perdie Ammid's numbered sheets with the word *copy* at the top, and Mara had underlined it in the margin of her own copy of that sheet without asking herself why, and the margin had sat there ever since, and she had never once looked at it again.

She signed it anyway, because not signing it left six doors under water with no paper at all, and a lane with no paper is a lane nobody has to look at. Then she sat a while with the pen in her hand, forty-eight years old, thinking what it meant to be good at floods. In a drought she had been helpless: every paper said shortage and no paper could make water, and her refusal to certify a discharge as a supply had cost her valley nothing and gained it nothing but truth. In a flood her papers moved magistrates, and magistrates moved bars. She had signed a piece of paper at ten o'clock in the morning and by Monday a man with a carpenter was going to nail one across a door in a lane two hundred miles off, and the only reason he would be there at all was the name in her box.

---

In the afternoon she took the certificate down to the tally office herself, instead of sending it by the room boy, because she wanted to ask the question the warden's report had raised and nobody had answered: who has been filling the form in.

The tally office was a long room with tall windows and desks in rows, smelling of dust and gum, and the woman at the far-bank desk was about thirty-five, with ink on her fingers and a bundle of returns in front of her tied with tape.

"I am Mara Quill, of the Memory Office," Mara said. "I have certified six households at Cinder Ford far bank on a warden's report. Your return says no inhabitants, nineteen years. I am not disputing it. I want to know whose hand I am reading."

The woman untied the bundle. Nineteen returns, nineteen years, each with the line. She laid the last five out.

"This one is mine," she said, touching the latest. "Four years. Before that, Hanne Ilk's, six years, retired. That is ten of the nineteen. The other nine are in five hands that came before both of us and I could not put a name to one of them if you paid me." She lifted the faded one. "The earliest is before my time and it is the brownest thing in the drawer. The instruction on the bundle is *copy forward unless reported otherwise*. Every year the tallyman brings the boatmen's count and the drain bank report, and if neither names the row, I copy forward."

"Has any tallyman ever named it," Mara asked.

"Not in my four years. The boats count men, not doors. The drain bank report says *bank sound, no dwelling affected* every year until this week." The woman looked at the certificate. "You have given it a name."

"So called by residents, reported," Mara said. "It is not a naming. It is a note that people call it something."

"It will be copied," the tally woman said. "Next year, and the year after. A name in a box is a name, whatever word is beside it. In four years a clerk will put your name and this name in a column and the name will be the record." She said it without hostility, the way clerks say true things. "You have done your job. I am telling you what your job does."

"I know," Mara said. "I entered a row number for a district that had none, last month, with my own name on the form. A row number makes a place countable. Countable is not the same as served. I wrote that too, in four buildings."

The tally woman tied the bundle up again, with the certificate on top of it. "Then you know," she said. "Wednesday the twenty-eighth. It is in the bundle."

Mara walked back up the hill to the Memory Office in the afternoon light. The board on the terrace road at Cresset was still up, with the drop in two figures and the reason they differed, and nine hundred and forty cisterns filling more slowly for about a day and a half, and nobody able to say by how much. She had been down to read it out on the Friday and had not been back since. The clerk who types that board had told her that morning that a man of about sixty had stood in front of it a while and read both figures twice, slowly, the way the boy of twenty-two had described a man doing, and had shaken his head and gone inside. Nobody had signed either figure, because there was nobody in that district who could be made to sign anything, and that was still the truest thing anybody had told anybody about that city in eight weeks.

She did not go up to her desk. She went down instead, to the low store under the reading room where the district returns were kept by the quarter, and asked the storekeeper for the Cinder Ford file, and the woman said it was nineteen years and would be a heavy armful, and Mara said she would carry it herself.

She carried it up in two trips and set it on the reading table under the tall window, and she took the nineteen returns out in order and laid them in a row, and she counted the hands. Seven. The two earliest were faded brown and the pen had gone through in two places. The nine after them were in five hands she could not name, and one of those five had put a blot across the line and had not wanted to erase it, because you can see where a nib went and stopped. Then six years of a heavier hand that went at a slant, and then four years of the same woman and the same tidy, unhurried, entirely correct hand, one line, and the word *no* with a full stop after it, four years running.

Nobody had ever gone and looked. Every hand had done its job honestly, and the job was to copy forward. She put the returns back in order and bound them with a tape of her own, and wrote on the spine in pencil, in her own hand, the district, the quarter, and the fact that the file had been opened by her on the twenty-eighth of this month and had not been opened before. That was the first mark that file had carried in nineteen years. It was not on any form. It was in pencil, and she did not mind, because pencil is what a conservator reaches for when she is not yet sure the paper will outlast her.

At her desk she took out a second sheet and began a letter to the lower Sere warden, in her own hand: *send a tally of persons by name, if willing, and of butts and supply, on Monday the second of next month, and note the pump and the well daily until dry underfoot*. She dated it Wednesday the twenty-eighth and signed it.

Before she sealed it, the room boy brought up the afternoon bundle: three valley sheets, come early by hand of a carter who had ridden through the night. Numbered sheets with *copy* at the top, in Perdie Ammid's hand. Shutter figures for the week, never divided. Tank staff readings, falling and standing. And a stick reading from Sunday the twenty-fifth, taken by Wick Venn at Cinder Ford: lane water to the top of a boot, pump under water, two butts for six doors, and eleven persons counted at six doors, one of them an infant of nine months and one of them a man on the water to Verrin who is counted off his own door hook.

Mara read the stick reading three times. A man of thirty-eight with one hand had gone over a ford on a Sunday with a washer in his pocket and come back with bread's arithmetic: two butts is tonight and tomorrow morning. No form. No seal. No name in a box. Just a stick and a step and anybody able to check it.

She took out her day-book and wrote under the morning's line: *Stick reading Sunday the twenty-fifth received Wednesday: lane to the top of a boot. My certificate says six households reported. The stick says the same in wood. Two different hands, same water.*

Then she did the thing she had been taught not to do at twenty-one and had done anyway ever since: she wrote what the record cost, in plain words, on the back of the tally slip, for no bundle. *Nineteen years of nobody in that row and not one cart went over the ford. Water has been there since Friday and the first cart went over on Tuesday, and it was bread and not a form. A name in a box will be copied four years, and in four years a clerk will put my name and this name in a column and the name will be the record. That is what my job does, and I do it anyway, because a lane with no paper is a lane nobody has to look at.*

She blotted it and put it under the brass weights, with the tracing and the nineteen-year line. Three papers in a drawer, none of them in any bundle, all of them true.

Then she took out a third sheet, and on it she copied, for her own drawer and not for any bundle, the nineteen-year line in all seven hands as nearly as she could render them, with the dates under each, because a conservator who does not keep her own tracing of what she has changed is a hand nobody can check. She put it under the tray of brass weights in her drawer, where the unlogged drawing had once been, and locked the drawer.

She had published nothing yet. The certificate would go out with the Thursday bundle, and a copy of it would lie on the warden's district file before the warden got back to it. But the damage was already in it, the way a mark is in a wall before anybody reads it: she had done her job, and her job had a name in a box, and a name in a box is the first thing a magistrate needs.

She wrote the date at the foot of her day-book — Wednesday the twenty-eighth — and under it one line: *Cinder Ford far bank certified six households, return noted not reconciled, tally to follow Monday*.
