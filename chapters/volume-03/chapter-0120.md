# Chapter 0120 — At Each Door

On the Thursday morning, the twelfth of next month, the district of Cinder Ford far-bank row went into the district book, and Cobb read the rate out in the lane once at the ford end and then again at each of the six doors, because a notice read in a public place is a notice a public place has heard and a notice read at a door is a notice a door has heard, and those are two different things and only one of them is the law.

He had the stiff cover and a second clerk and the form of the twelfth, and a carpenter.

The lane had been dry underfoot between the doors for eleven days. The pump drew. The well came up nearly clear and had to be boiled and everybody had boiled it. There were eleven people in six doors and the form said so and said where every one of them had come from, and there was a man in the lane that morning who had not been in it for a month.

Harl Bray had come back on the Wednesday, three days short of a run, off the water to Verrin, with a sea-coat and a box of line and a face that had been in the sun. He was the man Corrie Lille had counted off his own door's hook on the Sunday the twenty-fifth of the month before last, and nobody in the lane had seen him since, and he was in the fifth door at eight in the morning helping his mother carry a bucket, because Jess Marr had put a boy on the flour boat on the Monday with a folded paper in his hand and no name on it except a door number, and a boatman had carried it up to Verrin and a boatman had brought back a man. Ada Bray looked at him for a while and then said:

"Eight days. Corrie counted you off the hook on the Sunday before last, and there has been a flood and a magistrate and a rate in that lane since, and a boy came up on Monday with a piece of folded paper in his hand that had a number on it and not a name, and I have not once been able to work out who sent for me."

"I sent for you," Jess Marr said from the step of the fourth door, with the boy on her hip. "And I wrote the number and not your name, because the number is on the form and your name is not, and I did not know that till Wednesday and I would not have cared."

"I say well," Harl said. "I have not had a vote and I should not have known what to do with one."

"Then I shall not have to write that I am the only one here," Ada said, "which is a great relief, because I asked to have it written that way on the Wednesday and I meant it more than I have ever meant anything, and now there is a man in my kitchen and I shall have to think of a different true thing."

Cobb read the form at the ford end, all of it, standing on the dry ground with the clerks holding the cover, and about eleven people heard every word of a district entering a book for the first time in nineteen years.

*District: Cinder Ford far-bank row, so called by residents, reported. Row number as entered. Six doors. Eleven persons, of whom nine are named; one name is withheld by the person himself, recorded unwilling and not argued with; and one person was absent at the taking and is not named for him. Entered in the district book of the lower Sere this twelfth day of next month. Work of improvement: bank at lane end, making-good to the line, executed on the seventh, materials four shillings and eightpence, labour eleven shillings and sixpence, total nineteen shillings and twopence, charged to the district and carried by the office until the rate is struck. The rate will be struck at the next valuation. The valuation has not been made. Date in the coping stone: the seventh of next month.*

Then he read it at each door.

At the first door, the widow's, Cobb read the lines and she said: "Is that on me or on the house."

"The door," Cobb said. "It is always the door. If you sell the door the rate goes with it and the new person stands it. If you go and live at your sister's the door stands empty and stands it. That is the whole of a rate and it is the reason it is not a punishment, and I am telling you the other half of it as well, which is that a door with a rate on it can be sold for the rate, and that is the half nobody prints."

At the second door, Jess Marr's, Wyn Marr was in the lane with a barrow and did not put it down.

"The boy," he said. "Does the boy stand it."

"The boy is on the return at eleven months and is named, and the rate is on the door, so the boy is a ratepayer in the sense that a name is against a door. And he will be on a valuation one day, which is the thing you are about to say."

"The valuation is the thing I object to," Wyn Marr said. "It will be made by a man who comes through, and there is nobody in this district who can object to that man except me, and I have a barrow, and a barrow is not a ratepayer. So what is the point of being in a district, if the only thing in it that can answer the man with the book is me, and I answer him with a barrow."

Cobb thought about it a while, with the sun on the open form.

"You can answer, and that is new. Three days ago there was nothing on any paper in this basin with your row on it and the man with the book would have had no occasion to come. Now there is, and he will have to come, and you can stand in front of him and say the ground is foreshore and the drainage is a discharge, and he will not have thought of it." He shut the cover on the pen. "And you will not be able to stop it. I have not found a way to give you one without the other, and I would rather say that now than let you find out at a door in a year."

He read it at the third door, which was Sella Marr's own and had a new lintel on it that the carpenter had done on the Sunday, and Sella came out with her hands wet from the trough.

"Stop there, Mr Cobb," she said, "before you read it. I have one question and I would like it answered before the paper, because afterwards you will be finished and I will be finished and we will both have to go and do something."

"Ask."

"You laid stone to a line on the seventh of next month and cut a date in the coping, and that date is worth having whatever else is, and I said so at the time and I have not changed my mind. And a district has been made, and a rate will come, and a man will come in a year to say what my door is worth. So tell me the true thing and do not dress it. What has the rate bought that the stone did not."

Cobb did not answer at once, and the lane waited, and about eleven people heard a clerk of about forty stand in the sun at a doorstep and be honest.

"Nothing yet," he said. "The stone is the stone. What the district has bought is that this morning I could stand in this lane and ask a question, and on Tuesday the third I could not have asked you anything, because there was no district to ask and no paper to ask it on. That is not nothing. It is also not a good thing. It is a new handle on your door, put there under a schedule printed in 1902, and the handle is mine and the door is yours, and I cannot tell you honestly that a handle is a kindness. The girl of nineteen at the fourth door told me on Monday it was a good trade, and she was the only person here who said so, and I have thought about it every day since and have not bettered on it."

"Then I will say the thing I said in the shop," Sella said, "and you may write it down, and Perdie is not here and I do not care. A good trade. My door is in a book, and a man in a year will tell the world what it is worth without ever having been in my kitchen, and I have got a bank with a date on it and a pump that draws and a hole I can see the bottom of, and I would not go back to the twenty-seventh of the month before last for anything, and I am sixty and I have had enough of paper."

Nobody in the lane said anything to that either, which is the correct number of things to say about it.

He read it at the fourth door to Mr Trew and Mrs Trew and Nell, and Nell asked whether the valuation would be made on the ground or on the houses, and he said he did not know, that it was made on a valuation, and that the valuation was on whatever the man chose, and that she should find that out before he came and that she would not find it out from this office.

At the fifth door he read it to Ada Bray with her son standing behind her, and Ada Bray said, before he had finished the second line, "You will want to know he is here."

"I have noted that he is here," Cobb said, "and I have noted that he was not here on the fourth, and both of those are in the return, because the return is a record and it does not get tidied."

At the sixth door, Corrie Lille was sitting on the pump coping with his waders off and his hands in his pockets, and he had heard the form five times already, and he said:

"Read the bit about the name."

Cobb read the line. *One name withheld by the person himself, recorded unwilling, and not argued with.*

"Read it again," Corrie said, "and then tell me what my door is rated at."

"Your door is rated at the same as the other five, in proportion, and it will be rated at the same as the other five whatever it is worth, because a rate is divided by value and not by opinion, and nobody in this lane gets to say their door is worth less because they said no to a form in a city four days ago."

"Then the no did nothing," Corrie said.

"The no did nothing to the rate," Cobb said. "Your refusal stands. It is in the return in your words and not mine, and it is not argued with, and it is in the district book as long as the district book stands. And it bought you precisely nothing, because the rate does not come through the name, it comes through the door, and the door was already yours before you said anything. There is not one box on that form where a man could put a refusal and have it do anything at all."

Corrie sat on the coping for a while.

"That," he said, "is the most honest thing anybody from that office has said in this lane, and I have been waiting nineteen years for one of them to say something and go away."

He read it at the sixth door because a form is read at every door in the district or it is not read at the sixth.

---

The carpenter nailed the notice to a bar at the ford end at about two o'clock, and then stood back and looked at it and said, out loud, to nobody, that nobody had ever asked him about the hammer, and that he had told himself for eleven days that he dropped it, and that he had put it down and the bank went and the hammer went with the bank, and that there was a form in this lane now that would say what he did if anybody asked him.

"You have not been asked about the hammer," Wick Venn said.

"So I am telling a bar," the carpenter said.

"That is worth a nail as much as the notice is," Wick said, and said nothing else about it, and it was on the bar and not in a book, and that is where a thing said to a bar goes.

The lane emptied by four. Harl Bray went down to the landing to see whether the boats were still landing flour, and they were, and they had been on every day of it.

At Nether Sallow that evening the butt was filled at six, forty trips, an hour and fourteen minutes, the shoulder better and the boy counting out loud and Nesta marking the shutter *full, Thursday the twelfth of next month, eighty-second day*. The frame on the culvert post went past empty for the tenth day, ninety years old, and there was a notice nailed to a bar at the far bank four miles off and not one thing in the frame.

Wick came down to the wall at dusk with the tank card in his coat and stood and looked at the frame.

"Would it take a notice," he said.

"It would," Tarin said. "And there is nothing in this valley anybody can be charged under, which is the true reason, and a boy of fifteen worked it out on his own a week before I did. A frame is a frame that somebody nails a notice to, and a notice is for making somebody do something. That card said CLOSED and NO WATER and NO ENTRY and UNDER PENALTY and named no office and named no person, and six hands cut it out of in daylight on the evening of the second of this month and put the frame back on the post, and the twine came off it and it has been wound twice round a pail handle ever since. The only notice this business has produced is four miles off on a bar, and it is on a bar and not in that frame because it names a district, and a district is a thing a man can be served. I would rather it were empty another ninety years."

Wick put the tank card away. "Eighty-second day. Fourteen weeks run to the sixth of the month after next. The card is not read again until the last Saturday in the month. The mark is the same inch it has been since the twenty-ninth of the month before last and it has no rate in it and never has had one. The district book is written at Vellum and the valuation is a year off. The requisition to the writs went in on the fifth, and a polite letter is coming back in about six weeks, and it will name a place." He looked up the lane. "That is the whole of what is in front of you and every part of it is dated."

Tarin tipped in the thirty-eighth pail and thought about the only one of those dates that was not a date, which was a stone at the end of a lane with a district cut into it in three cramped words and a date, and the man who had cut it knowing.

"What happens now," the boy said, from the top of the butt, with the slate under his arm and thirty-eight trips behind him.

"Tomorrow the butt is filled again, and the day after. And a man comes in about a year with a book and writes a number against six doors, and nobody in that lane can stop him and one of them can stand in front of him and say the ground is foreshore, and that is the whole of what eleven people and a count and a rate have bought. And in about six weeks a letter comes from the Low Ward naming a room, and in the room there is a register, and in the register a number, and the number will say who took a gathering out of a flood book fourteen years ago."

"And the book is in the hut," the boy said. "With the door open."

"The book is in the hut with the door open, and the door has hung open for six years, and a man who wanted it could put it in a cart in an afternoon, and after that there would be stubs and a pencil line and a date. Eleven people in that lane have seen the stubbed page and not one of them is on anything except Corrie Lille's hook, and a hook is not a record, and a man is not a witness until somebody writes him down." He tipped in the thirty-ninth. "So the book stays where it is, and the frame stays empty, and the butt gets filled. That is what happens now, and it is not enough, and I have no better plan, and I am not going to start on a Thursday."

Thirty-nine. Forty.

The butt was full. Above them the frame stood on the culvert post with nothing in it, and below them the plank ran at a hundred and ninety gallons an hour into the dark, and two miles below that a stone launder stood empty and chained on a chain a man had bought with his own money, and the tank above it stood at two feet six and an eighth with no rate in it, and four miles and two chains off the water was down and a lane was dry underfoot where a return had said nobody for nineteen years, and six doors in a district written in a book were going to be rated in a year by a man who would not stay the night, and a woman of forty-eight in an office sixty-one miles east had put her own name against a withheld thing and had told nobody why.

He could not leave. On Thursday the twelfth of next month, at Nether Sallow, with a full butt behind him and eighty-two days behind him, he had got the day's work done, all of it, and there was no part of it anybody would be able to check, and he went down the lane to the cottage at Brant at no rent and set the pail by the door with the twine wound twice and tucked under, and did not sleep well, and was up before light, because the butt is filled at six and the day is eighty-three days long and there is a whole valley out there that has started counting things.
