# Chapter 0114 — What a Rate Is For

On the Friday morning, the sixth of next month, the shop at Brant held about eleven people and a slate, and Perdie Ammid read out sheet thirty-four, and the shop argued about a bank four miles off that four of them had made for nothing, and not one of the six doors was in the room, and one of the four was a man who lives at the bottom of this lane.

The sheet was the bank view. She read it standing up, because everything from the sixteenth was read out standing up. *Bank at lane end, Cinder Ford far-bank row. Second view, Tuesday the third. Gap of one yard closed on the first of next month by three persons of the row and one man of Nether Sallow, at their own request and without charge to any district. The works are sufficient for the present state of the drain. The works are not a making-good under the schedule of 1902, there being no bond, no joint and no date.* She read out then that a fourth clause had been struck in the lane on the Tuesday by the magistrate, that the striking was on the record, and that she was reading the finding as it stands and not as it was written. And then her own line, which was the only line in it that had cost anybody anything: *that a work which is not a making-good under the schedule is a work of improvement, and is charged to the district it stands in, and the district must be in the district book before it can be charged, and the district book entry is made on the twelfth of next month, and after that the six doors will be rated.*

Bess Ilk wrote it on the shop slate under nothing else, so that no later hand could move what a Friday had done, and wiped her hand down her apron.

"Thirty-four," she said. "Thirty-three and thirty-four. Where are they going."

"Down on this morning's cart with the bundle," Perdie said, "and the satchel is empty and the chest stays, and there is no book."

"Then say the thing everybody is thinking," Armist Sarg said. He was fifty-eight and had dug a trench in a foot of water on the night of the twenty-second and had not been thanked for it since. "The row made its own bank. Four of them, on a Sunday, with a rope and a willow and their own two coats. And now a man from a city writes on a form that the row is going to be charged for it. That is a rate on a Sunday's work. And I say the row should pay for its own bank and I say it here so that it is written that I said it, and I say it knowing that three of the four are not in this room and cannot be asked what they think about it this morning."

"You may say it," Perdie said, "and it is written, and it is also not a thing this room can do, and I will tell you why in one sentence and then I will sit down, which I am going to want to do. A rate is not a decision. It is a division. There is a sum, and there are six doors, and the office divides the sum by the value of the six doors, and the value of the six doors is not made and cannot be made by anybody in this shop or in that lane. So the rate is not going to be what the row thinks it is worth and it is not going to be what this room thinks it is worth. It is going to be what a man with a book says the six doors are worth, and he is not coming until next year."

"Then that is a bill for a bank before there is a price on the bank," Armist said.

"Yes."

"And nobody can say what it will be."

"No," Perdie said. "And I will tell you the one thing I can, and it is the thing that makes it fair, and it is also the thing that makes it unbearable, and both of those are the same thing. The row will pay less for a door in that lane than any other door in this district, because the ground there is foreshore and the drainage is a discharge and there is no house on it worth a great deal. Which means the rate will be small. And it will be small, and it will be on them anyway, forever, and that is the shape of it: a small sum that does not end."

Mrs Halward put her hand up as though she were in a schoolroom, and she was about fifty and was not.

"Can I say the thing I have been holding since the Saturday."

"Say it," Perdie said.

"We have drained our own lowest field into the Sere at seventeen thousand six hundred and forty gallons a day for six years." Mrs Halward did not have a paper and did not need one, because she had lived above that field for forty-one years. "Seven hundred and thirty-five an hour. I have known that figure since I was married because my husband used to stand in the drain and swear at it. There is no rate on it. There is no schedule item for it. Nobody has ever sent a form about it to anybody in my house, and it goes west every day of the year into a river that goes past a city, and it cannot be used and it cannot be stopped, and it will not stop on the sixth of next month, and I would like somebody in a shop to say that out loud in front of other people, because I have said it to nobody in six years and I have just said it to eleven."

Somebody wrote it on the slate. It was Armist, with a bit of chalk, and his hand was not a clerk's hand and the figures were wrong and Bess Ilk corrected the last two and he let her.

"That is the Reach's own field," Perdie said. "It is in a schedule printed in 1902, and item one of that schedule is the sole discharge of the Reach, and item one stands as printed, and the Reach pays for works that are on the schedule, and that is why the Reach pays for the ditch it dug and does not pay for the water that leaves it. And the row has no schedule, because the row was nobody, and now the row has a certificate, and the certificate is what the rate hangs on. That is the whole of it. A place nobody was in was not charged because there was nothing to charge. A place that is in a book is charged, whatever the book is for, and the book in that lane was a book about a flood."

"And the two are the same size of piece of paper," Mrs Halward said, "and one of them is fair and the other one is not, and I am the only person alive who has stood on both sides of that."

"You are," Perdie said, "and it is on the slate, and I am sorry it is on the slate and not in a book, because a thing on a slate is a thing somebody can wipe."

---

Mr Arm had come in from the yard with his coat on and stood at the counter and said, "Mrs Ammid. The book in the hut. Is that the book of eighteen hundred and forty-three, the one that keeps getting asked for."

"No," Perdie said.

"Then the flood book is the old book."

"No," Perdie said again, and she said it the same way twice on purpose, and the room heard the shape of the two noes. "I said that on the thirty-first in a hut to three people, and I am going to say it once more in a shop and then never again while I live. A book that is gone and a book that never existed are two different things, and joining them is how a valley gets a story instead of a record. That book does not exist in this valley and nobody has asked for it in a hundred and thirty years, and that is on a numbered sheet going to Vellum and it is not resolved. The book in the record house is a hundred and eighty-one years old and it is a ledger of gate orders for the flood year in three hands at the front, and a gathering has been cut out of the end of it by a hand with a knife, and Vellum has established this week that the hand belonged to a desk and not to a man, which fortnight the pencil was, and that no return was ever recorded because the book went out on a writ and the writs are kept in another ward of that city." She put the satchel down on the counter. "There is no story in it yet. There is a book with a piece of the end of it gone and a pencil line with no name, and I have said my piece about that and I am not saying it again."

Nobody asked her a second question about it, which was a kindness, and she took it.

---

The cart came up at eleven with the bundle, and among the papers in it, folded in three and tied with tape, was the witness schedule of the flood court.

Bess Ilk took it, looked at the seal, and read the heading out loud, because a thing in a bundle in a shop is read out whether anybody wants it or not. *Flood court, sitting of the second of next month. Schedule of witnesses. Inquiry: the record book of gate orders, lower Sere record house, and the pencil line within it. Hearing of witnesses: the nineteenth of next month, at ten of the clock, at the counting-house, Vellum.*

"Ten of the clock, at the counting-house," Armist said. "Vellum."

"Sixty-one miles," Wick said. "Two days there and two days back in this weather, and the road under from the ford to the low bridge."

"And it names you," Perdie said, "and it names me, and it names the boy, and the boy is fifteen and may be put off, and I will see to that. It names Mr Venn. It names Mr Rusk."

She turned the sheet round and set it on the counter and Bess read the columns, because Bess reads forms faster than anybody in the room and always has.

*Name. District. Occupation. Papers read out to the witness in his hearing, by number. Whether the witness attends, or gives evidence in writing at the place of the holding, to be read into the record.*

"District," Armist said. "Occupation."

"Write what there is," Perdie said.

Bess wrote it in, and Tarin's name was on the slate above the room's own decision about a rate, and the district column wanted one line, and there was no line in the Reach that would take him. He was in two books at the Sallow Head and four hundred yards apart and neither of those books was a record of a route, and he was five shillings a day for six days at a shed with a level staff, and he had no district and no office and had not had either since he was nineteen and signed something on a Riverside cut.

"Write *of no district*," Tarin said. "That is not a gap. That is the answer to the question they asked."

Bess wrote *of no district, of no office, of Nether Sallow by residence, at the plank* in the district column, which is a great deal more than the column asked for, and nobody crossed it out, and Armist said that a man who is entered in a register at Vellum as belonging to no district is a strange thing to have done to a man who has done nothing wrong, and Tarin said that it was the first time in thirty-three years a piece of paper had described him accurately and he would rather it were on the right side of the basin.

The last column was the hard one. *Papers read out to the witness in his hearing, by number.* And Perdie Ammid stood in front of a counter in a shop at Brant with a chalk in her hand and a form in front of her and understood, in about two seconds, that she was the only person in the room who knew the answer to the column, and that the answer was not one number, and that it was not two.

It was three.

On the Saturday the thirty-first, in a stone hut below a culvert, in front of three witnesses, she had read out sheet thirty-two. She had also read out sheet thirty-one, in the morning, at the plank, to about nine people. And she had read out sheet thirty-three, in the afternoon, at the wall, to the same about nine people, out loud, word for word as nearly as she could from a man's mouth, and then she had read back her own line saying the witness had declined to give a schedule of persons and had declined to give a number.

The three witnesses to the reading of thirty-two were Wick Venn, the boy, and Tarin Rusk.

The court wanted to know, of each of them, by number, what was read out to that witness in that witness's hearing.

Wick had heard thirty-two and nothing else. The boy had heard thirty-two and nothing else. Tarin had heard thirty-two and nothing else in the hearing, because thirty-one and thirty-three were read to about nine people and not to three witnesses, and there was no proceeding at that hut called a hearing of any kind, and Perdie Ammid had said the word *witnesses* herself when she wrote thirty-two down, and had said it because thirty-two was the sheet with the book in it.

Perdie took the chalk.

"Thirty-two," she said, and wrote it in all three rows, and put the chalk down, and read the three rows back, and her voice did not do anything at all, which took more out of her than anything she had said in that shop all morning.

"That is the whole of it," she said. "Thirty-two, in all three rows, and it is the truth, and it is the whole of the truth, and I am not going to write a fourth thing in a column because somebody at a counting-house has drawn a line under it."

Tarin looked at the three rows on the counter with his own name against the bottom one, and did not say anything, and went out into the lane, and stood in it for a while.

The sheet went down the road on the Friday afternoon cart with a witness schedule in it that said one paper, and a bundle that had two, and a register at Vellum that said three sheets received and two published, and a woman of forty-eight in an office sixty-one miles east who had written *the reason is withheld by the officer* over a ruled line, and a man at a counter in a shop who had put nothing in a column and would not, and the whole of it was correct, and every hand in it had done its job, and there were now three offices in the basin holding a document that was true and a number that was not, and no way whatever of asking any of them why.

---

The butt at six took an hour and twenty-two minutes. Seventy-six days. The boy counted out loud to forty and Nesta marked the shutter, and the frame on the culvert post was still empty and still ninety years old, and there was going to be a bank worked at six in the morning on Saturday the seventh by six people and one man who could not be there, and a rate divided by a valuation that did not exist, and a hearing at a counting-house on the nineteenth of next month that a man who could not leave a valley had answered by writing one number in a box in a shop.
