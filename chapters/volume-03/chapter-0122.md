# Chapter 0122 — A Line a Tallyman Wrote

Amos Nye came up the lane on the Saturday morning, the fourteenth of next month, with a satchel of his own and a spare horse, and he had not been up that lane since the fourth of next month and before that he had not been up it in twelve years, and he said so himself at the ford end before anybody else got a word in.

"You will want to know why," he said. "A court has called for the return of this district to be certified by the officer who took it, and the officer who took it is me, and I was not here. So I have to put my name under a count I did not take, and there is only one way to do that honestly, which is to go and find out where the number came from and write that down too. A man who signs a number without its source is signing a rumour with a very good hand."

He was forty-one and he had been twelve years in the job and he carried the satchel the way a man carries a thing he has decided to be exact about.

He went to the sixth door first, because the sixth door was where the count came from, and he stood on the step with the satchel open and asked Corrie Lille to say it again from the beginning, in order, and he wrote it down as he was told it.

"Six doors. Eleven persons. Sunday the first of next month, off my own hook, door by door, and I said it to Mr Nye's clerk and the lady wrote it down and the lady read it back to me and it was right." Corrie shifted his nets. "And I would not give my name, and it is written down that I would not, and it is written down that I was not argued with, and I said it once."

"That is all of it," Nye said. "That is exactly all of it, and I want it in the return in those words, and not *count given by a resident*."

"It is already in the return."

"It is in the return. I want it in the return again, in the certification, because a certification is read by a person who has never been to this lane and the word *resident* in a return has meant, in my experience, everything from a man who has lived here forty years to a man who slept here." He looked up. "You are not a resident of this district, Mr Lille. You are the source of it. Those are different words and one of them is a person and one of them is a ledger."

Corrie Lille looked at him for a while and then said, "Twelve years I have been the record of this lane and you are the first man to come up here and call it a ledger out loud. Nobody has ever said that word in this lane."

"They say it in a counting-house about nine times a day," Nye said, "and it means a book, and I have been in this job long enough to know that in this valley a book is a thing somebody has to be trusted with, and that is why I am here and not in an office."

Then he went to the third door, and Sella Marr had him stand on her step while she washed her hands, and she did not ask him anything, and he asked her.

"Mrs Marr, I am required to certify a count. I have the return and it says six doors and eleven persons and it is signed as given from a hook. I have been a tallyman twelve years and I have certified nine hundred returns and I have never once had a person in a district tell me the count was wrong. I am not asking you whether the number is wrong. I am asking you, for the first time in twelve years of this job, how you would put it if you were putting it."

Sella Marr dried her hands on her apron and considered the yard.

"I would not put it at all," she said. "Not because I do not know. Because there is no line on it for what a person is, and every time one of your men has written a number in this lane for nineteen years he has written it as though it were about a row of doors, and it is not, it is about who is in them, and the two come apart the minute anybody stays a fortnight who is not a resident of anywhere."

"Mrs Marr—"

"You have nine names on that paper and eleven people in six houses, and I know which of the two of those is the one you are going to write in your column, and I am not arguing with the number." She took the step down. "I have lived in this lane nineteen years and I have watched a boy come through it with a bundle of straw and stay three weeks, and he was not a resident of anything, and nobody was going to write him down, and he was here. That is the whole of what I know about being counted and I have had nineteen years to think of it and I have never once been asked, and you have asked me at eight o'clock on a Saturday with a satchel, and I am telling you that the asking is the thing that was missing and not the number."

Then she went inside, and Nye stood on the step with his pencil and did not write anything for a while.

---

He had the amendment half written when Jess Marr came down the lane with the two children and told him there was one more person in that lane than his paper had.

At the first door, Mrs Wade, seventy-four, had a sister's grandchild sleeping on a settle by the fire since the night the water came, because the fourth house of the mill yard was under two feet and the mother could not get back for it. The child was nine. Her name was Robyn. She was not a resident of this district and not of any other district in this basin, and her mother was in the mill yard and was a ratepayer there and had never been in a room in her life.

"Will you put her in it," Jess Marr said. "That is all. Not her name. Her number."

Nye turned round the sheet and looked at the form, which was a printed form, and at the box headed *persons not resident and not visitors, district of*, and the box was a good two inches wide, and the words after the comma wanted a district, and there was no way on that piece of paper to write the word *none* in a box that wanted a district without the box being wrong.

"There is a line for her," he said. "That is the honest answer and it is the worst thing I have found this week. There is a line on this form for a person who is not resident and not a visitor, and the line wants a district, and she is not in one, and I cannot write a district that is not there, and I cannot write none in that box without the box being a lie in my hand." He put the pencil down. "So she goes in the note underneath, in my own writing, and the note is not the form."

"Then the note does nothing," Corrie Lille said from the sixth door.

"The note does something worse than nothing," Nye said. "It is my hand under a sentence that says the form could not be filled, and in about four years a clerk will be copying forward the columns and he will copy the columns, and he will not copy my note, and he will not know there was a note. And I have known that since I was twenty-nine and I have never once been in a position to do anything about it, and this morning I am."

He wrote it. *One child of nine years sleeping at the first door since the twenty-second of the month before last, not resident in this or any district, entered at the request of Mrs J. Marr, second door, there being no line on the return for a person of no district, the return being eleven and the lane being twelve.*

Then he read it out to four people at the ford end and Mrs Wade said, "Say the last of it again," and he did.

Cobb came over the ford at about two with the district book under his arm, because a rate cannot be struck against a district whose number of doors disagrees with the office that holds the doors, and there is a clerk in Vellum whose business it is to make the two agree and who had sent a rider out from the counting-house on the Thursday to ask the question.

He and Nye stood on the dry ground at the ford end and compared two documents that had been made nine days apart in two buildings, and it turned out that they agreed about six doors and about nothing else whatever.

"You have eleven persons," Cobb said. "I have six doors and a work of improvement at nineteen shillings and twopence and a date in a coping stone. Neither of us has the other's paper. I have never sent a document to the office that fills your return and I do not believe anybody has in nineteen years, and if you had come to me in a year instead of me to you I would have had no idea where to send it."

"Then we are two offices writing about one lane and neither of us can say the other's name."

"We can say each other's names perfectly well," Cobb said. "That is the difficulty. It is not that the offices do not know of one another. It is that a rate is on doors and a count is on persons, and neither paper has a line for the other, so neither office has to send the other anything, and a lane gets two sets of figures and one set of doors and everybody's work is correct."

Nye wrote that down too, in the same hand, in the same note, and then looked at it and said, "I have been twelve years in a job where I was taught that a return is a return and a district book is a district book and they are both true and neither is the other's business. And I have been up this lane for three hours and I have found a child of nine, and I have found that a man's name went down to a desk and came back as a one, and I have found that a rate will be struck on six doors in a building that does not know how many people sleep in them." He shut the satchel. "None of those three things is anybody's fault. All three of them are the shape of the form."

Cobb said, after a while, "When the valuation is made, in about a year, the man who makes it will be given the district book, and he will be given the number of doors off it, and he will be given the rate off it, and he will not be given your paper, Mr Nye, because your paper is not about value and value is what he is for. And he will go round those doors and ask each one what it is worth, and if there is a child of nine asleep on a settle at the first door, that is not a matter of value, and it will be a matter of nobody's business, and he will be a decent man about it, and in twenty years it will be a house with two people in it that everybody in this valley remembers had three."

"Write that in the book."

"I will not write a child in a district book," Cobb said. "There is no line for her there either, and I have looked twice this week, and I am beginning to think that two lines are missing in the whole of this business and that I shall be looking for lines in other books for a long time."

---

The return went back down the road on the cart on the Saturday afternoon with the certification on it, and Nye rode down behind the cart because he had to see it into the office, and it came back up again on Monday the sixteenth with a stamp on it, which is not the office's way, because the office's way is to keep a thing and send a copy.

On the back of it, in a small hand, somebody at the Receipts desk at the counting-house at Vellum had written the count back as figures, because that is what a desk does when it is handed a count and cannot leave it alone.

*Persons 11. Named 9. Unwilling 1. Absent 1.*

Perdie Ammid read that out in the shop at Brant on the Monday morning, the sixteenth of next month, in front of about nine people, and she read it twice, and then she put her hand flat on the counter and said the thing that was in the room before anybody had found it.

"Nine names went down the road on the twelfth in a column that said names, and this came back on the sixteenth as a one and a nine and a one and a one, and nobody anywhere has done anything to the names. It is the same nine. It is just arithmetic with a small hand on it. And in four years a clerk will take this second line and not the first, because this second line is short, and then this district will have been counted four times and there will be no people in it at all, only a column, and it will be a true column, and that is the part I cannot get past."

Armist Sarg said, "And the girl of nine."

"The girl of nine is in a note in a tallyman's own hand underneath a box that could not be filled, and the note is dated the fourteenth of next month and is signed, and it is in the same building as the line with the small figures on it." Perdie put the paper down. "Both are true. That is what I have been writing down in front of this shop for a fortnight and I have got tired of saying it and I am not going to stop."

---

The butt at Nether Sallow was filled at six on the Saturday, forty trips, an hour and nine minutes, and the boy counted to thirty and stopped and started again at one without being told, which Nesta said nothing about and marked on the shutter as usual. Eighty-fourth day.

Wick Venn was at the notch with the stick at dusk and gave the drain as down, and falling, and the lane following, and then said the thing he had come to say.

"Amos Nye rode down that lane behind the cart this afternoon with a satchel and a spare horse and he has been a tallyman for twelve years and he has never been up here in twelve years, and he came for a count of eleven and he left with a count of twelve, and he wrote the twelve down in a box that wanted a district and would not have it." He put the stick in the ground. "Nobody has ever made the paper worse on purpose before in my hearing. He made it worse because the child exists."

"There is going to be a lot of that," Tarin said.

"So there is," Wick said, "and I have been in this valley thirty-eight years and I have never seen anybody put a worse hand to a thing than they were asked for, and I have only ever seen it done by accident."
