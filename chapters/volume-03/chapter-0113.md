# Chapter 0113 — Three Hands and a Desk

On the Thursday morning, the fifth of next month, the Municipal Memory Office at Vellum put a hundred-and-eighty-one-year-old book on a reading table under the tall window and did the only thing anybody can do with a book with a gathering cut out of the end of it, which is to look at what is left and write down what the looking establishes.

Mara had asked for the session on the Monday. She had it in eleven years of service as a thing she was owed, and she asked for it in writing, in one paragraph, with the date on it, and the store keeper brought the book up from the low store in two arms and set it on the table under a weight, and would not let anybody open it until she had the room to herself for four minutes, which she had.

The book was in a plain wooden case with a lid tied on by cord, and the cord had been cut and retied twice since Saturday. The court had sent for it on the Monday and the district's cart had carried it down on the Wednesday, wrapped in sacking with a return docket against it, and it had been in the low store since the Wednesday evening because nobody in the building had been told what it was and the store keeper does not open what she has not been told about. Perdie Ammid had gone into the record house on the Tuesday with a lamp and made a tracing of the back board, the stubs, and the pencil line inside it, and had sent the tracing up with the cart, because a tracing is a thing a conservator makes and a tracing cannot carry a drop of ink. Both were on the table, and Perdie was in the room with a slate on her knee.

The court's order of the second had asked four questions — whose hand, what batch of ink, what date, and whether any return was ever recorded — and Mara had written them at the head of a page in her own hand and put the page where anybody in the room could see it, because a session that starts without its questions on the wall is a session that ends with a conclusion in it.

They answered the first three inside forty minutes and did not answer the fourth at all.

The back board's pencil line, said Mr Aldwin, who had kept the store for twenty-two years and who had not opened a lower-shelf box on his own authority in all that time, was in the same hand as a docket slip that had been in the store since before Mara came to the building.

"Docket," he said, and slid it across. A slip of the office's own printed form, four inches by two, a number in a box and a ruled line under it, out of a bundle of dockets tied with tape and cross-referenced, in court-supply pencil. The lean of the downstroke in the line on the back board and the lean of the downstroke on the docket were the same lean.

"That is your trade and not mine," Aldwin said. "I keep the store. I cannot tell you whose hand that is and neither can anybody in this building. What I can tell you is that it is the same hand twice, and that is a fact about paper and not about a man."

"It is the same hand twice," Mara said, "and I am not going to name a man, and I am also not going to soften it into a nothing. It is a clerk's hand. It has a habit of pressing at the foot of a downstroke and it does not lift its pen between words at the end of a line, and those are two habits that do not come from nowhere." She put the tracing and the docket side by side and did not write anything down. "What I will write is what the hand has. It has an office number."

The number on the docket was a series and a year. Aldwin took the store's index of series to the window, where the light was better than on the table, and stood with his thumb down the columns for a while.

"Four-one-one," he said. "That is the Receipts desk of the examination table, and four-one-one is a thing that goes *into* this office. A thing that comes in off a cart, off a man, off a district, it is numbered in that series and docketed and it goes on a shelf." He looked at the docket again. "So whatever the back board of that book says, and I have not read it and I am not going to, it was written by the desk that takes things in."

"And the desk that takes things in is not a man," Perdie said.

"No," Aldwin said. "Not on any list in this building. I will show you the list."

The establishment list was a printed sheet folded in four for thirty years with a soft edge to it, and the second column said *holder*, and the whole of the second column on that page read *vacant, vacant, vacant, vacant*, and there was no man's name on that page anywhere.

"Offices, not men," Aldwin said. "I have been telling people for twenty-two years that this list will not tell them who held a desk in a year, and it will not, because it is a list of where the work went and not of who did it. In that year the fourth desk was held by the examination office. It has never said a person. It would not have occurred to whoever printed it."

Perdie Ammid was looking at the list with both hands flat on the table, and she said, in a voice that was not steady: "I said that in a hut on the thirty-first. I said the hand is a desk and not a man, and a desk cannot be cross-examined, and I would not name a man on a specimen, and I said it to three people in a stone hut and I thought I was guessing."

"You were not guessing," Aldwin said. "You were right, and it took this office thirty years to write it down on a printed sheet, and you were right on a Saturday afternoon with a wet page in your hand." He squared the list with the edge of the table. "I would put that in your sheet if I were you, and I would put in it what it is and is not. A desk is not a man. A desk is not an alibi."

---

The ink was the second answer and it was the answer Mara had expected and the answer that settled the date.

The court-supply pencil came into the building in batches, and the store book said so, in a hand forty years older than anybody in the room: *batch eighteen, received in the second week of the month, issued to examination, exhausted, remainder four sticks returned.* One line. It was a thing that happened about twice a year, and the store book was the office's own record of its own consumables and had never once been in dispute in anybody's memory.

"So the line on that back board is in a batch that was exhausted," Mara said. "Which means it was written in the fortnight the batch was current, and not in the year before it and not in the year after."

"In the fortnight," Aldwin agreed.

"The pencil line gives its own date, and the pencil line's date is fourteen years ago, and the pencil agrees with it." She wrote it out. *Hand: the same hand as docket 411 of the Receipts desk series. Office: the examination office, which held the fourth desk in that year. No holder is named on the establishment list, which lists offices. Ink: court-supply pencil, batch eighteen, exhausted in the fortnight of its issue. Date: the fortnight of that issue, fourteen years ago, which is the date the line itself gives.* "That is the whole of the first three questions and I will read it out at the end and I will read it out in the reading room, because a thing in this building that is not read out is a thing in this building."

The fourth question was *whether any return was ever recorded*, and Aldwin put his hand flat on the index of series and did not open it.

"I can tell you the answer and I cannot tell you why it is the answer, and the why is on the other side of a wall from this room, and it is not my wall."

"Tell me the answer," Mara said.

"There is no return." He said it plainly, the way he said everything, and then he said the rest of it, because he had been keeper of a store for twenty-two years and had never in that time made a thing worse by saying a whole sentence. "And I want to be exact, because I am aware that *no return* is a sentence that a man in a bad position would give about himself. A return is a thing that comes back into this office. Four-one-one is the series for things that come in. This book was not received in that series. It has a docket, and the docket is in a different series, and the slip on this table is a specimen the store keeps of the four-one-one and is not this book's receipt, and I have not looked up that other series in front of you because it is not mine and it is not this office's, and a keeper of a store who reads out of another office's book in his own room should hand in his keys."

Mara put her pen down. "Which series."

"The writs," Aldwin said. "A thing that goes *out* of an examination office to a place it is wanted at is entered on a writ and goes out on a writ, and the writ is registered by the clerk of the writs, and the writ is returned when the thing is returned, and the writ is not kept here. It never has been kept here. It is in the Low Ward, in an office that has been in the Low Ward since before the building this one was built, and the Memory Office has never held a writ book and could not if it wanted to, because a writ book is a district record and this is not a district."

"So there is no return," Perdie said. "Because the book left on a writ and the writ is not ours, and the writ has not come back, and nobody in this building would have known, because nobody in this building would have been told."

"Exactly," Aldwin said. "Which is the most frightening sentence I have ever had the occasion to say in a room, and I would like it minuted that I said it in front of a witness, and that the witness is fifty-four and writes sheets."

---

Oriel Fenn was the minute clerk of the counting-house and had nineteen years in the room and had entered and read back a requisition to inspect that same counting-house in the spring, and he came in at two o'clock with the form and a black ink that had to be dry before it could be folded.

He entered it in his own hand, in the third person, and read it back, which is the whole of why the form exists.

> *To the clerk of the writs, Low Ward, Vellum. Requisition to inspect the register of writs for the year of the flood inquiry, fourteen years ago, and in particular writ 214, being a writ upon a volume received from the lower Sere record house and not returned. Entered by O. Fenn, minute clerk, at the instance of M. Quill, officer, Municipal Memory Office, this fifth of next month. Answered at the address of this office. O. Fenn.*

"That is a proper requisition," Fenn said, "and I will say what it is worth. The ones that get answered inside a month are the ones that are rude. This one is polite. It will be answered politely, in about six weeks, and the answer will name a place, and the letter will be true." He dried the ink. "I have never known a writ answered and never known one not answered. They are both long."

"And what the answer will be matters to nobody but us," Aldwin said.

"It matters to you," Fenn said, without malice, "and I am sorry for it, and it will come.""

---

The rest of the afternoon was the register, and it was ordinary, and it was the part of the day that cost something.

The intake clerk indexed the valley satchel. Thirty-three numbered sheets came into the office on the Saturday night in one bundle with the return docket against it, and the clerk wrote them into the receipt register in order and counted them twice, which is the only part anybody enjoys.

"Thirty-one, thirty-two, thirty-three," he said. "And the bundle to the flood court on the Sunday carried thirty-one and thirty-two."

"Thirty-one and thirty-two," Mara said.

"That is what the bundle docket says." The clerk had his pen up. "So the third is not published?"

"No," Mara said. "The third sheet is a man's own sheet. It is his, and it is about a matter of his own that is not this flood, and it is not mine to publish, and it is not published. Enter that."

"Enter it how."

"Enter it as one sheet withheld from publication, and enter the name of the officer withholding it, because that is what the column is for and because I would rather have my own name in a register in this building than have a blank in it that somebody fills in in about four years when nobody remembers who did what."

The clerk wrote it, and did not like it, and wrote it. One sheet withheld from publication. Officer: M. Quill. Date: the fifth of next month. And then, in the space under it, nothing at all, because the column beneath that one was for a reason and she had not given one and the form did not ask for one.

Perdie Ammid sat at the end of the table with a slate and had heard all of it. Thirty-three sheets had come up on the Saturday night in one bundle, thirty-one and thirty-two had gone into the court's bundle on the Sunday, and the third had not gone anywhere, and she had written all three of those numbers herself in three different hands, and she knew to the penny what was in the third sheet, because she had written it out word for word as nearly as she could from a man's mouth at a wall on a Saturday, and had put a line under it saying the witness had declined to give a schedule of persons and had declined to give a number.

She did not ask. She looked at the register for about four seconds and then wrote the date and the number of the sheets on her slate, and not the rest of it, and that was not tact. It was that a keeper of a book who asks what a gap is, in front of a clerk, has told the clerk there is something in the gap, and she had been taught that by four books in a chest for twenty-six years and not by anybody.

Mara read across the table that she had not asked, and went on writing, and the price of it went into her the way a price does.

---

Perdie Ammid came back up the road on the afternoon cart, four days after she went down, with an empty satchel and nothing to write and a slate in her hand that she had not filled, and she got to the lane at Nether Sallow as the light was going off the hill, and the butt was three-quarters full and a man of thirty-three was on the bank with a pail and a boy of fifteen was counting out loud in the cold.

"Seventy-fifth day," the boy said, without being asked. "Thirty-eight."

Tarin tipped the pail in. "Thirty-eight."

"You were at the plank all day."

"I was at the plank all day," Tarin said. "I did the noon trial and I did the four and I have been on this bank since half past four, and Mrs Ammid went down the road on Monday with a lamp in her head and came back tonight, and there is no form in this basin in which a man may go and stand at a table in Vellum and be back before the light goes, and I could not have gone if there were one."

"Was it worth the four days?"

"It was worth the four days," Tarin said. "It is worth exactly what the paper is worth and not one trip more, and a man who wants it to be worth a trip is a man who has not been on a cart." He went down for the thirty-ninth. "What did they say."

Perdie stood at the bottom of the bank with the satchel at her feet and looked at the pail going up and coming down empty.

"They found out that a hand belongs to a desk," she said. "They found out which desk, and which fortnight, and that the book went out on a writ to an office in the Low Ward that this office has never heard of, and there is a letter going asking after it, and the letter has not been answered. They found out that no return was ever recorded and that nobody in that building thinks anybody was hiding anything, and that the list they keep of who held a desk in that year is a list of offices and not of men, and the holder column says *vacant* four times." She picked the satchel up. "I did not learn anything that is worth a number, Tarin, and I have been on a cart for four days hoping to."

He went up for the fortieth and the butt was full, and the shutter said *full, Thursday the fifth of next month, seventy-fifth day* in Nesta's hand, and the frame on the culvert post was empty, and the boy wrote nothing on his slate at all, which was the correct thing to have written.

---

At the Memory Office in Vellum, at about eight o'clock at night, Mara Quill sat at her desk with the receipt register open in front of her and read one line of it twice.

*One sheet withheld from publication. Officer: M. Quill. Reason: —*

She had signed a certificate with her own name on it eight days ago, and a row number three days ago that she had entered herself for a district that had none, and she had published the valley's flood book into a court's bundle because that was what the office was for, and she had done all of it well. And in eleven years she had never put her own name in a register against a withheld thing, and she had sworn at twenty-one never to be a keeper of a thing nobody could check, and on the fifth of next month she had become one in a building with a printed column and a ruled line for the reason.

She could have written a reason. She had a true one, and it was that a man had told nine people in a valley on a Saturday what he had never told her, and that publishing it would put a man's unfinished sentence into a court's margin where somebody would be tempted to fill in the blank themselves. That was true, and it was publishable, and it was the kind of reason that a register column is for.

She did not write it in, and not because it was a secret. Because a reason in that column is an instruction to the next hand — *this officer judged this, and here is the test she applied* — and there was no test in the world she could put down that would not have to include a number, and she did not have the number, and would not have it from him, and the honest thing was to be the sort of officer who withholds a sheet and says nothing, and let the blank be the blank.

She wrote under the line, in her own hand, and only that: *The reason is withheld by the officer. M. Quill.* And then she closed the register, put it on the shelf where a register goes, and locked the drawer, which held three papers under the brass weights and a nineteen-year file with a pencil mark on its spine and a man's act in it that she would not explain to a single person in the building.

The drawer and the register now said the same thing in two different offices, four days and sixty-one miles apart, and neither of them was wrong, and there was nobody in the world she could tell that to.
