# Chapter 0121 — Two Empty Chairs

On the Friday morning, the thirteenth of next month, the satchel was already gone down the road with thirty-nine numbered sheets in it, and Perdie Ammid made a fortieth at the counter at Brant and read it out standing up in front of about eleven people, and the fortieth was the only thing she made that week which was about a room nobody in that shop was ever going to sit in.

*Forty. Friday the thirteenth of next month. Of the witness schedule of the flood court, forwarded to the counting-house at Vellum with the bundle on Friday the twelfth. The hearing of witnesses is the nineteenth of next month at ten of the clock at the counting-house, sixty-one miles east. The witness before the court is Mr T. Rusk, of no district and of no office, who has given his evidence in writing at the place of the holding, Nether Sallow, on the tenth of next month, before the warden of the lower Sere and in the presence of Mrs P. Ammid, and who is not attending. The district of Cinder Ford far-bank row is not a party to the hearing. Nine persons of it are named in a return of that district which is in the file. No notice of the hearing has been sent to the district and none is to be.*

She read the last line twice, which she had not done for anything in thirty-one years, and about nine people heard it the first time and about eleven heard it the second.

Then she stood there and waited, which she always did, and Mrs Halward spoke first.

"They can take money off a door," she said, "in a lane that is not on a map of this valley. I have known that since the twelfth and I have not slept properly since the twelfth, because a man came and stood in that lane and read it out at six doors and did not once say where he got the power to read it out. And now you tell me a room full of men in a city I have never been to is going to sit on Thursday about a river I have stood in, and the six doors are in the file, and nobody is going to tell us one word of it."

"That is what it says," Perdie said.

"Then I have two things and I want to know which of them is the worse. A door can be charged and nobody has to be in it. Can a district be put in a room?"

"A district can be summoned," Perdie said, "the same as a man. Whether anybody has thought to do it is a different question and I have looked at four columns of a printed form and there is no column in it for a district."

"So we are the only thing in that file that cannot be told anything."

"You are the only thing in that file that cannot be told anything at all," Perdie said. "Not because anybody has decided to keep it from you. Because there is no line in the form on which your being told could be written down, and a thing with no line does not get done, and that is not wickedness, and I have not got a better word for it than *no line* and I have looked."

Armist Sarg put his hod down outside the door and came back in without it, which Bess Ilk noticed and did not comment on.

"Mrs Ammid. A man of forty in a counting-house cannot find a lane. I have been to Vellum twice and I could not find it the second time with a map in my hand and a man pointing at it. And there is a district book in that building that says where it is, and it went in on the twelfth. So write to them and say the row is at the ford end of the lane, past the pump, and the water is off the ground, and the pump draws, and we are eleven."

"Which of those four things is the answer to a question they have not asked," Perdie said, "and I will write every one of them down, and the clerk will take the one he likes and the other three will be in a bundle somewhere in four years and no man will ever read them. That is what a form is. It is not a thing that is asked wrong. It is a thing that is asked narrowly, and it is asked narrowly because it is short."

She wrote it down anyway, on the back of the fortieth, and dated it, and Armist watched her do it, and Mrs Halward said, "That is the first thing anybody in this valley has written down in a month that nobody asked for, and I have been here forty-one years and I could not have told you that it was allowed."

---

They found it out at Cinder Ford at about eleven, from a different direction entirely.

Jess Marr came over the ford on the flour boat's errand with her boy on her hip and said, without coming up the lane, that the district return had gone down the road on the Friday with the cart, and that it had gone with it the number of people in six doors, and that nobody had stood in that lane and asked whether it might.

"Then whose return is it," Wyn Marr said, from the barrow.

"It is the tallyman's," Jess said. "The officer at the desk filled it out of what the tallyman wrote, and the tallyman wrote it out of what Corrie said off a hook. That is how it works, and it worked, and it is not a complaint."

"But it went out of this lane on a Friday morning before anybody in this lane knew it had gone," Sella said from her door, with her hands wet from the trough, "and that is what I object to. Not the number. The Friday."

Corrie Lille came out of the sixth door with his nets over his arm and did not say anything for a while.

"Twenty-three years," he said at last. "I have mended the nets of that landing and I have never had a thing of mine go down that road. If I had wanted a thing of mine to go down that road I would have had to ask, and nobody ever came and asked me, and now a piece of paper has gone with a number on it that I said off a hook and I did not know it was going and I would have said yes." He shifted the nets. "That is the part I mind. Not that it went. That it went and it was mine and I was not in the room."

"A hook is not a record," Tarin said.

"A hook is not a record," Corrie agreed, "and I have said so for eleven years, and it turns out a hook is good enough to go down a road on."

---

Harker's letter came up the road on the same cart, in a second envelope, addressed in a hand nobody in the lane recognised, and he brought it down to the plank at dusk on the Friday and read it out himself because he said a man should hear his own summons in his own voice.

"Thursday the nineteenth. Ten of the clock. The counting-house at Vellum. You are the officer before whom the evidence of a witness was taken in writing and whose return has been called for. You are required to attend."

Then he folded it once and put it in his coat.

"I am not going."

Wick Venn had the stick across his knees at the notch and did not look up. "Say the reason out loud, Harker, because there is a boy standing here and he will ask me in a month and I am not going to invent one."

"Nine men sworn in this ward since the first of next month," Harker said. "Two of them are not back from the county town and one of those two has a wife with a month of fever on him. The long drain is down two feet and falling and the bank at the ford end is made good on a Sunday's work and will be taken out by a wet week, and there is nobody in this ward but me and a boy of fifteen and a man of thirty-three who cannot leave a valley. If I go down that road on Monday I am on the plank on the twentieth and I come back on the twenty-fourth, and in those four days this ward is a length of drain with a notice nailed to it."

He said the rest of it to the water.

"And I will send a return in writing, in my own hand, and the reason will be in the return in my own hand, and I know what that is. A warden's non-attendance is a thing a man can be questioned on. There is no line on that form either that says *the wardenship was kept*, and I have looked for one, because I have been a warden for twenty-six years and I have never once been asked to look for one before."

Nobody said anything for a while. The plank ran at a hundred and ninety below them and did not vary, and the frame on the culvert post went past empty for the eleventh day, and the butt behind the wall was empty because it was not yet six.

Then Tarin said, "You should not have said it that way."

"I have said it the only way there is," Harker said. "I have written nine men down and two of them are not back, and there is a drain. A man who leaves his ward for four days to be in a room does not go and leave his ward. He goes and leaves a hole with his own name on it, and there is nobody in this basin who can stand in the hole, and that is the truth and it is dull, and dull is what it is going to sound like to a clerk in a city on Thursday when he reads it."

Then Harker looked at him, and did not say the other half of it, and went up the lane.

The other half of it was that a man of thirty-three had written one in a box on the Tuesday of the ninth day and had had a warden write *true* in the margin of it, and that on Thursday a room full of strangers was going to read four sentences he had written at a plank in front of a boy, and that the warden was not going to be in that room to say anything about the man who wrote them, and that on the Friday night of the thirteenth the two of them stood at a plank and neither could say it.

A man who says *I know nothing of it* is believed. Harker had told him that in a shop on the tenth and it was the whole of the trade, and this was the same trade done by a different man in a different week for a better reason and the price was exactly the same, and neither of them was going to be thanked for it, because the thanking is the part that cannot be done in writing.

---

The butt at Nether Sallow was filled at six, forty trips, an hour and twelve minutes, the shoulder sound since the Saturday, and the boy counting out loud and not writing it down, and Nesta marking the shutter *full, Friday the thirteenth of next month, eighty-third day*. The boy lingered.

"Mr Rusk. Two chairs."

"Two chairs," Tarin said.

"You put one there on the Wednesday and now he has put the other one there himself, and neither of you is going to be in the room, and the room is going to decide about the six doors anyway." The boy scratched at it with the edge of the slate. "I have been trying to work out what a room is for."

"That is a bad question to end a day on."

"It is the only one I have got," the boy said, and went off up the lane with the slate under his arm.

Tarin set the pail by the wall with the twine wound twice round the handle, and stood a moment with his hand flat on the low stone, and did the sum he did every evening without putting it anywhere: the butt full, the plank at a hundred and ninety, the tank at two feet six and an eighth with no rate in it and never to have one, the flume empty and chained on a chain a man had bought with his own money, the frame empty and to stay empty, the district book written at Vellum, the valuation a year off and a stranger who would not stay the night, the requisition entered on the fifth and unanswered at eight days and a polite letter in about six weeks that would name a place, and a hearing on the nineteenth at ten of the clock sixty-one miles east at which there would now be two empty chairs where a district's voice should have been if there were such a thing as a chair for that.

He could not leave. He was thirty-three years old and on his eighty-third day in a valley with a four-shilling and eightpence of stone and a cart of clay and a bushel of lime in a bill he had helped to earn and could not be in a room about, and forty trips was the only sentence he had that did not need anybody's leave, and the butt was full, and it was Friday the thirteenth of next month, and in six days a court would sit without him and he would find out nothing for two more.

Eighty-third day. That was the whole of it, and every part of it was dated, and the only one of the dates that was not a date was a door.
