# Chapter 0106 — The Magistrate's Line

On the Thursday morning, the twenty-ninth of this month, Cael Dorrin came down the lane to Nether Sallow with two clerks and a plan case, because a certificate with six households on it had come up to the Fair Copy with the Thursday bundle, and a lane under water where a return said nobody lived was the one thing his office could not leave alone.

He was a man of about fifty, narrow in the face, with grey at the temples and a coat brushed that morning, and he walked the lane the way a man walks a thing he has already read: doors first and people second, because doors are on plans and people are not. The clerks carried the case between them and said nothing. They had been with him six years and knew when he was reading.

At the plank he stopped. Nesta Sarg was doing the eight o'clock trial with the boy, and the figure on the shutter was a hundred and ninety, and she did not divide it by anything. Dorrin watched her fill the pail and tip it, the way a magistrate watches work he has no authority over, and then he stepped forward.

"Madam," he said. "I am Cael Dorrin, magistrate of the Fair Copy for this bank of the Sere. This is Thursday the twenty-ninth. I am here on a warden's report of Monday the twenty-sixth and a certificate of Wednesday the twenty-eighth, concerning six households at Cinder Ford far bank. Are you the keeper of this plank."

"I keep this plank," Nesta said. She was forty-one, with thirty-two years of it in her hands. "It is not a rod on any card. It is a plank and a pail. The figure is a hundred and ninety gallons an hour and it is on the shutter for anybody to read."

"And the fifth rod," Dorrin said, glancing at the card on the post at the culvert, where the third rod stood empty with a padlock printed under it and the new line with a heading and nothing under it. "The West Flume at the third pier, in your own words, promised and not cut. Is that yours."

"It is promised," Nesta said. "In my own words. It is not cut. I said I would cut it in my own hand when there is a sheet in front of me instead of a shutter, and there is no sheet."

Dorrin nodded. He looked along the lane past her to the post at the culvert, where a tin frame ninety years old was nailed at a right angle to the wood. The frame was there. There was nothing in it.

"That frame," he said. "There is nothing in it."

"There has been nothing in it since the Tuesday of the second of this month," Nesta said. "Six hands took the card off it in daylight and turned it over and cut the tin out of the frame, and the frame went back on the post, because a frame is ninety years old and a card is six. It said CLOSED. No water. No entry. Under penalty. It named no office and named no person, and it was the reason this lane's water was a secret for six years."

"And nobody has put anything in it since."

"Nobody has put anything in it since, because there is nothing anybody has to say, and a woman of forty-one will only say it in her own words, and there is no sheet for it yet." She lifted the pail. "You want something in it. You are a magistrate. A magistrate wants a notice."

"I want a notice," Dorrin said. "A notice is a thing a court can serve. Six years of a plank with nothing on the frame beside it is six years I could not have used, and I would have used it."

Dorrin took a notebook from his coat and wrote in it, standing at the plank, with the date. One of the clerks opened the plan case on the wall.

"I am going to say my sentence," Dorrin said, "because a man who does not say his sentence in a lane is a man with a plan case and nothing else. *What is recognised is real, and what is real must be recognised, or the law cannot protect it.* That is the whole of my office. The Fair Copy keeps the plan that courts use. If water stands in a lane, the plan must show the lane, or no cart can be sent, no rate can be set, no dwelling can be defended. I am here to recognise six doors so that the law can protect them."

"Nobody crossed that ford for nineteen years," Nesta said flatly. "Now there is water in the lane and a magistrate comes with a case. That is what I know, and I did the plank at eight and it is a hundred and ninety."

"That is true," Dorrin said. "And it is to my office's cost. The return said no inhabitants for nineteen years because every hand copied the last, and nobody went and looked. The record's defect is that nobody has ever gone and looked. I have come to look." He closed the notebook. "Take me over the ford, madam. Or tell me who will."

Nesta looked at the magistrate's brushed coat and the plan case on the wall and the two clerks with nothing to say, and she looked at the shutter with a hundred and ninety on it, and she made the decision the way she made every decision: by asking who would have to carry it.

"Wick Venn will take you," she said. "Thirty-eight, one hand, wet boots already. He fitted the washer Sunday. He carried bread Tuesday. He has a stick and a step and anybody can check them." She picked up the pail. "I keep this plank. It is Thursday the twenty-ninth. The trial is done and the figure is on the shutter. The plank does not go over fords."

Dorrin wrote that down too, standing at the plank: *plank keeper declines ford, refers to W. Venn, figure on shutter*. Then he did something neither clerk expected. He took off his boots, rolled his trousers like a working man, and stood in his stockings at the edge of the plank run.

"My daughter is married on the low bank of the Alden," he said to nobody in particular, looking at the water coming up out of the notch. "Three years ago I moved her myself when the bank was re-cut. I carried her table through water to the ankle, and I did not sleep the night before the carts came, and I have never once told a court that part." He put his boots back on. "A magistrate who has never stood in lane water should not seal doors over it. That is not in my sentence. That is in my feet."

One of the clerks coughed. "Sir. The plan case."

"The plan case comes too," Dorrin said. "Held high. Thursday the twenty-ninth. Take me to Mr Venn."

---

Wick Venn took him over after noon, because Wick had wet boots already and a washer story, and because a man of thirty-eight with one hand is the man a magistrate can ask without putting anybody's hand in anything. Tarin did not go. Tarin filled the butt at six o'clock the evening before and would fill it again that evening, forty trips, about an hour and sixteen minutes, and his name was in two books and there was no form for going anywhere. He stood at the wall and watched them go down the lane to the ford, Dorrin with his coat skirts tied up and the clerks with the case held high, and his hand flat on the low wall knew where the water was before any of them reached it, and he said nothing, because Monday had taught him what saying it cost.

The far-bank lane was still standing Thursday afternoon, lower by an inch at the edges and the same in the middle, with the drain full behind the row and the bank soft. Sella Marr came out of the third door in bare feet with her skirts tied, and Jess stood in the second doorway with the boy, and Corrie came down in his waders. Six doors. Eleven people by Wick's count, counting the infant. Dorrin stood at the end of the lane a long time with his notebook, looking at the doors and the pump and the water and the drain bank, while the clerks set up the plane table on the driest ground they could find.

"It is Thursday," he said at last. "The twenty-ninth. Six doors occupied, pump under water, well under water, lane standing to the top of a boot in the middle and falling at the edges. Madam, how long have you lived in the third door."

"Nineteen years this April," Sella said. "My husband died twelve years ago. My daughter was born in it. Nobody has ever asked me for a name for anything."

"Somebody is asking now," Dorrin said. "I am asking, so that the plan can show what is here." He wrote it down. "And the water came on the Thursday, the twenty-second, into the Friday, the twenty-third."

"It woke us on the Friday, the twenty-third," Sella said. "It has stood every night since. That is seven nights. The bread came Tuesday. The magistrate comes Thursday. That is the whole of the order of it."

Dorrin walked the lane with Wick, from the ford end to the drain bank, with water over his boots, and at the drain bank he stopped and looked up the drain toward the flume mouth two miles above, where the stone launder stood empty and chained, and down it toward the Sere, full and brown and slow.

"The drain is the sole discharge of the Reach," he said. "Item one of the schedule of 1902. It stands as printed. This water came down the flume Thursday night, four hours and six minutes, three thousand four hundred and eighty-five gallons, into this drain, and the drain put it here, because the ground at the flume mouth is four feet below the field." He said it the way a man says a thing he has read and checked. "The record is not a lie. The window was lawful, for one night, by declaration of eight men on the twentieth. The declaration does not survive the night. The gate is shut and chained. All of that is right."

"Then what is wrong," Wick asked.

"That six doors stand in a sole discharge," Dorrin said. "A discharge is a channel and not a dwelling. The plan shows this bank as drain bank and foreshore, and the foreshore is the Sere's own margin, and dwellings may not stand in it. If the drain is the sole discharge, water will come here again, and next time it may not be three thousand gallons in a night. It may be a season." He closed the notebook. "I am right about the record. The plan says what it says, and the water says the plan is true. Six doors in a discharge cannot be protected where they stand."

Sella had come up behind them, bare feet silent in the water. "What does protected mean, magistrate."

Dorrin met her eyes, and for a moment the man who had carried a table through water to the ankle was standing in the lane instead of the magistrate.

"It means the Fair Copy seals the two lower doors against habitation until the bank is raised and the plan is amended," he said. "The second and third doors stand lowest. The occupants go up the lane and to Nether Sallow across the ford at the office's expense, bread and cartage paid, until the water is down and the bank is made good. Bank viewed Saturday. Return to the flood court Monday the second. That is a procedure and not a punishment, and the deadline is not mine: the court sits Monday, and any sealing must be laid before it with persons accounted for. That is four days, Thursday the twenty-ninth to Monday the second."

"Sealed," Sella said. "My door. Nineteen years."

"Sealed against water, madam, not against you. The seal is a bar and a notice, to keep persons from sleeping where water stands. It holds the boundary between dwelling and discharge, so that when the bank is raised the dwelling is still a dwelling in law and not a ruin in a channel." He said it carefully, believing every word. The line on the plan was the only thing that could send carts, and a seal was how a line was held while water stood on the wrong side of it.

Jess came down the lane with the boy on her hip, wading. "And if we do not go."

"Then I record a refusal, and the seal stands anyway, because water does not wait for a signature." Dorrin looked at the boy, nine months old, with a fist in his mouth. "Madam, I am not asking you to like it. I am asking you to be out of the lower doors by Saturday evening, the thirty-first, so that I can lay *persons accounted for* before the court on Monday."

He wrote the order on a printed form on the plane table, standing in water to the ankle, with the clerks holding the case steady: two doors sealed, occupants removed to the upper doors, bread and cartage at the office's expense, bank to be viewed Saturday, return to the flood court Monday the second of next month with persons accounted for. He signed it with the date, Thursday the twenty-ninth, and read it out standing in the lane water, because a thing in a form that is not read out is a thing in a form, and Sella heard every word with her hens on the step and her daughter in the doorway.

Sella listened without moving. Then she said, "Write my name right. Sella Marr, third door, nineteen years. If you are going to seal my door, the paper will say who it belongs to."

Dorrin wrote it. *S. Marr, third door, resident nineteen years.* The first time her name had been on any paper in nineteen years, and it was on a sealing order.

Wick walked back over the ford with them in silence, carrying his boots, having measured the middle of the lane against the third doorstep at the far end of it on the way, with his stick, in front of Sella and Jess. He gave Nesta the figure when he got back. It was a third of an inch lower in the middle than his mark of that morning and an inch lower at the edges. Nesta wrote it on the shutter beside the plank, which is a hundred and ninety, and did not divide it by anything. At the plank Tarin was starting the evening butt, first trip, six gallons, forty to go. Dorrin stopped at the wall and watched him tip it in.

"Mr Rusk," Dorrin said. "You are the man on the coping Thursday night."

"I am filling a butt," Tarin said. "It is Thursday the twenty-ninth. Forty trips. About an hour and sixteen minutes."

"I know what you are," Dorrin said. "A fair copy of a shed day-book out of a valley sixty-one miles east came down to me on Wednesday with the district return and nothing else in the cart. Four men at three points and a boy with a slate. It is the only paper about that night that anybody has sent me, and I have read it twice." He set the notebook against his knee. "Your name is in two books and neither of them is a record of a route. I am telling you because you live here now. Two doors are sealed on the far bank and the flood court sits on the Monday. If you know anything about that drain that is not on a shutter, this evening is the time to say it."

Tarin put the pail down on the wall. His hand knew where every gallon was between the tank and the Sere, two miles and four chains of empty launder and four miles two chains of full drain, and a lane standing to the ankle that would be dry underfoot when his hand said and not before, and there was no way of showing it to a magistrate who dealt in legal recognition.

"The drain will fall Saturday," he said quietly. "The lane will be dry underfoot—" He stopped. "Wick's stick says the edges are falling. The shutter says the plank is a hundred and ninety. That is what is on paper. My hand says nothing anybody can act on."

Dorrin looked at him a long moment. "A man who knows without showing is the most dangerous sentence in this basin," he said. "I am a magistrate. I deal in what can be recognised. Keep your hand off my procedure, Mr Rusk, and I will keep my seal off your butt."

He went up the lane with his clerks and his case, and about the fourth trip Nesta said the thing she had said on Tuesday she would say.

"Your hand said Wednesday, Mr Rusk, and the lane is not dry underfoot. It is Wednesday and the lane is falling and you were half a day out, and the number is on a shutter that anybody may come and read, and that is all I am going to say about it."

Tarin set the pail down on the wall. "I said Wednesday before the lane was dry. I did not say Wednesday dry."

"That is a careful way of putting it," Nesta said, "and I have kept a plank for thirty-two years and careful is what I have got instead of a rope. I am not complaining. I am putting the number up."

He went on, five, six, seven, with two doors sealed on paper across the ford and water standing in them to the ankle, and the light going off the hill on Thursday the twenty-ninth, and the boy counting each trip out loud to forty, the way he counted every evening, and Nesta marking the shutter when it was done, and none of them speaking about magistrates, because a butt is a butt and a seal is a seal and the two do not meet until Monday the court sits.
