# Chapter 0104 — Nineteen Years in the Third Door

On the Tuesday morning, the twenty-seventh of this month, Wick Venn went back over Cinder Ford with a sack of bread and a can of clean water from Nesta Sarg's butt, because eleven people in a room had said Tuesday morning and Wick looks, and because a decision of a room that is not an assembly is still a thing a man does.

The water in the lane was higher than Sunday by half an inch. He measured it against the doorstep of the third door with a stick, the way Nesta did a plank, twice, and wrote nothing down, because the stick was his own and the lane was not a rod on any card. Sella Marr watched him from the doorway with her hens on the step behind her, out of the water.

"It is Tuesday," she said. "The twenty-seventh."

"It is," Wick said. "And the man with the hand says Wednesday, and I said a day or two, and the room said Tuesday morning. So here I am, and here it is, and it has not gone down."

"The lower butt is empty," Sella said. "We finished it last night. The upper butt is half. Jess is giving the boy butt water and drinking lane water herself, boiled. The old man is boiling it too."

Wick set the bread and the can down on the step. "This is from Nether Sallow. A loaf each door, and a can for the boy. It is not a supply and not a rate and nobody's party. It is bread."

Sella looked at the bread a long time. She was about sixty, with short grey hair and hands that had kept hens nineteen years in a row nobody had ever counted.

"Come in," she said. "You have waded twice. You may as well sit down out of it."

The third door opened into a kitchen with a stone floor that was dry, because the house stood six inches above the lane, and a table with a cloth on it, and a shelf with cups, and a framed sampler on the wall that said *Keep the pump washed*. Everything in it was ordinary: a kettle, a broom, a chair with a cushion, a window looking over the drain bank. A woman who had lived nineteen years in a place that said no inhabitants had lived them the way anybody lives anywhere, with a kettle and a broom.

"Nineteen years," Wick said, sitting down. "Jess says you came nineteen years ago."

"I came with my husband in the spring, nineteen years this April," Sella said, putting the kettle on. "He worked the lower Sere boats. He died twelve years ago, of a fever. I stayed because the door was mine and the hens were mine and nobody told me to go." She set out two cups. "In all that time no man has come to count us. No form. No rate. No assembly. Twice a tallyman came for the boats and wrote the men down and not the doors. Once, eleven years ago, a woman from a city office came to look at the drain bank and stood at the end of the lane and never came down it."

"Did you ever go to anybody."

"For what." Sella said it without bitterness. "We have the pump. We have the well at the end until this week. We have butts. The boats bring flour. What was I to ask for — to be written down. A woman who is written down can be told to go. A woman who is not written down is only wet."

Wick drank the broth she gave him. It was thin and salted, made on butt water, and it warmed his hands round the cup.

"My husband could read," Sella went on. "He went to Cresset for work one winter and saw a return in an office, pinned up, and our row was on it. Six doors. And beside it, in the column, *no inhabitants*. He laughed about it all the way home. He said, Sella, we are nobody, and nobody does not pay rates. He was a boatman and that was his joke."

"Who fills the form in," Wick said quietly. "Jess asked Sunday. That is the question."

"I do not know," Sella said. "Some woman in a city office, with a form with a box that wants a number and a name, and there is no name to put in it, so she writes what was written last year. That is what my husband said. He said, somebody copies it, every year, and the copying is the record." She looked out of the window at the drain, full and brown. "And now your valley has put three thousand four hundred and eighty-five gallons into our lane, and somebody is going to have to come and look, because water does not copy. Water stands where it stands."

---

Jess came in with the boy on her hip while they were drinking, and the boy was nine months old with a round face and a fist in his mouth, and Jess was twenty with a tired face and a shawl over her shoulders.

"Is he drinking the lane," Wick asked.

"Boiled," Jess said. "We boil it and let it settle. It is brown but it does not smell." She sat down. "Mister, on Sunday you said the gate is shut and chained again. Is that true."

"It is shut," Wick said. "A man of thirty-one put a new chain on it Friday morning with his own money, and the old chain is in a shed, and the inspection cover's padlock is on. I saw the shed book. It is shut."

"Then this will go down," Jess said, looking at the boy. "A day or two, you said."

Wick looked at his cup. Tarin had said Wednesday with his hand on a wall, and Wick had said a day or two with wet boots, and eleven people had believed the boots and not the hand, and the water was half an inch higher Tuesday morning.

"It will go down," he said. "The launder is empty. Nothing more is coming." He hesitated. "But the drain is full four miles, and the fall is slow. Keep him out of the lane until it is dry underfoot. That is two days, maybe three."

Jess nodded. She did not ask how he knew. She asked a different question, the one Sella had told him to expect without telling him.

"Will they write us down now."

Wick put the cup down. He had priced days lost in a column headed *to be added to and not taken back*, and he knew what a number at the foot of a column did in about a year in a counting-house at Vellum.

"If water stands where it stands, somebody comes," he said. "A magistrate, maybe. The Fair Copy keeps the returns. If a lane is under water, a court can act where a village cannot ask." He said it carefully, because he was a man of thirty-eight with one hand and not a man with a form. "Being written down can be a door or a bill. You can leave a district at a door. You cannot leave a bill. A woman in this valley told me that, and she keeps a plank."

Sella poured more broth. "Then we will be written down wet," she said. "That is all. We were dry and nobody. Now we are wet and somebody has to look." She looked at Wick levelly. "You tell your valley that. Tell the woman with the plank. Tell the woman with the books. Six doors stood dry nineteen years and nobody came. Six doors stand wet on Tuesday the twenty-seventh and bread comes over the ford. That is the whole of it and there is no other figure for it."

Wick finished the broth and stood up. "I will tell them."

He stopped at the door with his boots in his hand. "Mrs Marr. Tuesday the twenty-seventh. When the lane is dry, what do you want. Not what the valley wants. You."

Sella looked out of the window at the drain, full and brown, at the flume mouth empty above it, at six doors with water between them.

"I want the pump washed and the well bailed and my hens back in the third door," she said. "I want Jess's boy to drink butt water and not lane water. I want the tallyman to write six doors for once instead of copying nobody." She turned round. "And I want it written that we stood wet and nobody told us to go. Five days so far, and not one person has come and told us to go, and the number is not finished yet. If paper comes, let it say that first, whatever the number turns out to be. Six doors stood wet and stayed. That is the whole of what I want, and there is no box for it on any form."

"There will be," Wick said. "A woman in a city office put your name in a box last Wednesday. Cinder Ford far-bank row, so called by residents, reported. That is what bread buys in this basin. A name with *reported* after it."

"Then reported is a start," Sella said. "Go home, bread man. Get dry. Tuesday the twenty-seventh. Tell them."

He waded back down the lane with his boots in his hand, past the pump standing in a foot of water and drawing nothing, past the old man boiling lane water on a doorstep, past the well at the end with its head under water. At the ford he stopped and looked back. Six doors and a pump and a lane, with water between them to the top of a boot, and a woman nineteen years in the third door standing in her doorway with a kettle in her hand, watching him go.

---

At Nether Sallow that Tuesday evening, Tarin filled the butt at six o'clock, forty trips, about an hour and sixteen minutes, with Nesta's pail and the twine on the handle. The boy counted out loud to forty and Nesta marked the shutter with the date and the word *full*.

It was Tuesday the twenty-seventh, and the beans stood nine inches deep in the trench, and the plank ran a hundred and ninety, and the tank stood at five and seven-eighths with no rate, and the flume stood empty and chained, and the third rod stood empty with a padlock under it. The plank, the tank, the flume, the padlock on the third rod, the shutter, the wages, the weeks: all of it held. The only thing out of place in this valley on Tuesday evening was water where a paper said nobody, and it stood half an inch above Sunday's.

Wick told them about the half inch, and the empty butt, and the boiled lane water, and the bread. He told it plain, standing at the wall, with wet boots in his hand.

"She said to tell the woman with the plank and the woman with the books," he finished. "Six doors stood dry nineteen years and nobody came. Six doors stand wet on Tuesday the twenty-seventh and bread comes over the ford."

Nesta looked at the full butt a long time. She was forty-one, with thirty-two years of a plank and a pail, and a fifth rod promised in her own words and not cut, and nineteen butts in a lane with forty-three people in them, and a man filling one of them every evening who was not her.

"Wednesday," she said quietly, not looking at Tarin. "Your hand said Wednesday, Mr Rusk. Wick's boots said a day or two. The room said Tuesday morning and Wick looks. It is Tuesday evening the twenty-seventh and the water is half an inch higher than Sunday morning." She put her hand on the shutter with the week's figures on it, never divided. "I keep this plank. The plank says a hundred and ninety and the shutter says the same. Neither says the lane. Your hand says the lane and nothing else in this basin says it."

She turned to Wick. "Thursday you go again. Bread again. Stick again. Measure the middle against the third doorstep and bring me the figure, and I will put it on this shutter beside the plank, because a shutter anybody can come and read is the only record this lane has until Perdie has a sheet to spare."

"And if the magistrate comes," Wick said. "A certificate with six households went into a bundle last Wednesday. Paper sends magistrates."

"Then a magistrate comes," Nesta said. "He comes with a case and clerks, and he stands in lane water, and he writes Sella Marr right or he does not. That is Thursday or Friday. Tonight is Tuesday." She picked up the pail. "I will send bread again Thursday. That is a thing anybody can see. The rest — hands, sticks, certificates — the rest is paper and water arguing, and I have kept a plank thirty-two years through both."

"My hand says the lane is still standing Wednesday morning and dry underfoot Thursday," Tarin said. "And there is no way of showing it to anybody, and no form in this basin for it."

"I believe you," Nesta said. She said it the way she said figures, flatly, and it landed harder than any argument in the shop. "Wick's boots said a day or two and Wick's boots were wrong by half an inch Tuesday morning. Your hand said Wednesday and the water is still rising Tuesday. I believe you, and I cannot act on it, because a plank is a plank and a shutter is a shutter and a hand on a wall is neither. Thirty-two years I have kept this plank through paper and water arguing, and the plank is what I can stand behind." She set the pail down in the notch and squared it. "You will hear one more thing out of me and then I am going up the lane. I am not going to pretend Wednesday has not been said out loud in this lane twice, because it has, and a figure that is true once and never spoken is not a figure. It is true twice. Wick has Thursday's figure coming to this shutter, and if your hand is wrong you will hear me say so at six o'clock in front of the boy, and I will not be pleasant about it."

She went up the lane with the boy, and Wick went home to Brant, and Tarin stayed at the wall with the full butt in the dusk on Tuesday the twenty-seventh, with his hand flat on the low wall and two miles and four chains of empty channel under his palm and a lane full of brown water at the end of a drain he could feel and nobody would act on.

He stood there long after the light went off the hill, with the plank running below him at a hundred and ninety and the beans standing in the trench and the drain full four miles, feeling the lane water half an inch higher than Sunday and the lower butt empty and the boy asleep in the fourth door, all of it true and none of it checkable. Sixty-six days in the valley. Five shillings a day, six days, cottage at no rent, fourteen weeks to the sixth of the month after next. A name in two books four hundred yards apart. A hand that knew where water was and no way of giving it back and no room that would listen.

He took his hand off the wall. The stone was cool. He had been right on Monday, and being right was the most dangerous sentence in the basin, and eleven people in a room had been right not to listen to it, and on Thursday at six o'clock he would have to stand at that wall with a number and be told by a woman with a shutter whether he had been right, and she had told him already that she would not be pleasant about it. There was no way to be ready for that except to be wrong first, and he could not arrange to be wrong, and that was the whole of what a hand on a wall was worth him on Tuesday the twenty-seventh.
