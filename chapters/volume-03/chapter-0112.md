# Chapter 0112 — If Willing

On the Wednesday morning, the fourth of next month, the tallyman of the lower Sere came over Cinder Ford with a printed form and a wet ink-horn, and the row counted itself for the first time in nineteen years, and one man in it would not give his name, and nobody argued with him.

The tallyman was Amos Nye, forty-one, twelve years in the job, and he was not the boatman at the landing, who had landed flour there nineteen years and never put a name on a form for it. His instruction was printed at the top of every sheet he carried: *copy forward unless reported otherwise*. He had a soft pen and a hard hand and he had never written the word *door* in the district return.

The lower Sere warden came with him, a lean man of about fifty called Harker, with a stick and a watch, who had reported the lane on the twenty-sixth of the month before last and asked for instruction, because a warden records what he can see and asks about what he cannot decide. The instruction came back from the flood court on the second in three words and a conditional: *the tally of persons by name, if willing.*

"If willing," Harker said, standing at the ford end with the form on his knee, "is the whole of my instruction. I have had two days to think about it and I have not found a way to make it mean anything else, and I am not going to try, because if I go round that lane asking questions until somebody says no, then the no is mine and not theirs."

"You will go door by door," Nye said. "You will stand at the step and ask. If a person says no you will write no, and if a person says nothing you will write nothing given, and you will not ask twice, and you will not ask anybody on anybody else's behalf."

"And the houses that are not here."

"Are in the return as they were," Nye said, "and the return says *no inhabitants*, and I have copied that forward twelve times, and today it will be wrong in a different way from the other twelve, and I would like to be finished with the lane before noon."

---

They went up the lane together, and the lane did what a lane does when a man with a form comes up it, which is to come out of its doors. Jess Marr was on the step of the second door with the boy on her hip before the warden had got to the third. The widow of the first door came out and dried her hands on her apron and asked whether she wanted to say her name out loud, and the warden said she wanted to say it however she liked saying it, and she said it, and it went down.

The form has boxes. The first box is the district and the row number, and the row number went in on Monday in Mara Quill's own hand and came up here on the warden's paper, and Nye looked at it and then at the lane and then at his own twelve years of the same line and said nothing, and wrote it.

Nye's hand shook a little on the second box, which is *houses*, and it went in as six.

"Door by door," Harker said. "Not house by house. A house is a thing this office can count without asking anybody, and a door is the thing we are here about, and if the two come to the same number in this lane that is a coincidence and not a result."

"Then write the word doors under it," Nye said, and did.

The second door. Jess Marr, twenty, and the boy, who is ten months now and teething and was named on nobody's paper at all, and her husband Wyn Marr, thirty-one, who had gone to the landing with a barrow because there were nine loads of stone on the bank and a barrow was the point of a man with two hands.

"Three," Harker said. "And names, if you are willing."

"Jess Marr. Wyn Marr. And that one has no name on anything you have ever had," Jess said, "because I have never been asked for it, and if you want to put one on him you may, and I have been thinking about it for eleven days."

Nye wrote it. Then he said, "I would like to enter the husband separately, Mr Marr, for the reason that there is a copy of a return hanging up in an office in Cresset with your row on it and *no inhabitants* in the column beside it, and I would like to know what you want done with that line."

Wyn Marr put down the barrow.

"I want it struck," he said.

"That is not a thing anybody can do," Nye said, "and I am the man who would have to do it, so I will tell you why. A return cannot be struck; a return can only be answered. If this year's return says six doors and eleven people, then next year a clerk turns the leaf and reads this year's, and the line that says *no inhabitants* goes on being true about the year it was written. It is not a lie any more. It is a record of a year in which nobody went and looked. You cannot get rid of it and you should not want to, because in about forty years it will be the only proof in the basin that your wife and her mother were ever here, and there will be men writing about the year the far bank was unlisted, and they will want a document to argue with."

"So it stands," Wyn said.

"It stands," Nye said, "with your wife and your boy and you written underneath it. And that is the whole of what a return can do for anybody, and I have been copying a worse thing forward for twelve years and never once said so out loud, because nobody was standing in front of me."

The third door was Sella Marr's own, and she gave her name and her nineteen years in one breath, and one person.

The fourth door gave three: Mr Trew, seventy-two; Mrs Trew, sixty-nine; and Nell, their granddaughter, nineteen, who gave her name and then said, without being asked, "I am the only one of the three of us who was not here on the twenty-third," and the warden wrote *one absent at the date of the flood* and she said no, do not write that, write *one at her mother's*, and he wrote that.

The fifth door was Mrs Ada Bray's, and there were two in it, and one of them was on the water to Verrin and would not be back before the month turned.

"I am Ada Bray," she said. "That is my house and my door and it is my name on it now and I have had it nineteen years and never wrote it down. My son Harl is on the water to Verrin and he is not back before the month turns. He is not here, so do not ask me to say things for him."

"Then he is one away," Harker said, and wrote it, and looked at what he had written. "Madam, I will tell you what that box does. It says two in this door and one of them away. In a year somebody reads that and is pleased with it, because a door with somebody away in it is a door with a life in it. Nobody in a counting-house is ever comforted by a column that says nobody. I have never had cause to say that before."

"Then say it again next year," Mrs Bray said, "when you come back with the bundle, and if by then I am the only one here, write that I am the only one here and do not put his name under mine as a courtesy."

---

The sixth door is Corrie Lille's and it is where the morning stopped.

Harker asked him the way he had asked the other five, in the same words, standing on the step. Nye had the pen ready. The lane was behind them and about nine people had come out of five doors.

"Corrie Lille," Harker said. "Sixth door. How many in it."

"One," Corrie said. "And it is me, and I have been the record of this row on my own hook for eleven years, and the hook says eleven, and the hook has said eleven since the Sunday, and you may have the hook."

Nye wrote *sixth door, one, count of the row given by C. Lille, of the door, from his own hook, eleven for the whole row*.

Then he said, "And your name, Mr Lille, if you are willing."

Corrie Lille did not say no straight away, and everybody in the lane noticed that, and everybody in the lane waited.

"I will give you the number," he said. "I gave you the number on the Sunday in the open, and I will give it to you again in the open as many times as you want it, door by door, and it is eleven and it is on a hook and it is in my head and it has been both of those for eleven years. I will not give you my name into a book that a man in a city reads."

"Why not," Nye said, and he asked it the way a man asks a thing he genuinely wants the answer to, which is to say flatly, without any weight behind it at all.

"Because a book is the record," Corrie said, "and a hook is a record, and a hook has one man on it, and a book has a district. You put my name in the book and the book is the record of this row, and then the next man does not come and stand in the lane and ask a woman how many she is. He sends for the book. And a hook you can burn, and a man can be wrong in his head, and a book does not have either of those doors in it." He pulled his waders up apeg. "That is all of it and I am not doing it out of spite at anybody, and I would like it written that I said yes to the number and no to the name, and that I said it once and it was not argued with."

"It is not argued with," Harker said, to nobody, to the whole lane, and then to Nye, "write that on the form and let us have the next door."

But there was no next door, because the sixth was the last, and the lane was standing in the sun with a form with five names on it and a sixth box empty and a hook in a man's house that nobody in a city was ever going to read.

Corrie went in. He did not slam the door. He took his waders off on the step, which is a thing a man does when he has decided a thing and is not in a hurry about it.

Harker said, to Nye, in the flat voice of an officer doing an instrument correctly, "Enter it as unwilling, name withheld, and enter the person as counted, one, and enter the count of the row as given, eleven. It is lawful. The court said *if willing* and he has said it plainly and I will not put the words *refused* on that form, because he did not refuse anything, he answered one question and declined another."

Perdie Ammid had a sheet out before Nye had the pen back and wrote on it, in her own hand, that on Wednesday the fourth of next month, at the sixth door of the Cinder Ford far-bank row, the witness Corrie Lille gave the count of the row, door by door, from his own hook, being eleven, and declined to give his name to the form, and said no further reason than that a book is the record and a hook has one man on it, and that his answer was taken once and was not argued with. She read it back to the lane, and the lane heard it, and Nye asked her whether she would countersign it, and she said no, because she was not on the form and had no business on the form, which was correct, and which is the kind of thing that makes a document worth something in about four years.

Tarin was standing at the pump the whole time with his hand on the frame, and had been for forty minutes, and it had told him nothing whatever, and at some point in the middle of the fifth door he had offered the count to Nye as a check — eleven, the same eleven, which he knew because of the way a lane is built and not because of a hook — and Nye had looked at him politely and said:

"Mr Rusk, I have a column in a book for the boats and the men, and I have never once in twelve years put a door in it, and if I put your figure in it this morning then in forty years there will be two ways of knowing how many people live on this bank and a clerk will have to choose, and he will choose the one that costs him nothing, and it will be yours, because a man's hand is a thing a district can be built on and a hook cannot."

"That is fair," Tarin said.

"It is more than fair. It is the only reason I am able to stand here." Nye dipped the pen. "Ask me a question about the water. I will write that down and I will not sleep on it."

---

They were off the bank and on the landing by eleven, and the water was down and the Sere was running clear over the stones, and Nye sat on the landing stone and entered the whole thing in the district return, and the district return said *no inhabitants* for nineteen years and was now going to have a line under it that said six doors and eleven persons, of whom nine were named, one name withheld by the person himself, and one person absent and not named for him because his mother would not be asked for it.

"That is the first line I have ever written in that column that was not in the column the year before," Nye said, "and I have written that column twelve times, and I would like somebody to understand what that means, which is that next year I come back here again, and the year after, and the year after that, and the district has a rate on it now, and there is a form for districts with rates, and I will be the man who brings it." He capped the ink-horn. "I have never come back to this lane in twelve years. I have never had a reason to."

Harker wrote his own note and read it out: *Wednesday the fourth of next month, tally of persons taken at the row, door by door, on the court's order of the second, if willing. Eleven persons, six doors. Nine names given. One name withheld by the person, recorded unwilling, not argued with. One person absent and not named for him at his mother's request. Count of the row given by the person of the sixth door from his own hook. No person removed and no person added. Copy of this note to the district file, to the Fair Copy, and to the warden's own book.*

Nobody thanked the warden, and he did not ask, and Nye packed his form into his satchel and put the satchel on the cart and the cart went on up the road to Vellum with a line in it that had never been in it, and the line would be copied forward every year after this by his hand or another man's, and that was the machine, starting, in the only way it ever starts: with a man doing his job properly.

---

At the plank at Nether Sallow that evening, the butt took an hour and eighteen minutes because the water at the notch was cold and the boy counted slower in the cold and laughed about it. Seventy-fourth day. Nesta marked the shutter, and the drain figure went on the shutter beside the plank, and the frame on the culvert post went past empty, ninety years old, with nothing in it and nothing to be said in it.

At about the twenty-second trip the boy said, "Eleven, door by door, off a hook, and one man would not say his name."

"Eleven," Tarin said.

"You said eleven on the first of next month too." The boy waited with the slate under his arm, and Tarin could feel him waiting, because a boy of fifteen who has learned to wait is a boy who has been in rooms.

"You had it nearly right and it matters that you had it nearly right," Tarin said. "On the first of next month it was on a sheet, in the same man's hand, and it came off his own hook, and Mrs Ammid wrote down that it did and read it back to nine people. Not a hook on its own. A hook, and a woman with a slate, and nine people who heard it. That is the whole of what a count is in this valley and it is a poor thing, and it is all there is."

"Then it is the same eleven both times, and both times off a hook, and a hook is not a book."

"It is the same eleven both times," Tarin said, "and there is a number on a schedule in a file in a city that I have not said out loud in fourteen years, and the two numbers have nothing whatever to do with one another, and there is nobody alive I could stand in front of and say that to."

The boy wrote it down. He did not read it out, which was correct, and he did not ask, which was also correct, and he went on counting. At about the thirtieth trip Tarin stood at the bottom of the bank and thought about a hook in a sixth door and a blank box in a printed form, and about how a thing that is not written can be asked about every year for eleven years by a man who is not to blame, and how a thing written down once is a door and a bill at once, and how a woman in a kitchen on a Tuesday had said the first half of that sentence out loud without knowing she was quoting anybody.

He carried the last ten without counting, because a man does not count the last ten of forty, and at the fortieth the butt was full and the water came up out of the notch the way it had come up for six years under a notice that had said there was no water, and there was no form in this basin in which a man could go and stand in a sixth door and be told the number of himself.
