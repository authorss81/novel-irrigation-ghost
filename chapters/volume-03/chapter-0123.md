# Chapter 0123 — Read It Aloud

On the Sunday morning, the fifteenth of next month, the reading room at the Municipal Memory Office in Vellum was taken for two days, because the hearing of witnesses was on the Thursday, and the two clerks who could copy a fair hand of a hundred and eighty-one years old were working a Saturday and a Sunday to have the transcript in the file by the Wednesday, and because the room was the only one with a long table in it that was not also somebody's office.

The book came out of the store at ten past nine in a wooden case with a lid tied on by a cord, and the cord was cut and retied, and had been cut and retied twice since the fourth of next month, and Mr Aldwin carried it himself because he has kept the store twenty-two years and did not want it carried by a boy.

He set it on the long table under three lamps, and undid the cord, and lifted the lid, and did not open the book.

Mrs Quill said, "Open it."

"I am going to," he said. "I have been going to for a quarter of an hour."

"You have been going to in front of witnesses," she said, "which is different, and it is the correct way to do it, and I would like that in the transcript, and I would like it now rather than afterwards, because afterwards is when it gets put in as though somebody thought of it."

She wrote it in her own hand at the head of a printed sheet, and the woman at the far-bank desk — about thirty-five, ink on two fingers, four years at that desk and four years of the Cinder Ford returns in a press at her elbow — came in and sat down, and Aldwin opened the book.

It was worse than the description and better than the description. The front leaves came open as far as the eleventh day of the flood year, and there the pages were firm and the ink was brown at the edges and the writing was small and even and entirely legible. Then three leaves had gone into a single wad about the thickness of a thumb, and after the wad there were four days that could be read by holding the book against a lamp and by nobody wanting to, and then the last gathering was stubs, about a dozen of them, clean at the binding, the last week of the year.

"Eleven days, four days, twelve stubs," Mrs Quill said. "That is what there is. Read those and no more, and where the wedge is, say *here the leaves are together and the text cannot be taken without taking ink off two others with it*, and at the back say *here the gathering is cut at the binding and there are twelve stubs dated the last week of the year*."

"And nothing about what was on them."

"Nothing whatever about what was on them," Mrs Quill said. "Not one word, in this room, on this transcript, or in anything that goes down the road. A stub has a date on it. That is all a stub has got, and a date is not a sentence."

The clerks wrote it all down, and then Mrs Quill read the first leaf aloud.

*Orders received. Gate the third opened, sixth hour, by word of the upper villages, for the melt. Gate the fifth shut, ninth hour, by word of the lower bank, against the rise.*

She read it the way a woman reads a form in a shop, standing up, unhurried, giving the words to the room rather than to the two men with the pens, and by the fourth line the woman at the far-bank desk had stopped writing her own hand and the room had gone the way a room does when a thing it has been handed turns out to be a thing it has not been handed before.

"That is the first order in a hundred and eighty-one years," Mrs Quill said, "and there is no signature at the foot of it. There is no *by order of*, and there is no *authority*, and there is no column for one. There is a column headed *orders received* and a column for the hour and a column for the words used, and the words used are *by word of*."

"Three of them," the woman at the desk said. "Upper villages. Lower bank. Middle parishes. Three bodies of people and a book that took what they said."

"And not one of them saying *from*," Mrs Quill said. "It is a receiving book. Every order in this volume came in from somewhere. Nobody wrote one in it. And that means the three hands on these leaves are not three men arguing with each other about a flood. They are three offices writing down what arrived, each of them at a different desk, on a different day, in a different building, each of them writing as though the writing down were the same as deciding."

She turned a leaf, and read the second.

*Gate the third opened, sixth hour, by word of the upper villages, for the melt.* — no, that had been the first. The second leaf gave the ninth day, and the fourth, and the fifth, and the sixth, gate orders, opening and shutting, for the melt and against the rise, and she read them through without comment, and the clerks' pens went on, and at about a quarter past eleven she came to the eleventh day and read two entries and then stopped in the middle of the room with the book flat under both hands.

"Go on," the woman at the desk said.

"I am going to. I want the room to have it as it is on the page and not as I am saying it." Mrs Quill read it again. *Eleventh day. Gate the third shut, ninth hour, by word of the lower bank, against the rise. Entered the same afternoon, by the hand on this leaf.* And above it, on the same leaf, an hour earlier in the day: *Eleventh day. Gate the third shut, fifth hour, by word of the lower bank, against the rise.*

Nobody said anything.

"There are two orders in one hand on one day for the same gate, four hours apart," she said, "and the earlier one is the eleventh day at the fifth hour and the later one is the eleventh day at the ninth hour, and both are true on the page, and the second does not refer to the first, and it is in the same hand, and the hand is the same hand that has been copying forward at the lower bank since before my mother was born."

She read the entry above it again, pointing at it with a fingernail so that the men with the pens could find the line.

"And that one, the sixth hour, is by the hand on the front leaves, and the words are *by word of the upper villages*, and it says *gate the third opened*, and it is the eleventh day as well, and it is in the leaf above, and it has no hour at the foot of it that matches anything, and nobody has struck it out and nobody has written *see above* against it, and there is no marginal note in that book anywhere in nine pages, in a hundred and eighty-one years, and a flood book of nine days of a river that has a word in the valley for what it does when it comes, has no marginal note in it at all."

She shut the book gently and took her hands off it.

"That is on the page," she said. "That is all that is on the page, and I have read it aloud, and two men have written it, and one of them is the man who read it as well and the other is the man who wrote and did not read, and we are both witnesses, and that is the way a transcript is made. I am not going to say one word more about what it means until the whole of the legible part has been read in order, and I am not going to say it on Wednesday, and I am not going to say it in a form."

Mr Aldwin had been standing at the end of the table for an hour without sitting down, and he sat down.

"May I say the thing I have been thinking since the fifth hour," he said, "and I will stop if it is not wanted."

"Say it."

"A book of orders received is not kept to find out what happened. A book of orders received is kept so that afterwards nobody can say the office did not know." He put his hands flat on the table. "I have kept a store twenty-two years and I have seen the reason a thing is kept about nine hundred times, and it is almost never curiosity. That book was kept because a flood was coming, or a flood had come, and when the inquiry sat somebody was going to ask what the office was told, and the office was going to want to be able to say that it was told exactly so, at an hour, from a body of people, in a hand. That is what those nine days are. They are not a record of a river. They are a record of three offices being able to say *we did as we were told*."

Nobody in the room contradicted him, and one of the two clerks wrote it down, and Mrs Quill made him write it again because he had put it in his own hand and not as read aloud, and he did it twice, and the second time it was in the transcript as hers and not his, and he did not object, because that is what a transcript is.

"Then that is the answer to the room," she said. "Not a fact about water. A fact about why there are nine pages with no note on them. A book kept to prove that three offices obeyed three sets of orders, in three rooms, without hearing of one another, cannot carry a note saying the orders did not agree, because the first man to write that note would be the man admitting that somebody in his own office had been told to do something impossible. In a hundred and eighty-one years, across nine days, three of them never wrote it. And the ninth day is not the week the water cut through the middle basin. I want that said now, before we read any more, because I have been in this trade long enough to know what happens to a room that is handed the first half of a thing on a Sunday."

"Enter it," the woman at the desk said, and wrote it herself, and signed it, and pushed it across the table.

Then Mrs Quill went round the wedge and read the four days that came after it.

*Twelfth day. Gate the seventh opened, dawn, by word of the middle parishes, for the melt.* And, out of the hand that had the fifth hour on the eleventh: *Gate the fifth shut, third hour, by word of the lower bank, against the rise.* And out of the front hand, the sixth hour: *Gate the fifth opened, second hour, by word of the upper villages, for the melt.*

"Same gate. Same morning. Opened at the second hour and shut at the third," she said, "by two different offices, neither of which had any idea the other had written anything, and a gate that is shut raises what is above it and a gate that is opened lets it down, and both offices were acting on what they had been told was true of the water, and both of them were told, and both of them wrote it down."

"Eleven pages," the woman at the desk said, "and not one line in any of them that says *the other office has said otherwise*."

"No. And that is not because they were careful men or careless men. It is because a book has no line for it," Mrs Quill said. "If I had been an office in that year and had wanted to write *and see the entry of the other office of this day*, I should have had to invent the column, and no office invents a column during a flood, and afterwards nobody invents a column at all, because the book is full and it has been full for sixty years and it is copied forward."

She squared the book and looked at the clerks.

"Copy the rest of the legible days in order and put the wedge in where the wedge is," she said. "And write at the head of this sheet, in capitals, that it is a transcript of what was legible and nothing else, and that the book is at Vellum and is not produced, and that no person in this room has an opinion and nobody is to write one down."

"Ma'am," the older of the two clerks said, "I have been copying for twenty-six years and nobody has ever told me not to have an opinion before the first page."

"Then this is a good Sunday for you," Mrs Quill said, and the woman at the far-bank desk laughed, which she had not been known to do in the four years Mrs Quill had been passing her desk.

Mrs Quill wrote one more line at the foot of the printed sheet before the copying began, and the woman at the desk read it upside down.

"Enter that the book was not produced and is not produced and is not to be produced, it having been brought out of the store for the purposes of this transcript only and returned to the case at the hour named below, and that the case stands in the store and the cord is upon it." The woman at the desk looked up. "Why did you write that first, before the copying."

"Because in about a year somebody will ask whether the book was in the room," Mrs Quill said, "and the answer is going to be that it was, and it was, and the only reason it is worth writing is that the last time a book left a record house in this valley nobody wrote a line saying it had left."

The book went back into the case at half past two on the Sunday afternoon and the cord went on twice and the case went onto the shelf in the store and the shelf was the shelf it had stood on since the fourth of next month, and Mr Aldwin said, aloud, to nobody, that it was the third time in his career he had taken that case down and the first two had been a man in a bad hat and a woman in a plain dress, and that he could not tell either of them from the other and did not intend to try, and that was the whole of his twenty-two years and it was not nothing.

At Nether Sallow that evening the butt was filled at six, forty trips, an hour and eighteen minutes, and the boy read the tank off his own slate at eight that morning and put it on Bess's slate under nothing else, and Nesta marked the shutter *full, Sunday the fifteenth of next month, eighty-fifth day*, and the frame on the culvert post went past empty for the thirteenth day, and Tarin Rusk stood at the plank at half past nine that night with a fortnight in front of him and did not know one single thing that had been said in a room sixty-one miles east, and would not until the cart came up on the Saturday, and had not asked, and could not have asked, and would not have been answered.
