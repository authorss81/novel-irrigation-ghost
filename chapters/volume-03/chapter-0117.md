# Chapter 0117 — Nineteen Shillings and Twopence

On the Monday morning, the ninth of next month, Cobb came over Cinder Ford with a printed form in a stiff cover, and read it out in the lane, because a bill that is not read out is a bill that arrives on a door without anybody having told the door it was coming.

It was the Fair Copy's form for a work of improvement charged to a district. It had a heading and nine boxes, and Cobb read the whole of it standing on the coping of the pump with the sun behind him and about nine people in the lane, one of them holding a windlass handle because she had just finished putting a bucket down.

"District: Cinder Ford far-bank row, so called by residents, reported. Row number as entered, and the number is good and I am not touching it. Work: bank at lane end, making-good to the line with a bond, a joint and a date in the coping, executed on the seventh of next month. Materials: stone from the ford bank, nine loads; clay, one cart; lime for the joint, one bushel. Four shillings and eightpence." He turned the sheet so the lane could see it. "Labour: six persons, forty-six hours between them, at threepence the hour, being eleven shillings and sixpence. Total, nineteen shillings and twopence."

Corrie Lille made a noise that was not quite a laugh.

"Nineteen shillings and twopence," he said. "That is a hen and a half and a change for a bucket. And the going rate for a man with a barrow in the year I came was fourpence, and at fourpence your forty-six hours comes to fifteen shillings and fourpence and your total comes to a pound exactly, and it would not be a penny out. So the hour is three-quarters of the hour I knew and the money is fourpence out of twenty shillings, and there is the whole of it: they have taken the price off the work and left it on the paper. It is still the first money anybody has ever wanted off this lane, and I would like somebody to write down that I said both of those."

"That is the sum for the works," Cobb said. "I want the next three things read out with it, because a form read in halves is worse than a form not read at all. And I will say before I go on that the sum is at the district rate for common work, and the district rate is threepence the hour, and it has been threepence the hour since a year before any person in this lane was born. One. That sum is charged to the district. Two. The district enters the district book on Thursday the twelfth of next month, and the entry will be made in the district book and not in the return, and the return already has your six doors and your eleven persons in it from Wednesday. Three. The rate is struck at the next valuation, and the valuation has not been made, and I cannot give you a rate this morning and neither can anybody else in this lane."

"Then what is it for," Sella said. "You have brought me a bill for nineteen shillings and twopence and told me you cannot tell me what I will pay. What is the paper for."

"To make the district," Cobb said. "That is what it is for. There is no charge on you this year and none next, and in about a year a man will come through with a book and make a valuation, and the valuation will be low, because the ground here is foreshore and the drainage is a discharge and there is nothing on it worth a great deal, and the rate will be a small sum a door, and it will be a small sum a door forever. That is the whole of what this paper does, and it is a good paper, and I have read it out to nine people in a lane and I will read it out at each of the six doors on Thursday, because that is what the notice says, and if I do it once here and not at the doors then on Thursday you will all be talking about a form nobody saw."

"Who is paying the nineteen shillings and twopence in the meantime," Jess Marr said. She had the boy on her hip and the boy was eleven months and had been eleven months on nobody's paper until the Wednesday.

"Nobody," Cobb said. "The office carries the works until the district is in the book and the rate is struck, and then the office recovers the sum over ten years out of the rate. That is a normal arrangement and there is nothing wrong with it and I have said so, because the alternative is that nobody ever pays and a man in a shed does it for nothing, and that is how a valley ends up with a ditch nobody cut."

"A ditch nobody cut," Corrie said, "and a bank nobody laid, and a lane nobody stands in. Yes. I know that valley. I have been in it eleven years." He stood up off the pump coping, which took him a while. "Magistrate's clerk. Answer me one thing plain, because you have been plain all morning and I have got used to it. My name is not on the Wednesday's form. I did not give it and I said no once and nobody argued with me. Is my name wanted on this one."

"No," Cobb said.

"Then this one is on doors," Corrie said, and looked round the lane at nobody in particular, and said, "Then I told you on Wednesday and you all heard it and there is the first thing it has done, which is to cost me nothing. Write that down, Perdie. That is worth writing down. A man says no to a book and a year later he finds out what the no was worth, and what it was worth was nothing, and now I know it, and there is not a penny on my door that is not on every door in this lane."

Perdie wrote it on the sheet, and read it back, and Corrie did not correct it. And then Corrie said the other thing that had been sitting in him since the third, which was that the form had three names of the row on it and one name of Nether Sallow, and that the row was going to be charged for the man from across the ford, and Cobb looked at the sheet for a while and then said that it was, and that he had not been able to see a way to make it otherwise, and that he had thought about it on the water on the way over. Nobody said anything about that either.

---

The lane asked the questions the shop had asked on the Friday, and asked them worse, because a shop has nine people in it who can go home and a lane has the people the questions are about.

Mrs Ada Bray asked the one that mattered, from the fifth door with her arms folded, and she asked it to Cobb and not to anybody else.

"My son's name will be on the door and he is on the water to Verrin and he is not back before the month turns, which is the month after next. Whose door is it when he is gone, and who answers for it, and what happens if we do not pay."

"You do, madam," Cobb said. "The charge follows the door and not the person, and you will answer for it while he is away, and if the sum is not met the district may be distrained, and a distraint takes the goods that are in the doors of the district, and it takes them from whoever is in the doors at the time. That is what the law says and I am not going to soften it, and I will tell you the true thing as well, which is that this office has never distrained a district with no rate, because for a hundred and thirty years there has not been one, and nobody in the building knows how it starts, and if it ever starts it will be in a morning with a man in a lane and a cart behind him and not with a letter."

"Then I have a month of him not here," Mrs Bray said, "and that is long enough to sell two hens, and I would like to have said that to somebody this week."

"You have whatever you have," Cobb said, "and I will say the thing no one in an office says, which is that the sum is not the danger. A door that is rated can be mortgaged, because a mortgage wants a rate to lean on, and a door with a rate on it can be sold for arrears, and this lane is foreshore of the Sere and foreshore of the Sere is worth nothing to anybody. So I would not lose a night's sleep over the sum. I would lose one over the fact that in about a year there will be a number against your door saying what your door is worth, written by a man who has never been in it, on the authority of a schedule printed in 1902 by men who had never heard of it. That is the danger and it is not the money."

Nobody in the lane had an answer to that, and Cobb packed the form away, and Perdie Ammid stood in the sun with a sheet in her hand and did not write it down, and then wrote it down, and Tarin watched her do it and understood why she had waited.

"Mr Cobb." It was the girl of nineteen from the fourth door, Nell Trew, who had been up the hill with a load of lime and had put it down at a point in the conversation where it could be heard. "What does the district get."

"Because you have all been talking about what we get," she said, "and nobody has said what the thing is for. If I pay a small sum on this door forever, what is the small sum for."

"You get the bank," Cobb said. "The bond, the joint, the date in the coping, a district that can say it paid for it, and a right to be told in advance if the drain is coming. That is the whole of what a district is in this office. It is not a supply. It is not water. It is a place that exists on paper and can be spoken about in a room."

"Then it is a good trade," Nell Trew said, "and I do not understand why everybody in this lane is standing about as though it were a bad one. I have been here three weeks. Before that I was in service at Cresset and paid four pounds a year and told it was a favour. This is nineteen shillings and twopence and a bank that lasts twenty years and somebody has to answer for it in writing. I would like somebody to explain to me what the worse offer was."

Nobody answered her, and Tarin Rusk, who was on the coping with a bad shoulder and a good ear, understood the whole of the lane in the four seconds after she stopped talking.

Cobb looked at the girl for a while and then he said the truest thing a man in his trade had said in that lane, which was that he had no word for it either and had been doing the work nineteen years.

"A favour can be withdrawn," he said. "A rate cannot be argued out, because a rate is not a favour, it is an arithmetic, and the arithmetic goes on whether you like it or not, and there is a difference between a thing that is done for you and a thing you are counted in, and I have never had to explain that difference to a person having to pay for it, and I have not got it ready, and I am not going to invent it on a Monday morning in a lane." He put the cover back on the form. "You are right that it is a good trade. I have thought so twice since the first. Do not tell your grandmother I said it and I will say it again in a worse place."

---

"Perdie," Sella said. "Say the thing you have been not saying since the Friday."

Perdie looked up.

"You wrote a sentence in this valley in about the middle of the month before last and it went onto a sheet and into a city office in a margin, and I have not heard you say it since and neither has anybody in this lane." Sella had her hands behind her back. "You can go out of a door. You cannot go out of a bill. That is your sentence, and you wrote it, and I have been carrying it round for a fortnight, and this morning you have brought a bill to a lane that has never had one, and I would like to know what you think of your own words now that they have arrived on my doorstep with a form on them."

Perdie Ammid, who was fifty-four and had kept four books in a chest for twenty-six years and had no book at all now and thirty-six numbered sheets in a satchel, looked at the form in Cobb's hand for a long moment.

"I think I was right," she said, "and I think it is the worst sentence anybody in this valley has ever written down, and I think I would not unsay it, and I would like it noted that I have said both of those out loud in a lane in daylight and have not gone round the back."

"Write that down as well," Sella said.

"It is on the sheet," Perdie said. "I am not adding to it in a lane. It goes on a numbered sheet with the number at the top or it does not go at all, and there are thirty-seven of them now and no book, and the satchel goes down the road on Friday with the notice of the district book on top of it, and you may all read the lot at the shop, and if I have said a foolish thing you may tell me and I will write it down and say so beside it."

---

They broke the middle of the morning at the ford end and Cobb read the form out a second time, because he had promised a thing about doors and a lane is a bad place to hear a number once, and the second reading was shorter and stuck better.

Then the lane went back to its business, which was boiling water and carrying it, and a hen-coop that had gone into the drain on the thirtieth and come back, and a boy of eleven months who wanted the windlass.

Tarin went over the ford at eleven with Perdie and her satchel and one shoulder that had carried a hod hooked against his chest for two hours on the Saturday, and he said nothing at all about the form or the rate or the valuation, and Perdie said nothing either, and at the top of the lane she stopped and said:

"You are not going to ask me what I wrote on the thirty-seventh."

"You are going to tell me on the Friday when it is read out," Tarin said, "like everything else in this valley, and I will be at the plank at six when you say it, and the butt does not care what is on a sheet."

"No," Perdie said. "It does not. I have noticed."

The tank stood at six and an eighth. The plank ran at a hundred and ninety. The flume stood empty and chained two miles and four chains up the hill. The third rod on the card at the culvert stood empty with a padlock printed under it and a new line with a heading and nothing under the heading, and the frame beside it was ninety years old and empty, and the frame was going to stay empty, and there was going to be a rate on six doors on Thursday the twelfth, and the rate was going to be a small number forever, and a man was going to be in a lane reading it out at each door, and the man was going to ask at each door how many people lived there, and the row was going to find out at the fourth door that a form with a box in it wants a number and not a person.

The butt was filled at six. Seventy-ninth day, an hour and eleven minutes, forty trips, the boy counting out loud, the shutter marked, and the twine on the handle wound twice and tucked under, and it was Sunday the eighth's work that did it and not the man's, and nobody said so, and Tarin carried the empty pail up the bank and put it by the wall and stood with his hand flat on cold stone and did not know a single thing about the rate, and had not needed to, and would be told on Thursday, at each door, in daylight, by a clerk with a stiff cover.
