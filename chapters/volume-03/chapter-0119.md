# Chapter 0119 — Two Numbers

On the Wednesday morning, the eleventh of next month, Perdie Ammid read out sheet thirty-nine in the shop at Brant, in front of about eleven people, and sheet thirty-nine had a line in it that no form in the basin had a box for, and the reading of it was the plainest thing that has happened in that shop since the twenty-sixth of the month before last, and the plainest things are the ones nobody can argue with.

She read it standing up, because everything from the sixteenth was read out standing up.

*Thirty-nine. Wednesday the eleventh of next month. Of the witness schedule of the flood court, the hearing of witnesses being the nineteenth of next month. The witness of the reading of sheet thirty-two at the record house, being Mr T. Rusk, of no district and of no office, gave his evidence in writing at the place of the holding, Nether Sallow, on the tenth of next month, before the warden of the lower Sere and in the presence of Mrs P. Ammid. The column headed papers read out to the witness in his hearing, by number, is answered one, being sheet thirty-two. The warden has written true in the margin against that answer. Four sentences of evidence were read out and were signed by the witness. The witness has signed, and dated the tenth, and has named the place.*

*And then, in Mrs Ammid's own hand, and with nothing above it, and no ruling, and no number against it because it is not on the form:*

*That on the thirty-first of the month before last three numbered sheets were read out at the record house below the culvert, being sheets thirty-one, thirty-two and thirty-three, and that Mrs Ammid read all three and is the only person who can say so; and that the court has asked this witness for the number of papers read out to him in the hearing of sheet thirty-two; and that this witness has answered one; and that both of those numbers are true; and that they are not answers to the same question, the one being the number of papers read out in a hearing and the other being the number of papers read out in a stone hut by lamplight to about nine persons; and that no form has a box for the difference between two true numbers, and that Mrs Ammid has set this line down herself, in her own hand, on a numbered sheet with the word copy at the top of it, because there is nowhere else in this valley to put it.*

She read that twice. The shop listened to the first half without moving, because a court form with a number and a signature in it is a thing a shop has heard about before. The second half they listened to differently, because it was a woman of fifty-four explaining that she had put a sentence on a piece of paper that was not required of her, and nobody in a shop at Brant had ever heard her do that before in twenty-six years of reading out.

Then she stood there and waited, which she always did, and about nine seconds went past.

Bess Ilk wiped her hand down her apron. Armist Sarg put down a hod. Mrs Halward, who was about fifty, said, "Say the last of it again, Mrs Ammid, the bit about the two numbers."

"There is nothing after it," Perdie said.

"Then that is a thing to think about over a week, and not a Wednesday," Mrs Halward said, and looked at Bess, and Bess looked at the slate, and then at the pot, and put the pot on the hob without a word, which is what a shop does when it has been handed something it cannot hold.

Then Armist Sarg put the question that everybody in the shop had been thinking and that nobody was going to be allowed to go home without asking.

"Does that go in the bundle."

"To the court? No," Perdie said. "The satchel is the valley's record. The bundle to the court is a separate thing, made out of such of the satchel as is published, and the officer who publishes decides, and she is not in this shop and I do not know what she will do with it and neither do you."

"So it goes in the satchel and not in the bundle."

"It goes in the satchel and not in the bundle."

"Then it has done nothing," Armist said. "Mrs Ammid, I am sorry. You have written a thing down and read it out twice and it has gone in a bag."

"It has done one thing," Perdie said, "and it is not the thing anybody in this shop wants, and I would rather have it and not want it than not have it and want it. In about four years a clerk in a counting-house is going to be copying forward a number out of a bundle about this flood, because that is what he is for, and he is going to have two of them in front of him and he is going to take one, and I cannot stop him and neither can you, and neither can Iven Marrow from Bexhill with a guild behind him. What I can do is make sure that the two numbers are in the same bag with the same word at the top of them, and that the woman who says so is the woman who read them out, and that a sheet with the word copy at the top of it is a thing that exists in four buildings and cannot be wished away by anybody in a hurry. That is all. It is a small thing. It is the only thing anybody in this valley has ever had for putting a true number next to a different true number, and I have been doing it since the sixteenth of last month and I am not going to stop."

Wick Venn had been standing by the door with his coat on, because he had the noon plank and had come in on his way.

"Mrs Ammid," he said. "The thirty-first. You read three."

"I read three."

"And there has been a form at Vellum with one in it since Tuesday, and the boy held the lamp, and I held the book, and we three are the three the form calls witnesses, and Mrs Ammid read three."

"Mr Venn," Perdie said, "I have said that once this morning and I am not going to say it again, and the reason I am not going to say it again is not modesty. It is that the next time I say it I will be saying it to one man instead of eleven, and I have not decided yet whether I will be able to do that, and I would rather not be deciding things in a shop at eleven in the morning with a pot on."

Wick looked at the floor for a moment. "That is fair," he said. "I will not ask you again." And then, to nobody: "I would like it on the slate that I did not ask."

The boy was in the shop. He had come in after the noon trial with the slate under his arm, and he had stood at the back through the whole reading, and when it finished he came up the room and put the slate on the counter and said:

"Mr Rusk. You have to answer one thing and then I will go and fetch the pails."

"Ask it."

"You wrote one," the boy said. "And Mrs Ammid has just read out three, and she says both are true, and Mrs Ammid has never read out a false thing in my hearing. So one of the two is a number of papers in a hearing, and three is a number of papers in a hut, and that is exactly what she said and she said it twice, and I have understood it." He looked up. "Did you want to write three?"

And the shop, which had been listening, went entirely quiet in the way a room of about eleven people goes quiet when a boy of fifteen has asked a man a question that four grown people have been circling for ten minutes.

"Yes," Tarin said.

The boy nodded once, and picked his slate up, and went to fetch the pails.

And that was the end of it, and nobody said anything else about the number that day, and Tarin Rusk stood in a shop at Brant on the Wednesday morning, the eleventh of next month, and had said one word, out loud, in front of nine people, and had thereby taken the only thing he had done on Tuesday — a true answer in a fair hand to a question that did not ask it — and put it beyond himself, and there was no hand on a wall in this basin that could put it back.

Nobody in the shop said *thank you*. Nobody said *you are a fool* either. Armist Sarg picked his hod up and went out. Mrs Halward put her shawl on and said, to nobody, "I have been in this valley forty-one years and the thing I have never once seen is a man say out loud that a number of his was the smaller number."

---

Two other things happened that day and neither of them was about the number.

The requisition came up the road in the boy's mouth before noon, out of Monday's bundle, where a fair copy of it had arrived under a sheet about the water, and it was the only answer anybody had got for the six days since the fifth.

"The clerk of the writs, Low Ward, Vellum. Requisition entered the fifth of next month by O. Fenn, minute clerk, at the instance of M. Quill. Answered at the address of this office." Wick read it out with the flatness of a man reading a card. "Six days, and no answer, and Fenn said in my hearing on the Thursday that a polite requisition is answered politely in about six weeks, and that the answer will be true, and that it will name a place."

"What place," Armist said, coming back in for his coat.

"He did not say. He said the answer will name a place and that it is not this building."

Perdie wrote the six days on the sheet and did not editorialize about it, and said only that a polite requisition and a rude one go into two different piles in a building sixty-one miles east, and that she did not know which pile this one was in, and that whoever had entered it had read it back in his own hand in front of three people, and that was a thing about her office she would carry herself for as long as she was in it.

That evening her chest had a letter in it, and it had been three lines since the sixteenth, and on the evening of the eleventh she made it four, and then she read all four and did not send it.

*Mrs Ammid. I am sorry that you have had to* — that was where it had always stopped, three times, and it was where it stopped now, and underneath it on the fourth line, later than the rest, in her own hand: *I have read out what I read out, and I will do it again, and I will not be asked to say it softly.* And then the date, and nothing else, and no *Yours*, and no address, because she had never once in four letters in thirty-one years got as far as both of those.

She put the lid down without the letter in it, and then put it in, and shut the lid, and sat with her hands flat on it.

**The fourth line was not for him.** It was a sentence with a counting in it, and he would read it as a figure, and there is no figure in it, and the only true thing in it is that she will not be asked to say it softly — and that is hers, and she has said it in a shop in front of nine people, and there is not one reason on earth why she should say it to a man four days away in Bexhill when she has nine people in a shop. She will not send it. It is not finished and it is not going to be finished this week, and she told Bess Ilk on the second of next month that a woman who has written to a man four times in thirty-one years does not learn a new letter in sixteen days, and that she would give the same answer in a month in the same words, and that is what a fortnight is for.

The butt at Nether Sallow took an hour and seventeen minutes. Eighty-first day. The boy counted out loud to forty without being asked and Nesta marked the shutter *full, Wednesday the eleventh of next month, eighty-first day*, and the frame on the culvert post went past empty and would go past empty tomorrow, and would go past empty on Thursday, and the only notice this business has is nailed to a bar four miles off.

The tank stood at two feet six and an eighth, the same as the twenty-ninth of the month before last and the same as every reading since. The boy had read it himself at eight that morning with the time on it, and Bess had written it on the shop slate under nothing else, and Wick had looked at it and said that a figure which has not moved in six days is not a figure and is not a rate, that the card is not read again until the last Saturday in the month, and that the tank has never had a rate in it in thirty years and would not get one out of him.

Tomorrow the district went into the district book, and a clerk was coming over Cinder Ford with a stiff cover to read a rate out at six doors, and a woman who had asked for one thing in nineteen years was going to hear it in daylight at her own doorstep.
