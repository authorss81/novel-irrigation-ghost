# Chapter 0107 — The Seal and the Wave

On the Friday morning, the thirtieth of this month, the Fair Copy sealed the second and third doors at Cinder Ford far bank, and the sealing put a wave down the long drain that nobody had planned, because a magistrate who closes a gate is closing the head of a channel and not the water.

It was done properly. Dorrin came over the ford at ten o'clock with his clerks and a carpenter and two bars and two printed notices, and Sella Marr and Jess stood in the lane with the boy and watched, because there was nothing else to do with a procedure except watch it and make it say names right. The carpenter barred the two lower doors with timber across the frames, nailed the notices to the bars — *SEALED against habitation until the bank is made good, by order of the Fair Copy, Thursday the twenty-ninth, returnable Monday the second of next month* — and Dorrin read both out, because a thing nailed up that is not read out is a thing nailed up.

"It is Friday," Dorrin said, when he had finished. "The thirtieth. The seal stands. The occupants are removed to the upper doors. The office pays for bread and cartage. The bank will be viewed tomorrow."

Sella stood in water to the ankle in front of her own barred door with her hens on the step of the fourth, where a neighbour had taken them. "Nineteen years," she said. "Write that on it too."

"It is on the order," Dorrin said. "S. Marr, third door, resident nineteen years. That is in the bundle."

They moved the beds and the kettle and the broom and the chair with the cushion up the lane to the fourth and fifth doors, wading back and forth with bundles held high, and the boy slept through it on Jess's hip. By noon the two lower doors stood barred with water between them, and the lane looked like a mouth with two teeth out.

Jess sat on the steps of the fourth door that afternoon with the boy asleep across her knees, watching the barred second door where she had slept every night of her twenty years except the last two, and Sella sat beside her with the hens in a crate, and neither of them spoke for a long time, because there are removals that have no words and this was one: two bundles, a kettle, a boy, and a seal with her mother's name on it.

"He will lift it Monday," Jess said at last. "The magistrate said. Persons accounted for. The court."

"Monday is the court," Sella said. "Friday is today, the thirtieth. Tonight we sleep eleven in three doors, and the boy teething, and the lane up half an inch from the gap." She put her hand on her daughter's knee. "I was married in the third door. You were born in it. Your boy took his first steps holding that doorframe. Paper says sealed. Wood says barred. The doorframe is still ours. Remember that order of things, whatever the court says Monday."

"I remember," Jess said, crying quietly so as not to wake the boy. "Nineteen years nobody. Now sealed with my mother's name spelled right. Is that better."

"It is written," Sella said. "Dry nobody is unwritten. Wet sealed is written. Written can be argued with. That is the whole of the difference, and it cost two doors to learn it."

And then the carpenter, finishing the second bar, dropped his hammer off the doorstep into the lane water, and went to pick it up, and put his foot through the bank at the edge of the lane where the drain seep had softened it, and the bank gave way in a piece about a yard wide, and the lane water and the drain water met through the gap with a rush, and the wave went both ways: into the lane, raising it half an inch in a minute round the doors, and down the drain toward the Sere, brown and fast, carrying straw and a hen-coop and the carpenter's hammer with it.

Nobody shouted. Sella grabbed Jess and the boy and got them up the steps of the fourth door. Corrie caught the carpenter by the belt and hauled him out of the gap. Wick, who had come over with bread, waded to the gap with his coat off and put it into the hole with his good hand, and Dorrin waded in beside him with his own coat, and the two clerks, and for about ten minutes five men stood in cold water to the knee holding coats into a bank while the drain ran past them.

"It is holding," Wick said at last. The coats had silted into the gap and the rush had slowed. "But the bank is soft the whole length. This is what the hand said Monday and nobody acted on. The bank is soft behind the row, Friday the thirtieth, noon, witnessed by a magistrate with his own coat in it."

Dorrin stood in the drain water with his trousers soaked to the thigh, holding a sodden coat, and looked at the barred doors with water halfway up the bars, and at the gap with two coats in it, and at the wave going down the drain carrying straw toward the Sere.

"A seal to protect a boundary," he said quietly. "And the boundary gives way under the seal." He looked at Wick. "Your room said Tuesday morning. The hand said Wednesday. It is Friday the thirtieth and the bank is in the drain. What does the hand say now."

Wick wrung out his sleeve with his one hand. He was thirty-eight, with a column in a book and a refusal in it about a day a man gives on purpose, and he stood in drain water to the knee holding a sodden coat that had been his own, and he answered the way he answered everything: with what he could check.

"The hand is not mine," he said. "Mine says the gap is a yard wide and the coats are silted and the rush has slowed, and that is on no sheet either, but you can see it." He looked at the barred doors with water halfway up the bars. "Magistrate. Thursday you said what is recognised is real. Here is what is real Friday the thirtieth: two doors barred with water in them, a bank with two coats in it, eleven people in three doors, and a drain carrying a hammer to the Sere. Recognise that, and the recognising is worth something."

Dorrin stood in the water a moment longer, soaked to the thigh, a magistrate of the Fair Copy with two clerks holding a plane table on ground that was no longer there. Then he took out his notebook, wet at the edges, and wrote in it standing in the drain: *Friday the thirtieth, noon. Bank gave way one yard at lane end under seal. Lane rose half inch in a minute. Coats of W. Venn and self holding. Wave down drain. No persons harmed. To be laid before court Monday with persons.*

"That is recognised," he said, closing the book. "A seal, a gap, two coats, a hammer gone. Monday the court gets that with the persons." He looked at the barred doors. "And the seal stands, because water does not wait for a signature, and Saturday evening the lower doors are empty whether the lane likes it or not. That is procedure. The rest — bread, coats, holding — that is people, and people are not procedure, and I am learning the difference at fifty with wet feet."

Wick wrung out his sleeve with his one hand. "The hand is not mine. Ask him. He fills a butt every evening at six and he is there now, doing forty trips. That is where he is."

---

Tarin was at the butt when Wick came back over the ford at four o'clock, twenty trips in, with the pail and the twine and the watch in his coat. Wick told him about the bars and the notices and the gap and the coats and the wave, standing at the wall, and Tarin listened without stopping, tipping each pail in as it came.

"Saturday evening they are to be out," Wick finished. "Monday the court sits. The bank is soft the whole length. The lane rose half an inch in a minute."

Tarin put the pail down. "It is Friday the thirtieth. The lane rose because the gap let the drain in. The drain is falling above. It will be dry underfoot—" He stopped himself the way a man stops at the edge of a plank. "No. Listen. I am going to say it right this time. Wick, go back over tomorrow morning with your stick and measure the middle against the third doorstep. If it is lower than your Thursday mark by the width of your thumb, the drain is falling and the lane follows by Sunday. That is a stick and a step and anybody can check it. Do not write down what my hand says. Write down what your stick says."

"And tonight," Wick said. "The boy is in the fourth door with eleven people and two butts and one of them empty. The lane is up half an inch."

"Tonight I finish this butt," Tarin said. "Forty trips. Then I carry what I can over the ford in the dark. Not as a man who knows. As a man with a pail." He picked the pail up. "A pail is not a procedure. A magistrate cannot seal a pail. Water in a pail is not water in a lane, and eight pails in the dark is eight pails, and that is anybody's arithmetic."

Wick looked at him. "Monday the room said no man here can carry water without putting a hand in it. That has not changed."

"Then I put my hand in it," Tarin said. "A day a man gives on purpose is a day he still owns. You said that about yourself and refused to write it. I am saying it about an evening. Forty trips for this lane and forty for that one. That is two evenings in one and nobody's sheet."

He finished the butt at half past seven, forty trips, with the boy counting to forty and Nesta marking the shutter *full, Friday the thirtieth*. Then he took a second pail from the shed, and the twine off his own pail's handle, and wound it round the second pail's handle twice, the way a man does with a length of twine he has carried through a count and a window and a chain and a card and a declaration, and went down the lane to the ford in the dark with Wick, carrying two empty pails and a full can between them.

The ford was low and the night was clear. On the far bank the lane water lay still and brown between the doors, with the two barred doors standing in it like a mouth with teeth out, and lamplight in the fourth where eleven people were crowded with a boy nine months old.

Tarin filled both pails at the upper butt, which was clean rain water, and carried them to the fourth door without a word, and Sella took them at the step with her skirts tied and her feet bare, nineteen years in the third door and one night in the fourth.

"Who sends this," she said.

"A man who fills a butt every evening," Tarin said. "Forty trips. This is forty-one and forty-two. There is no sheet."

He went back for more, and Wick went with him, and between them they carried eight pails of clean water over the ford in the dark on Friday the thirtieth, while the drain ran past with the carpenter's hammer in it toward the Sere, and no magistrate sealed any of it, because a pail is not a boundary and water in a pail is not water in a lane. The ford stones were slick and moonless dark, and twice Wick stumbled with his one hand full and Tarin steadied him without a word, and twice Tarin slipped and Wick caught the pail before it spilled. At the fourth trip Jess met them at the ford end with the lamp to light the stones, though the boy slept inside. At the sixth Corrie came down in waders over nightclothes to take the pails at the lane end. "Friday the thirtieth," he whispered, taking a pail in each hand. "Six doors. Eight pails. No sheet."

At midnight they stopped. The upper butt was low. Sella stood at the step with a lamp and said, "Saturday evening we are to be out. Monday the court. Tell your valley that bread comes in the dark and paper comes in daylight, and both are something."

Tarin picked up the empty pails. "Sunday morning Wick comes with a stick," he said. "Measure the middle against your step. If it is lower by a thumb, the drain is falling. That is anybody's figure."

Sella held the lamp higher so it showed both their faces, his muddy to the elbows, hers lined in lamplight. "Mr Rusk. You are the man on the coping. Thursday night, four hours and six minutes. My daughter's husband read your board in Cresset. Three and three-quarter gallons a property. He read it to her in a letter."

"I cannot measure what it costs nine hundred and forty properties," Tarin said. "I said so on the board. That is the whole of what I know about cities."

"Then hear what I know about lanes," Sella said. "Six doors stood dry nineteen years and nobody came. Six doors stand wet nine days and bread comes in the dark with the man from the coping carrying it himself. Monday eleven people in a room would not act on your hand. Friday night you carried eight pails in the dark and nobody sealed any of it." She shifted the lamp. "A hand on a wall is nothing anybody can check. Eight pails in the dark is eight pails. Remember that order of things, whatever any room says. Paper sends magistrates. Pails send nothing and carry water."

"I will remember," Tarin said. "Friday the thirtieth. Eight pails. No sheet."

"Good." She turned with the lamp. "Saturday evening we are to be out of two doors. Come Sunday and see dry ground, if the stick says so. That is anybody's figure, and mine says the edges are falling already."

He went back over the ford in the dark with Wick, with the twine on the pail handle, on Friday the thirtieth, and the seal stood on two doors behind him, and the wave he had not made went down the drain ahead of him, carrying straw to a river that had never heard of any of it. Behind him Sella barred nothing, unbarred nothing, and carried the lamp into the fourth door where eleven people slept, and the night settled over six doors with water between them going down a thumb at a time.
