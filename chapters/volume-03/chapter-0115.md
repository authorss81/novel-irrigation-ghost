# Chapter 0115 — Six in the Morning

He filled the butt in the dark, on the Saturday morning, the seventh of next month, because the bank was to be worked at six and six was the hour the butt was filled at, and both of those facts had been true for a week and had never until that morning been in the same sentence.

He went down at four. The water at the notch was black and colder than the air and the pail was Nesta's and the roll of tarred twine was wound twice on the handle where it had been since a Tuesday in the month before. He put a lamp on the coping by the notch because a butt is two hundred and forty gallons of room and you cannot see the rim of a butt at four in the morning, and he had gone down that bank forty times a night for a fortnight and could do it with his eyes shut, and could not do it with his eyes open and no light.

The plank ran at a hundred and ninety, same as every day, never divided, and the cold made his right shoulder set within about twenty minutes, and at the thirtieth trip he put his left hand down on the wet stone of the coping to get himself up the last foot of the bank and something in the shoulder went over to a place it had not been before, and he finished the last ten trips with it, one arm, and the butt was full at about three hours and a quarter instead of an hour and sixteen minutes, and it was twenty past seven in the morning and a hundred yards above a ford.

He had washed his face in the cold water at the notch. That was the whole of his preparation. He put his coat on and went down the lane and over the ford, and six people had been working the bank for an hour and forty minutes by the time he came round the willows.

---

They were working the lane end properly, which is to say to a line, with a bond and a joint, the way a man in a shed with a contract would have done it, and Cobb was there with a string and a book and a mallet and a cold chisel, and the joint was being laid in lime by Mrs Trew, who was sixty-nine and had laid a joint in a kitchen floor forty years ago and had told nobody.

Sella saw him first.

"You are late," she said.

"I am an hour and forty minutes late."

"You are an hour and forty minutes late," Sella agreed, "and I want to say the next thing once, at the start, and then not again all morning, and I am saying it to you and not to him, because he has been here since five with a hod and he cannot help you." She did not raise her voice; the lane was small. "Six o'clock is a butt. I have known that for a week. Every evening since the twenty-fourth of the month before last, at about six, there is a man on that bank with a pail, and if he is not there the nineteen houses drink out of a butt that was full yesterday. That is not a figure. That is a habit, and I keep a habit the way I keep a plank, and a habit is a harder thing to work round than a schedule. You filled your butt in the dark this morning so that you could be useful here at seven, and you were not useful here at six, and you were not much use to your own butt at four, and the row got a bank and a butt and a man who was in the wrong place at both ends of it, and that is what happened, and I am not saying it twice."

"Then I am sorry," Tarin said.

"Do not be sorry to me. Be useful to somebody." She turned back to the stone. "There is a hod by the third coping and it wants carrying to the line and Cobb has written that eleven o'clock is the hour the joint wants doing and it is nine. Take the hod."

He took the hod. It went about four times in the next two hours, and his shoulder would not take a hod at all after the first, so he carried it hooked in the crook of his elbow against his chest, and Cobb wrote in his book that the coping at the lane end was carried by four persons of the row, and by the man of Nether Sallow, and by one man of the valley whose name was not on the notice, and Tarin did not correct him, and nobody did.

They laid stone to the line from about ten until one, in courses, with a bond, and Wick Venn said nothing at all for two hours except *that one, that one, hold that one*, and the lime was mixed by Jess on a board with a bucket, and the boy was not there, because it was a working morning and a boy of fifteen with a slate has no business on a bank at six in the morning and Nesta Sarg had told him so on the Friday and he had obeyed her, which is a thing that ought to be worth something.

At about half past ten Cobb came down the line with his book shut under his arm and asked him where the foot of the bank wanted to go, and Tarin put both hands into the mud and said: a yard and a half along and down, and not all at once, and the second course in first, and he said it the way he said figures.

"Wick," Cobb said. "Take a bar and tell me if he is right."

Wick went along the foot with a bar and a length of chalk and came back and said, "A yard and a half along and down, and not all at once. It wants the second course in first, and it wants to know that before we put a stone on it that will not come up again."

"Then we do it that way," Cobb said, and wrote in the day-book line: *course set by T. Rusk, of no district, of no office, of Nether Sallow by residence, and checked with a bar by Mr W. Venn of Nether Sallow, the check being the authority and not the saying*, and Tarin read it over his shoulder and did not ask for a word changed, which was the first time in fourteen years he had said a thing about earth to a stranger and had a man with a bar agree with it in front of six people, and which was worth exactly what the bar was worth and not one trip more.

At about twenty past one Cobb cut the coping stone. It took him a while, because he was doing it properly, with a cold chisel and one light blow at a time, and the stone is hard and the letters are small, and he did not have a mason and would not pretend to.

He cut *VII · VII · MDCCC* — no. He stopped, and asked Sella's name, and then struck his own hand off the stone and said, "No. It is not a name."

"What is it, then," Sella said.

"It is a date and a district." He cut it in a line, small and square, and he showed them the letters, and it said *7th day of next month · far-bank row · district entered*, and the last three words were cramped and he had to reset the chisel twice to do them. "That is what it is for. A making-good carries a date and the district it stands in. That is the schedule and I am not going to improve on it."

Sella looked at it for a while.

"Nineteen years," she said, "and there is a date cut in a stone at the end of my lane and not one of my names on it. I want it said that I said I am glad of that. I have been on a sealing order and in a certificate and in a bundle and I am glad of a stone. And I want it said that I said that, and I am saying it to Cobb, and Cobb, put it in your book in those words, because in about sixty years a man will stand here and read a date and a district and he will know there were six doors, and he will not know my name, and that is what a date is for, and it is better than nothing, and I have had nothing for nineteen years and I know the difference."

"It goes in the book," Cobb said, and put it in the book, word for word, and read it back to her, and she said it was right, and then Cobb said, to nobody in particular, in the voice of a man finishing a piece of work and being honest about it because nobody else in the lane was going to be:

"I will say the other thing, since six people are standing here and four of them are the ones who paid for it in a morning. I have cut a district into a stone. That is me. And a district can be charged, and this one will be, and the sum will be on a form on Monday and the form will name this row and the six doors in it, and I cut the words that let it be named. I have done it knowing. I would like it in the book that I knew."

"Then you are the magistrate's clerk and you did the clerk's work," Sella said, "and I have been on the wrong end of a clerk's work twice this week and one of the times it was correct. I am not going to make it easier for you by pretending otherwise and I am not going to make it harder. Cut your stone."

Cobb cut his stone. And that was the end of it.

The bank came up to the line with a bond in it and a joint in it and a date in the coping, and Cobb struck the date off his list, and the joint wanted lime to cure for a day and a night and would be walked on Friday, and the work was finished and finished well, and it had taken most of a morning of six people's time for nine loads of stone off the ford bank and a cart of clay and a bushel of lime, and the office was going to charge the district for the lot of it.

"There," Cobb said, packing his chisel. "That is a making-good. It will be here in twenty years and your grandchildren will not have to think about it. Mr Venn, I will want you and the four others to sign the day-book line at the ford end, and Mr Rusk, I will want your hand on the sketch of the bond, because you are the only man here who has seen one before and I would rather have it from you than from me."

Tarin looked at the sketch for a while and then wrote under it, in the space at the bottom for a person who is not on the notice: *Bond as laid, three courses, broken joint at every third. Stone to the line. I have not laid a bond in nine years. — T. Rusk, of no district, of no office, of Nether Sallow by residence, at the plank.* And Cobb read it and did not cross anything out, and Tarin understood at the time that a man who can be wrong in writing is a man somebody can check, and had understood since the second of next month, and that this was the one thing his hand was for.

They came off the bank at about three, and he carried the hod back to the third coping with his arm against his chest, and Sella let him and did not help, and Corrie Lille watched him do it and said nothing at all, which was the last thing Corrie Lille had to say on the subject of hands, and had said on the sixth of the month before last, and on the twenty-seventh, and on the first of next month, and would not say again this season.

---

The butt at Nether Sallow was not full at four hundred yards from a bank.

He came up the lane at about ten past four and the butt was at the notch and the boy was not there, and Mrs Halward's nephew and two men of the upper lane were on the bank with Nesta Sarg, and they had been there since half past three, and they had a lamp on the coping and a second pail and a rope, and the boy was counting out loud at the top of the butt where he could see the rim, standing on the low wall with his slate under his arm.

"Twenty-two," the boy said.

Tarin stood at the bottom of the bank and did not go up it. It was the seventy-seventh day and the shutter was not going to say so, because the shutter was going to say what actually happened, which was *full, Saturday the seventh of next month, by four persons of this lane, at the request of N. Sarg*, and that was the truest thing anybody had put on it in a month.

"Twenty-three," the boy said.

Nesta did not look round. She kept filling and tipping, and the two men carried, and the nephew filled, and at about the twenty-ninth trip she said, without any particular weight in it, "Mr Rusk. Do not stand at the bottom of my bank in the dark and let four people do your evening. If you want to carry, get up here and carry. If you cannot carry, then go home and put the kettle on, which is a thing your hands can do."

"I cannot carry," Tarin said.

"No," she said. "You cannot. You have one shoulder and you filled a butt at four o'clock this morning in the dark, which I know because your lantern was on the coping at half past three when I came up to see whether the water was low, and I have kept a plank for thirty-two years and I know what a lantern at that hour means." She tipped a pail. "Thirty. Go home. I will mark the shutter and I will mark it true, which is that it was filled at six on the seventh of next month, at the request of N. Sarg, by four persons of this lane. That is what happened and it will say what happened."

He went home. He lay on the bed at the cottage at Brant with his coat on and the folding rule on the table and the watch in his pocket, and he could not have told anybody how long he lay there, and the last thing he was aware of was the sound of the shutter being marked with chalk from about a quarter past six, and a boy's voice from the lane counting out loud and not stopping, and the number going up, and the light going off the top of the hill.

On the Monday morning the boy told the shop at Brant that the drain was at its lowest on the Thursday before, and that Cobb's notice had said six o'clock, and that the making-good had been laid in the right hour, and that a man who fills a butt at six o'clock every evening of his life cannot be at a bank at six in the morning, and that this had nothing to do with the bank at all and everything to do with the lowest water.

"That is the whole of why it worked," the boy said, to about nine people in a shop, from behind a slate. "Nobody chose that hour. A clerk wrote a notice and a clerk chose six o'clock because the drain is lowest before the day's draw, and the man who is certain to be somewhere else at six o'clock in the morning is the man who fills this butt at six o'clock in the evening of every day of his life in this valley. And it worked, and the bank is laid to a line, and I do not think anybody in Vellum knows that."

Bess Ilk wrote it on the slate, and it stood two days, and it came off the slate in the ordinary way that things come off slates, and the only copy of it anywhere was the inside of a boy's head.
