# Chapter 0101 — The Butt at Six

He could not leave, and on the Saturday, the twenty-fourth of this month, he said so to a boy of fifteen before he had lifted the pail.

"It is Saturday," Tarin Rusk said, standing at the top of the lane at Nether Sallow with the six-gallon pail in his hand. "And there is a butt to fill at about six o'clock, and I am on my sixty-third day in this valley, and a man who is on his sixty-third day does not go anywhere. Write that down if you want. Do not put it in a book."

The boy wrote nothing. He had a slate under his arm and he did not take it out, because the slate was his own and the butt was not a rod on any card, and what a man did at six o'clock in a field of turned earth was not a thing anybody had asked him to copy.

The butt stood behind the low wall where it had stood since the spring, with its foot in the hole the notch had been dug into and the crack in its side cemented over. The pail was Nesta Sarg's pail, with the mark inside it and the split in the handle mended with wire and pitch, and it was not lent. The roll of tarred twine was on the handle, wound twice and tucked under, and it had come off a stake at a culvert on the Tuesday evening of the second of this month by six hands in daylight, and it had been on the handle since the Saturday morning after that, and it had been on it through a count, a window, a chain, a card and a declaration.

Tarin put the pail under the notch and let it fill and carried it up the bank to the butt and tipped it in, and then he went back down for another. Six gallons at a time. Forty trips. About an hour and sixteen minutes if the plank ran the way it had run all week, at a hundred and ninety gallons an hour, and it ran that way because Nesta Sarg had done the plank at four that afternoon and written a hundred and ninety on her own shutter and had not divided it by anything.

"How many is that," the boy said after a while, counting under his breath.

"Nine," Tarin said. "Thirty-one to go, and then it is full, and then it is morning, and then it is filled again. Nineteen houses in this lane and forty-three people in them and nineteen butts is one butt each, and nineteen butts is not enough, and a butt is a day and a half of drinking for three people. That is the whole of the arithmetic and there is no other figure for it in this basin."

"I know," the boy said. "She told me on Sunday."

They worked without talking for six more trips. The light was going off the top of the hill behind the lane the way it had gone off it the evening before, and the turned earth smelled of the morning's watering and of the beans standing about nine inches deep in the trench two feet wide at the bottom of the field. Eleven acres of them. A man of fifty-eight had put the seed in on the twenty-second of the month before this one, and the ground had gone like a bench on the twenty-fourth, and on Thursday night three thousand four hundred and eighty-five gallons had gone down a flume two miles below the plank and had stood nine inches deep in a hundred yards of trench and gone nowhere, and that was the whole of what a window was.

At the sixteenth trip Tarin stopped at the notch with the pail half full and looked at his left hand. The watch was in it and he had not used it, because a plank is a plank and a man does not need a watch for that. He put the watch in his coat and went on.

"You are counting," the boy said.

"I am counting trips," Tarin said. "Forty trips is a butt. A butt is an evening. An evening is a day I do not go anywhere. That is not a measurement. That is a job."

He set the pail under the notch and straightened with a hand on his back, and the boy said, "She did the plank at four this afternoon. I was there. A hundred and ninety, two trials, one minute fifty-two and one minute fifty-three. She wrote both on the shutter and did not divide them by anything."

"That is her plank," Tarin said. "Thirty-two years of it. The fifth rod is promised in her own words and not cut, and she will cut it in her own hand when there is a sheet in front of her instead of a shutter. Until then the shutter is the record, and a shutter anybody can come and read."

"Mr Rusk," the boy said, following him down the bank. "Thursday night you said four hours and six minutes is not a thing one man does. Is a butt a thing one man does."

Tarin tipped the pail into the butt. Seventeen. The butt swallowed six gallons without a sound.

"A butt is forty trips and an hour and sixteen minutes and a man with nothing else to do at six o'clock," he said. "It is not a window and not a service and nobody has a schedule for it and there is no figure for it anywhere in this basin. The card at the culvert has four rods and no rod for a butt in a field, and there is going to be a fifth rod and it is not this." He watched the water come up out of the notch, the way it had come up six years under a notice that said there was no water. "Nineteen butts is one each and nineteen is not enough. A man is going to do that every evening now until the ground gives a crop or the winter does."

"And your name," the boy said. "There is a day when somebody has to say it."

"There is," Tarin said. "Tonight nobody has to. Tonight there is a pail and twine and forty trips. Count them and do not put them in a book for about a year."

They went on, eighteen, nineteen, twenty, twenty-one, the water steady, the light thinning, his shoulder aching the way it ached every evening now, his hand remembering the tank wall with every lift.

"You are on the roster," the boy said, after a while. "Sef Ordry wrote me on it in a day-book in a shed, and you are on it too, and it says *at the tank, on the coping, with a level staff*, and nobody has ever sent for it."

"I know what it says."

"And five shillings a day, six days, a cottage at no rent, and fourteen weeks that end on the sixth of the month after next. I have written that twice and got it the same both times, and it is the only date anybody in this valley has given me to be certain about."

The boy wrote the day count on the slate and read it out, because a slate that is not read out is a slate, and then he put the slate away and took it out again and looked at what he had written. "Sixty-three," he said. "And nowhere on any of it says a man may stop."

"That is the first true thing anybody has written on that slate," Tarin said, and went down for the twenty-second.

---

At the twenty-second trip a woman came up the lane from the bottom with a basket of greens on her arm and stopped at the wall and watched him tip the pail in. She was about fifty, with grey hair tied back and mud on her boots to the ankle, and she had been at the plank on the Sunday when a woman of forty-one had said there was going to be a fifth rod on the card.

"Mr Rusk," she said.

"Mrs Halward," Tarin said, without stopping. He knew her house. Third from the bottom. Two children grown and gone to Cresset, a husband dead three years, a butt of her own that her nephew filled on Tuesdays.

"There is water at Cinder Ford," she said.

Tarin put the pail down on the wall. "Where."

"On the far bank. Below the outfall." She set the basket down. "My sister's girl is married over there, at the row past the ford, and she came across this morning with the baby and said the yard is under water. Not the field. The yard. Between the doors."

The boy looked up. "Which doors."

"The far-bank row," Mrs Halward said. "Past Cinder Ford, where the long drain meets the Sere. You go over the ford and there is a lane and a pump and six doors. She said the water is standing in the lane to the ankle."

Tarin picked the pail up again. "The outfall is two miles below the plank," he said. "The flume ran Thursday night for four hours and six minutes and ran on for three hours after the gate came down. That was Friday morning. This is Saturday evening."

"I know what day it is," Mrs Halward said. "It is Saturday the twenty-fourth. She came this morning and the water was there this morning. She said the pump stands in it. She said her mother has lived in the third door nineteen years and was never in a return, and she said, tell them, because water does not copy. Water stands where it stands."

"Did she say where it came from."

"She said it came up out of the drain in the night. She said the drain is full to the bank and the bank is soft, and the ground at the mouth of the flume is low." Mrs Halward picked up her basket. "She said her mother has lived in that row nineteen years and has never once been asked for a name for anything."

Tarin carried the pail up the bank and tipped it into the butt. Twenty-three.

"Tell her to keep the baby out of it," he said.

"I told her," Mrs Halward said, and went on up the lane.

The boy waited until she was out of hearing. "The nineteen houses are four miles two chains from the head of the drain," he said quietly. "And the flume's outfall is two miles below the plank. And the flume's water never comes back to the plank. You said the whole of that on the coping on Thursday night, in front of nine men, and Wick Venn wrote it on the back of his hand."

"It has," Tarin said.

"Then where is Cinder Ford."

"Two miles below the plank, where the flume's outfall is on the drain." Tarin went back down for the next pail. "The ford is over the Sere from us and the row is past it. I have never walked it."

"And who lives there."

"Nobody, according to somebody." Tarin set the pail under the notch. "Go and ask Nesta Sarg whether she did the plank at four. And do not run."

The boy went. Tarin filled the pail and carried it up alone, twenty-four, twenty-five, twenty-six, counting without the watch, and the light went further off the hill.

---

Nesta Sarg came down herself at half past six, with the boy behind her and a shawl over her shoulders though the evening was warm. She was forty-one, with thirty-two years of a plank and a pail in her hands, and she stood at the wall and watched him work the way a woman watches a man doing her own job badly enough to be worth correcting and well enough to be left alone.

"It is Saturday," she said. "The twenty-fourth."

"It is," Tarin said.

"I did the plank at four and it is a hundred and ninety. It is on the shutter." She looked at the butt. "How many."

"Twenty-eight."

"Then sit down for a minute and I will do two." She took the pail out of his hand before he could answer and went down to the notch herself, filled it, came up the bank without hurry, and tipped it in. The boy counted under his breath and she corrected him without looking. "That is twenty-nine. Count out loud or do not count."

"Twenty-nine," the boy said.

She went down for the thirtieth. When she came back she stood with the empty pail in her hand and looked at the field and the trench and the beans standing in it.

"Mrs Halward's niece came across this morning," Tarin said.

"I know," Nesta said. "Her sister's girl. Married at Cinder Ford, past the ford, far bank. Six doors and a pump."

"You have been there."

"I have been over the ford twice in thirty-two years. Once for a wedding and once because a pump washer fits both pumps and hers was the only spare." Nesta put the pail down. "The ground at the mouth of the flume is four feet below the field the water has been standing in for six years. Everybody in this lane knows that who has ever walked the drain. The drain is the sole discharge of this Reach. That is item one of the schedule, printed in 1902, and it stands as printed."

"Who lives in the six doors."

"People live in them." She said it flatly, the way she said figures. "A woman called Sella Marr has lived in the third door nineteen years. She keeps hens. There is a girl with a baby. There is an old man who mends nets. I do not know all their names. Nobody has ever come up this lane asking for them."

Tarin looked at her. "The return for that district says no inhabitants."

"I do not know what a return says," Nesta said. "I know there are six doors and a pump and a lane, and this morning there is water in the lane to the top of a boot, and a woman with hens is standing in it. That is what I know, and I did the plank at four and it is a hundred and ninety."

She picked up the pail and went down for the thirty-first trip. The boy watched her go and then looked at Tarin.

"Are you going over there."

"I am filling a butt," Tarin said. "Forty trips. About an hour and sixteen minutes. It is Saturday the twenty-fourth and there is a butt to fill every evening at about six o'clock until the ground gives a crop or the winter does, and my name is in two books in two hands in two places four hundred yards apart, and there is no form in this basin in which a man may say that he is going somewhere he is not going."

He said it harder than he meant to. Nesta came up the bank with the full pail and tipped it in without comment and went back down.

At the thirty-sixth trip the light was nearly gone off the hill. At the fortieth Tarin tipped the last pail in and stood with his hands on the rim of the butt, breathing through his mouth. The butt was full. Two hundred and forty gallons of room, forty trips, and a man's whole evening, and by the light off the hill tomorrow there would be six gallons of it in a pail and the rest of it in nineteen kitchens that had asked for none of it.

"Monday I will do the morning," Nesta said. "You do the evening. That is the arrangement and it is not a schedule and there is no figure for it anywhere in this basin."

"That is the arrangement," Tarin said.

The boy put his slate away without having taken it out. The three of them stood at the wall in the dusk, and below them the plank ran at a hundred and ninety gallons an hour into the dark, and two miles below that a stone launder stood empty and chained, and two miles below that water stood in a lane where, according to a paper, nobody lived.

"I am going over the ford tomorrow," the boy said. "After church. To look at the pump."

"No," Nesta said, at once. "You are going to church and then you are coming to the plank and doing the noon trial with me, because it is Sunday the twenty-fifth and the plank is done at eight and at noon and at four. Somebody else can go and look at a pump."

"Who."

"Somebody who is not fifteen and not answerable for a slate." She picked up her shawl. "Good night, Mr Rusk."

"Good night," Tarin said.

She went up the lane with the boy, and Tarin stayed at the wall with the full butt and the empty pail and the twine on the handle, and the water coming up out of the notch the whole while, the way it had come up for six years under a notice that said there was no water.

He put his hand flat on the low wall. The stone was warm from the day. Somewhere under his palm, two miles and four chains of empty channel ran down to a drain, and he could feel where every foot of it was the way a man finds his own front door in the dark, and there was nothing in it, and there was no way of giving it back.

He took his hand off the wall and picked up the pail and went down to the cottage at Brant where he slept at no rent, on his sixty-third day, and he did not go over the ford. The cottage stood dark with his coat on the hook and the folding rule in it and the watch in the pocket, and he set the pail by the door with the twine on the handle, wound twice and tucked under. Saturday the twenty-fourth. Forty trips. A butt full. Water standing where a paper said nobody, in a lane he had not walked down, on a bank he could feel from his own wall. That was the whole of the day, and it was enough, and it was not finished.
