# Chapter 0118 — One

The witness schedule came back from Vellum on the Monday afternoon on the cart, in the bundle, and on the Tuesday morning, the tenth of next month, the lower Sere warden came up the lane to Nether Sallow with it in a leather case, because a man who gives his evidence in writing has to do it at the place where the holding is, in front of somebody, and the nearest magistrate is sixty-one miles east in a building with tall windows.

Harker set the schedule out on the low wall at the plank and weighted the corners with two stones, and Tarin looked at it and did not touch it.

"You sign, you date it, and I have to be here for it. Then it goes down the road and into the file, and on the nineteenth of next month, if the writing is in, the register reads *evidence in writing, read into the record*, and nobody in that room will ever know it was a man at a wall at Nether Sallow. That is the arrangement and the arrangement is generous, and I would like it done before the noon trial, because I have been on the eight already and I have the noon as well."

"Nesta does the eight," Tarin said. "I can do the noon."

"Then write it at eleven and I will wait."

The boy was there, because the boy was always there, with his slate under his arm, and he read the columns off the wall upside down before anybody thought to tell him not to.

*Name. District. Occupation. Papers read out to the witness in his hearing, by number. Whether the witness attends, or gives evidence in writing at the place of the holding, to be read into the record. Evidence.*

"There is a box for words in," the boy said. "There is no line marked how long."

"There is no line marked how long," Harker agreed. "That is the court's trouble and not mine."

---

They wrote it on a flat board across two upturned pails, in the shade of the wall, and Tarin filled the first three columns the way Bess Ilk had filled them at the shop, and the district column took what it had taken before.

*Name: Tarin Rusk. District: of no district, of no office. Occupation: sluicewright, five shillings a day for six days, at a shed with a level staff. Nether Sallow by residence, at the plank.*

"That is a long occupation," Harker said.

"There is not a short one. Four hours and six minutes of it on the coping on the twenty-second of the month before last at eleven at night, with nine men watching, is in a shed day-book nine inches by six written in from both ends, and if you want a short occupation you want a man who has never done anything."

"Write it long," Harker said. "It is the only column in that form which is not asking you to be less than you are."

The fourth column was the one.

*Papers read out to the witness in his hearing, by number.*

And Tarin Rusk stood at a low wall at Nether Sallow on the Tuesday morning, the tenth of next month, with a flat board on two pails and a boy of fifteen reading upside down, and looked at a box about two inches wide.

*One.* Or *three.*

He had been three witnesses to one reading, in a stone hut below a culvert, on the Saturday the thirty-first of the month before last, by lamplight: Mrs Ammid reading from a numbered sheet, the boy with the lamp, Wick Venn holding the book steady with his good hand while the damp made its own decisions about the middle.

That was the hearing. That was the only reading in this business that anybody had ever called witnesses to, and the schedule itself said so — *the record book of gate orders, lower Sere record house, and the pencil line within it* — and sheet thirty-two was the sheet about the book, and the book was the thing the court wanted, and thirty-two was the number on it in Perdie Ammid's own hand at the top of the sheet under the word *copy*.

So one was true. One was the answer to the question the form had asked, asked properly, and the person asking it was a clerk in Vellum who had a right to ask it and had asked the correct thing.

And three was the number of numbered sheets that had been read out in a stone hut on the thirty-first of the month before last: the shutter figures, and the book, and the act of a man of thirty-three who had signed a closure on a side channel at nineteen with fourteen months in service, which had saved a hospital and had trapped people in a lower courtyard, and which an inquiry had called justified without authority, and which was written out word for word on sheet thirty-three and read back, along with Perdie Ammid's own line saying that the witness had declined to give a schedule of persons and had declined to give a number.

He had said the act out loud to nine people in a hut in the dark. He had said the rest of it in the same breath: that there was a schedule of persons the order closed on, and that he was not giving it over, and that he was not going to say how many names were on it, and that the number was the whole of what he had not said in fourteen years and he was not going to hand it over on a Saturday at a wall.

That was the thirty-first of the month before last. It was ten days ago.

He wrote *one*.

He wrote it in the same hand that wrote the staff readings on a boy's slate, and the bond sketch at a coping, and a refusal on a numbered sheet in front of three witnesses, and he wrote it as cleanly as he wrote anything, and he understood while the pen was moving that this was the part of it he would not be able to put anywhere: a man who writes a concealment badly at least leaves the hesitations in it for somebody to read. He wrote it in a fair hand with no stop in it, and the writing said *one* the way a writing says anything, which is without caring.

"Paper thirty-two," he said. "That is what was read out to me. I heard it, I heard it read in full, and I put my hand on the book before it was closed, and that is the whole of what I have to say to that column."

Harker read it back, and then said the thing that made the rest of the morning possible:

"That is a true answer, and I am going to write *true* in the margin beside it, and the word *true* in my hand is worth more in that file than anything either of us could add to it. Do you understand what has just happened. You have written a smaller number than what happened, and the smaller number is the one the form asked for, and a man in a shed in a village has put his name to it. I have sworn nine men this month and two of them are not back from the county town, so I will tell you the trick of this trade, which is that a man who says *I know nothing of it* is believed and a man who says *I know all of it* is not, and there is no third thing and I have never once found one."

"Then write *true*," Tarin said.

Harker wrote *true* in the margin and initialled it and ruled a line under the answer and put the date and his own name against the line and did not read the number again, because a man who rules a line under a number does not go back and admire it.

The three witnesses were the same three. Wick Venn's schedule had gone to Nether Sallow on Monday and had been filled in on Tuesday morning at the wall, because Wick had come up on the eight and had stood there while it was filled and had said nothing at all, and had put his one good hand flat on the board and had watched Tarin write the same number he had written, and then had said, "You are not going to tell me why, and I am not going to ask you, and I would like it noticed that I did not ask you."

The boy's schedule was different. Fifteen is under age for a witness, and Perdie Ammid had seen to that on the Friday at the shop, in a paragraph, and had sent it on the Monday cart, and the boy's column said *a minor, and no evidence required of him, and the court has excused him on the ground of age and does not ask him to answer.* He read that upside down twice and said nothing about it, which for a boy of fifteen was restraint of a very high order.

The last column was words.

"Make it a thing you would want read into a record in a room with a door shut," Harker said, "and make it short, because it will be read out by a man who is in a hurry and who is not interested in you."

Tarin put the pen down and thought about it for the length of about nine seconds, and then he wrote four sentences.

> *I was present at the record house below the culvert at Nether Sallow on the thirty-first day of the month before last, at about seven in the evening, by the light of one lamp held by a boy. Mrs P. Ammid read one sheet aloud from a numbered sheet, being sheet thirty-two, and it was read in full and not summarised. I was shown the book and I put my left hand flat upon the upper board before it was closed. I have no knowledge of that book beyond what was read to me and shown to me on that evening, and I have had none since, and I would say so in a room as readily as on this wall.*

"That is good," Harker said, and meant it. "Four sentences, every one of them true, and not one word in it that a man could pull apart. Sign it."

He signed it, and dated it, and put the place under the date, and beside his signature a line in his own hand: *written at the place of the holding, in the presence of the warden of the lower Sere and of P. Ammid, at the plank, Nether Sallow, on the tenth day of next month.*

Perdie Ammid had come up the lane at about eleven without being asked, with the satchel, because a thing written at a wall in this valley is written in front of the valley's record. She had stood a yard and a half off and had heard every word of it, all of it, both numbers, or at any rate the one that was said out loud, and she had said nothing, and she said nothing now, and she took out sheet thirty-eight and wrote the number at the top with the word *copy* under it, and wrote out what he had said as nearly as she could, and read all of it back to the wall, including the column, including the *one*, including the word *true* in the margin, and she read it back in the flat voice she used for sheets.

"That is a fair copy," she said. "Thirty-eight."

"It is a fair copy," Tarin said.

The boy came round the board and read the fourth column upside down one more time, and Tarin watched him do it, and the boy looked up and his face did a thing Tarin could not read and did not understand until the following Wednesday.

"Mr Rusk."

"Well."

"You heard one paper," the boy said. "I was there. I held the lamp. It is a right answer to that question."

"It is a right answer."

"It is not the whole of the day," the boy said.

"No," Tarin said. "It is not."

The boy put the slate under his arm and went to fetch the pails for the noon trial, and did not ask another question about it that day, and that was the first cost, and Tarin paid it at the wall at six that evening with a shoulder that had been bad since the Saturday and a butt that took an hour and thirty-four minutes because he would not let anybody else touch it, and neither of those was the cost.

The cost was that a man had written one in a box in a fair hand, in front of the one person in this basin who had written down everything he had ever said and had never once put it in a column, and that she had heard him, and had said nothing, and would go on saying nothing, and that the not-saying was the exact thing he had done to her on the thirty-first, and that it was the reason he could not be at that wall on the thirty-first without something happening to him, and that on the thirty-first nothing had happened to him, and that on the tenth of next month it had.

She stood at the wall for a while after the form was folded into the case, with the satchel at her feet, and looked at the flat board across the two pails.

"Mrs Ammid," Tarin said. "You read three sheets out on the thirty-first."

"I did," Perdie said.

"And you are not going to say anything about that."

"I am not going to say anything about that today," Perdie Ammid said, "and I am not going to say it never, and you will find out what I have decided about it before I do, because I do not decide things quietly, and you know that better than anybody in this valley." She picked the satchel up. "Thirty-eight sheets. No book. The schedule goes down on the cart tomorrow. And you will be at the plank at six, and the butt does not care what is in a box."

He filled the butt. Eighty days. The boy counted out loud to forty and Nesta marked the shutter, and above them the frame on the culvert post went past empty, ninety years old, and the tank stood at six and an eighth with no rate in it, and on Thursday the twelfth a district went into a book and a clerk was going to read a rate out at six doors, and in a counting-house sixty-one miles east a form was going into a file with a number in it that was true, and the whole of what he had not said in fourteen years was still exactly where it had been on the thirty-first of the month before last, which was in a woman of fifty-four's memory, in a hut, by a lamp, in front of three witnesses, and which had just become the only thing in this valley with no sheet of its own.
