# Chapter 0108 — The Record House

On the Saturday morning, the thirty-first of this month, Perdie Ammid came down the lane to Nether Sallow with a satchel of numbered sheets and stood at the plank, because she was fifty-four and she had no book, and everything from the sixteenth was a numbered sheet with the word *copy* at the top, and there was no column in that valley any more.

She had not stood at the front of anything for a fortnight. From the sixteenth every entry had gone on sheets, read out to about nine people, and there was no *days lost* column and no *what it cost* column in that valley any more, because both of them were in a cart. The column stood at twenty-seven and a half days in five lines in the cart, and the figure that existed anywhere was thirty-four and a half days in seven lines on a sheet — four and a half, five and a half, four and a half, one, twelve, five and a half, and a day and a half — and there was nothing at the foot of it, and a number was due there in about a year. Twenty-seven and a half and five and a half and a day and a half is thirty-four and a half. Wick Venn had said the sum out loud in a yard at about four o'clock on the Friday the twenty-third and had priced it himself in six minutes, and she had written down every figure he gave her and had read it back to about nine people afterwards. She was the only person in the Sallow Reach who could say what a record costs, and she had spent a fortnight saying it to about nine people at a time.

"It is Saturday," she said to Nesta at the plank. "The thirty-first. I have come for the shutter figures and the stick."

Nesta gave her the week's shutter readings: a hundred and ninety, and a hundred and ninety, and a hundred and ninety, and a hundred and ninety, every trial, all week, never divided. The boy gave her the tank staff readings, which were on a slate with the times on them: two feet six and an eighth on the Thursday night at eleven o'clock, two feet five and an eighth on the Friday morning, five and seven-eighths on the Monday at eight, and five and seven-eighths every reading since. It was the last Saturday in the month, which was the day the tank card is filled in, and the card does not go anywhere until October and goes in a cart with nothing else in it. Wick, back over the ford at eight that morning with his stick, gave her the lane: middle against the third doorstep, lower than Thursday by the width of a thumb. The drain falling. The lane following by Sunday.

Perdie wrote all three on a numbered sheet with the word *copy* at the top, sheet thirty-one, dated Saturday the thirty-first, and read it out standing at the plank, because a thing on a sheet that is not read out is a thing on a sheet. About nine people heard it. Nobody thanked her and she did not ask.

Then she said, "And now the other thing. Mr Venn, walk with me to the culvert. Mr Rusk, you come too. There is a paper I have carried a fortnight and not read, and today is the day."

---

The old record house stood below the culvert, half sunk into the drain bank, a stone hut no bigger than a shed, with a door that had hung open six years and a roof of slabs grown over with grass and a step at the threshold worn into a hollow by one boot. Nobody in the valley went into it. Children said it was where the flood papers lived. Old men said it was where a keeper once sat with a book and watched the drain. There was a three-legged stool in it that nobody could remember the last use of, and a run of candle wax on the sill, and forty years of wasp paper built in the corner and gone hard as a wafer. Perdie pushed the door open with her shoulder and went in with a lamp, and Tarin and Wick and the boy went after her.

Inside were shelves of stone, and on the shelves were bundles tied with tape, grey with dust, and on the lowest shelf, where the damp had reached them, was a ledger bound in boards, swollen with wet, with *FLOOD ORDERS* tooled on the spine and a date under it a hundred and eighty-one years old.

"This is the Reach's flood book," Perdie said quietly. "Not the four field books. Older. The orders for the gates in the year of the Ninefold Flood — every order sent, every order received, with a date and an hour against each and the hand it came from. It has sat here since before the schedule of 1902 and nobody has opened it in my lifetime."

She lifted it with both hands and set it on the stone shelf by the lamp. The boards were warped. The pages inside were stuck together in places with damp. She opened it at the front first, the way a conservator opens a thing: title leaf, then the first order, checking the binding's temper before trusting the middle.

The first pages were in a clerk's fair hand, each order numbered: *gate the third opened by word of the upper villages, sixth hour, for the melt; gate the fifth shut by word of the lower bank, ninth hour, against the rise.* Dates under each, a hundred and eighty-one years old, ink gone brown at the edges. The boy leaned in, breathing through his mouth, and Wick held the lamp steady with his good hand.

"Read one out," Perdie said to the boy. "A thing in a book that is not read out is a thing in a book."

The boy bent close, squinting. "*Gate the seventh opened—* by word of— the middle parishes, at dawn, the— twelfth day." He looked up. "Three different words in three orders. Upper villages. Lower bank. Middle parishes."

"Then the gates were fought over," Wick said.

"I do not know that," Perdie said. "I know what is on the page. I know there are three hands on the front leaves and a fourth somewhere behind the damp. I know three leaves in the middle have gone into one wad that will never open again without taking ink off two others with it. I am not going to read a flood off a wet page in a hut in the dark, and not one of you is going to either, and if a man from a counting-house at Vellum stands in that doorway and asks me what the year of the Ninefold Flood was, I will hand him the book and not an opinion." She turned the leaves as far as the middle, carefully, the way a woman handles a thing that can only be opened once, and stopped where the damp had done its work.

Then she turned toward the back, where the last gathering should have been, and stopped. Her breath caught once, the way a woman's breath catches when a book she has known all her life shows a wound. The last pages were gone. Cut out, cleanly, at the binding, with a knife, leaving stubs about a finger-width wide. About a dozen pages. The stubs showed dates at their heads in the same faded hands, the same flood year, the last week of it, the week the water cut through the middle basin. Between the last remaining page and the back board there was nothing but stubs and a faint shadow on the board where a leaf had lain against it for a hundred and eighty-one years.

Perdie held the lamp closer. On the inside of the back board, in pencil, in a hand she knew, was a line: *taken to Vellum for the inquiry, with the keeper's leave —* and a date, fourteen years old, and no signature, and no name of any keeper, and no record anywhere of any return.

Wick bent to read it, holding the lamp with his good hand. "Whose hand," he said quietly.

"A clerk's hand," Perdie said. "Fourteen years old. I know it the way a conservator knows hands, by habit and not by proof, and I will not name a man on habit, because a man is never to be taken to a desk on a specimen, and that rule is right." She straightened. "What I will say, on Saturday the thirty-first, in front of three witnesses, is what is on the board. Taken to Vellum for the inquiry, with the keeper's leave, fourteen years ago, no signature, no keeper named, no return recorded. Somebody with the authority to have taken it has not written down that they took it. That is the whole of it. The hand is a desk and not a man, and a desk cannot be cross-examined."

"Fourteen years," Wick said quietly.

"Fourteen years," Perdie said. "A pencil line with a date and no name, saying taken with leave, and nobody's leave is written anywhere, and nothing has come back." She closed the ledger gently. "I am not saying whose hand. I am not saying what was on the pages. I am saying there are stubs with dates and a pencil line with a date, and that is what goes on a numbered sheet and gets read out, because that is what I do now."

She wrote it on sheet thirty-two, at the culvert, standing up, with the ledger on the stone shelf beside her: *the flood book in the record house below the culvert, orders for the gates of the flood year, in three hands at the front, last gathering cut out clean at the binding, about a dozen pages, stubs dated the last week of that year, pencil line inside the back board saying taken to Vellum for the inquiry with the keeper's leave, dated fourteen years ago, no signature, no keeper named, no return recorded*. She read it out. The boy, Wick, and Tarin heard it. The drain ran past below them, falling, with the flume mouth empty above.

"It is Saturday the thirty-first," Perdie said, writing the date under it with the sheet number. "Thirty-two sheets. No book. Three witnesses, and all three of them are in it." She looked at each of them in turn: a man of thirty-eight with one hand, a man on his seventieth day with his name in two books four hundred yards apart, a boy of fifteen with a slate of his own. "In about a year somebody in a counting-house at Vellum looks at a column with seven lines in it and nothing at the foot of it. In four years a clerk lays a card with a date and a gate with a date side by side. That is what paper does. It waits. What I do is write it so that the waiting leaves a mark."

"Is that the book of 1843," the boy asked. He was fifteen, and he had the wording of a fifth rod on the back of his own hand in his own writing, and he had heard the number said out loud twice in a shop in a month without ever once being told what it was about.

"No," Perdie said. "The book of 1843 does not exist in this valley and nobody has asked for it in a hundred and thirty years. That is on a numbered sheet going to Vellum and it is not resolved. This is a different book, in three hands, a hundred and eighty-one years old, and it sat here the whole while the far bank went on being nobody on paper, and now there is water in that lane and a magistrate has barred two doors in it. Do not join them. A book that is gone and a book that never existed are two different things, and joining them is how a valley gets a story instead of a record."

The boy looked at the stubs a long time. "Mrs Ammid. Who cuts pages out of a flood book."

"Somebody with a knife and authority," Perdie said. "The cut is clean, at the binding, one gathering, and it is not damp and it is not mice and it is not time. A hand did it, fourteen years ago or thereabouts, and wrote a pencil line saying taken with leave, and no keeper's leave is written anywhere." She set the ledger down square on the shelf, its fore-edge flush with the shelf's own edge, which was the way she had been taught to put a book down by a woman who is dead. "I am fifty-four and I have kept four books twenty-six years in a chest, and I know what taking a page costs. It costs the book. This book paid it. What is left is stubs, and a pencil line, and a date on both."

---

They walked back up to the plank in silence. At the wall Tarin stopped. The butt stood full behind it from the evening before, and Nesta's shutter showed the week's figures, never divided, and the card at the culvert showed the third rod empty with a padlock printed under it and the new line with a heading and nothing under it. The plank, the tank, the flume, the card, the wages, the weeks: all of it held. The thing that did not hold was a gathering cut out of a flood book fourteen years ago with a pencil line and no name, and it was in a stone hut below the culvert because nobody had ever opened the door.

"Mrs Ammid," he said. "Saturday the thirty-first. I want a sheet too."

Perdie looked at him. She was fifty-four, and she had written to a master four times in thirty-one years, three about a book and one about a book that was not hers, and her letter was three lines, unfinished, not sent, with four days of the fortnight left on the twenty-third and the fortnight long past now.

"Say it," she said.

"Fourteen years ago I signed a closure." Tarin said it standing at the wall with the pail in his hand, with Wick and the boy beside him, the way a man says a thing he has carried fourteen years. "I was nineteen, with fourteen months in service, on the Riverside cut. A side channel. A flood. I signed the order that sealed it. It saved the hospital. It trapped people in a lower courtyard. The inquiry called it justified and it was made without authority." He stopped. "That is the act. There is a schedule of the persons it closed on. I am not giving you the schedule and I am not going to say how many names are on it, because the number is the whole of what I have not said in fourteen years and I am not going to hand it over on a Saturday at a wall. I am saying that I signed it, and that I have known fourteen years there is one thing in that file that is incomplete, and that I will not say what it is, because I would have to say a name."

Nobody said anything. The plank ran at a hundred and ninety below them. Perdie took out a fresh sheet, numbered it thirty-three, wrote *copy* at the top, and wrote what he had said, word for word as nearly as she could, with the date, Saturday the thirty-first. Then she wrote one line more in her own hand, which was that the witness had declined to give a schedule of persons and had declined to give a number, and that the sheet therefore carried the act and not the list, and she read all of it back.

"Is that right, Mr Rusk."

"That is right," Tarin said. "And it is not in a book. There is no book. Put it in the satchel with the rest."

She did. Thirty-three sheets. No *days lost* column. No *what it cost* column. A flood book with its last pages cut out, and a man's fourteen-year closure on a sheet, in the same satchel, on the same day, and neither of them joined to the other.

"Monday the court sits," Perdie said. "The magistrate lays *persons accounted for* before it. Sella Marr, third door, nineteen years, is on a sealing order. That is in the bundle." She looked at the full butt behind the wall. "And you fill the butt at six. That is not on any sheet and it is the truest thing in this valley."

She went up the lane with the satchel, fifty-four years old, with thirty-three sheets and no book, on Saturday the thirty-first, leaving the record house open behind her with its stubs and its pencil line and no name.
