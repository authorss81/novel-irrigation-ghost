# Chapter 0063 — Three Jars And A Ring

She came up the Kerrow lane on the Thursday evening of the fourth day of this month in a hired chaise with a boot on the near side, with a woman of twenty-six beside her who had been paid four pounds to come and would have to go back for the other four, and she walked the last eleven miles before the chaise had finished it, and she had left her own coast on the Tuesday morning on a road that takes two days each way, which is why she came in on the Thursday and not on the Friday.

Perdie Ammid had a room made up and a butt of water in the yard and did not once say that this was a long way to come.

The three of them had supper. Yara Sen ate what she was given and drank the tea, which was made with the butt and not with the jar, and everybody in that room had known within about four minutes that it was the butt, and nobody said anything about it, and Bess Ilk noticed that a guest had noticed and was pleased about it in a way she did not show.

---

On the Saturday morning, before anybody had asked her anything, she put it on the table in the front room of the shop.

"The fourth line," she said. "The witness box is still empty and a woman of seventy-one has said she will not fill it.

"That is what I came for. I have wanted for nine days to know what the water of a lane of nineteen houses is where the oldest woman in it is seventy-one and there is nobody in the lane younger than the water, and I have wanted it badly enough to come sixty-one miles on a road that takes two days each way, and I have wanted it for a reason that had nothing to do with your count or your notice or your book.

"I want you to understand the shape of it, because I would rather you had it from me than work it out. I could have written to you. I did not write to you. I am a physician and I do not ask a man for a water in a letter, because a water has to come out of the ground in front of me at least once and a description of one is a description of a thing I have not touched, and I have been fooled four times in my life by a description and twice by a plate.

"And the fourth line was not about evidence. It was about a woman, and it was the only one of four lines with a woman in it, and a physician reads a line like that the way other people read a name, and I have not got a rule against it and I have thought about having one since Tuesday and I have not got one."

Tarin Rusk said, "Then you have come for the wrong thing and I should have told you on the Friday."

"You should have," said Yara Sen, "and I did not give you a chance, and I would have refused it. That is not your fault. I have been doing this for nineteen years and I know exactly what I do when a room offers me a better reason than the one I came with, and I took it."

---

She asked for three glazed jars and a ring and a fire and about two hours of the Saturday, and Bess Ilk gave her three jars out of a box of nine that she keeps for preserves, and a ring, and the fire, and a hook in the fire that had been made for a kettle in 1844 and had been the right size for four different kettles.

"The first is off the plank at Nether Sallow," said Yara Sen. "Fill it yourself, off the notch, with the pail, and do not rinse it, and do not ask anybody to pour for me, and if a man in this lane tells you that jar is for a count and not for me, he is right and I want him to say it out loud."

She filled it herself with a pail, at the notch, at half past ten, and she filled it to the mark and no more.

"The second is off my own churn."

Nobody in that room had asked.

"It came up the lane behind the chaise and it has been in a churn for two days and a half across sixty-one miles in a heat," she said, "and it is the same water as the water in the arch at Cresset where a girl of seven lives, and I have been drinking it since before I came west, and I have not boiled it, because I could not afford to boil it, and if you are about to tell me a physician should do better then you are right, and the physician has eleven gallons a day for a hundred and twenty-six people, and the arithmetic of it is in a book, and I have not come west to be given it by a village."

And the third was rain.

It had rained about a quarter of an inch on the top of the Kerrow valley in the early hours of Thursday the fourth of this month, for about forty minutes, and Bess Ilk had put a clean bowl out on the shop step at half past two in the morning because there had been nothing else to do at two in the morning in a drought, and on the Friday morning Yara Sen asked for it and Bess Ilk said, "Then that is yours, and it has stood on that step since Thursday morning and nobody has had it out of it and it is your jug now."

She boiled for an hour each, with the jars standing in the water on the ring, in the same pan at the same heat, and she wrote the times on Bess Ilk's slate and would not let anybody else touch the slate. Then she put the three jars on the sill in the sun in the order she had boiled them for an hour, and then she picked them up one at a time and turned them.

"Any person may do this," she said. "A pint of the water, a glazed jar, a ring, an hour of boiling, and an hour in the sun. There is nothing in that list that costs money and nothing in it that takes a trade, and I have known a district of a hundred and twenty-six people for two years who believed they could not do anything about their water, and that belief is the most expensive thing in this basin and I am handing it out for nothing in a bookshop in a village.

"The crust is the dissolved part of the water. It comes down out of it when it boils. It is white, and this is a hard country, and every one of these three has come down out of rock, and there is a crust in all three of them and the crust in all three of them is the same colour and that is not nothing, because a great many bad waters in this basin do not crust white, they crust green, and green is a different complaint with a different name and a different death.

"That is the whole of what I can tell anybody and it is worth having and it is a great deal more than I had before a wagon of engineers came up a mountain road in the third week of a drought."

She set the jars down in a row.

"Now the other two things, and one of them is about my own work and I am not going to leave it out.

"Two gallons an hour goes down the Reach's long drain into a drain that a woman has been catching with a plank for thirty-two years, and a great many of those gallons go into nineteen houses' kettles, and there is a crust in every kettle in this lane, and that is the classification and the classification is correct and I will defend it against anybody in this room, including you, and it is that the water in this lane cannot be given to a child unboiled and that is not a discovery, that is a practice, and a village that has boiled its water for sixty years has a practice and not a defect and I have no more business here than the water has.

"And I am wrong about it, and I am right about it, and both of those are true, and it took three jars and an hour to show me both of them. I am right that the crust is there. I am wrong that the crust settles anything, and a physician who has found a crust thinks she has found a question and she has found a test, and a test that gives the same answer on three different waters from three different places in sixty-one miles is telling me about the country and not about the water, and I have known that since about the fourth of this month and I came anyway, because I am not able to leave a question alone once I have heard one and that is the other fault and it is the older one."

---

The classification was put to a figure on the Sunday morning, in the front room, with about twenty people in it, and it was done by Domen Prouse, who had brought no beans to Brant and had brought a chair.

He put it in the plainest form anybody in the room had heard.

"Doctor. The count is on Sunday. The window is on Monday. There are eight hundred and fifty gallons an hour coming out of that embankment and there are nine hundred acres of apple in this Reach and there are ten days to set them in. Tell us the water is good and tell us it is not good and we will act on it in a room this morning and I do not care which."

Yara Sen listened to the whole of it and then asked for a slate and took it off Bess Ilk and turned it round.

"Eight hundred and fifty gallons an hour," she said. "For ten days. That is two hundred and four thousand gallons. For seventeen hundred acres, that is a hundred and twenty gallons an acre.

"I am not going to certify that number, and I want the reason given in the room and not afterwards, and the reason is not the water.

"The water is not the reason. The water will do whatever water does at a hundred and twenty gallons an acre. The reason is that a hundred and twenty gallons an acre is not a crop and is not half a crop, and it is a number a man can put on a card and take into a field and say *we have water*, and then four hundred families in this Reach will spend the ten days of a window that has already closed putting water on ground they cannot water, instead of doing the only two things there are, which are to set what can be set and to write down what cannot.

"I am not being clever. I have been a physician for nineteen years and this is the fifth time somebody has asked me to make a number of gallons mean something, and four times out of five the gallons are the wrong number, and the only question anybody ever asks me is a question about the gallons.

"And I would like it written down that I have said the number out loud. I have not certified it. I have said it, so that a room has got it, and I have said I will not put my name to it, and both of those are true, and if a man wants a certificate he is welcome to go and find one in this basin and I do not think he will."

Nobody thanked her. Domen Prouse wrote the two figures on the back of his hand, which he did with a nail, and did not say thank you, and said something else instead.

"You have been here two days," he said. "Is that all you came for."

"No," said Yara Sen.

"Then I am glad I asked you in that order," said Domen Prouse, "because I am the one who is going to have to say something about it on Sunday and I would rather be told on the Saturday."

---

There was tea in the middle of the Saturday and Bess Ilk made it out of the butt because it was Saturday and there were twenty people in her front room and the jar is not boiled, and Yara Sen poured it, and Tarin Rusk drank a cup of it standing up the way he had drunk a cup of it on a step in a lane sixty-one miles east in the spring.

It was hot. It went down the way water goes down. It had no taste of any kind, and it had no bitterness, and it had not had any for thirteen weeks and a day, and he had drunk it every working day for eleven years before that, and he stood in a bookshop in a village and drank a cup of it and could not find anything there to miss.

Yara Sen watched him drink it, and she watched him the way a woman watches a man who has done something to himself and does not know that he has done it, and she waited until the others had the cups and then said, quietly, in about nine words:

"You have not tasted anything for some weeks, and you are not going to tell me what, and I am not going to ask you twice."

And Tarin Rusk said, "No," and Yara Sen said, "Right," and they both drank their tea, and that was the end of it, and it was on the page because it happened.

---

On the Sunday evening she got it wrong out loud, in the front room, with the last of the light going off the top of the hill behind the shop.

"I was wrong about one thing and it is not about the water and I would rather say it here than carry it down sixty-one miles and put it in a book.

"I came west expecting to be no use. I have had a practice for nineteen years in a city on a coast road, and a hundred and twenty-six people of it are on a ration cut by a fifth, and I have got very good at being the one who can be made no use of, because being the one who can be made no use of is the only position I can hold that nobody can be turned out of, and I came here for two days' work with a jar.

"And I have been here three days and I have been useful, and it has taken me about a day and a half to notice that being useful in a place with no patients is the most dangerous state a physician can be in, and it is dangerous because there is nothing in this village to get worse and so every hour I spend in it is an hour I am not spending on a girl of seven."

Then she said the thing, and she said it standing up, in about twenty-five words, and she did not soften it and she did not make it smaller by being quiet.

"I am going back on Monday morning with the chaise, and I have not seen a child since the day before I came, and on Wednesday I am back with a girl called Sab Doulton who is seven and is at the fourth point and is not improving, and the count of my cases has not moved since before I came west, and it stands at four children at four points and four at two, and the requirement I set out for that district was sixty-eight gallons a day and the derivation of it has stopped, because a child in the fourth stage does not take four gallons and there is no figure for a child in the fourth stage anywhere in this basin, including in me.

"I have said that out loud in a room because a physician who keeps it back has already lost the ground, and I said it here rather than in the lane because you are all going to hear about the jars for a year and you are going to hear about the count on the fourteenth for a year, and both of those are true and this is also true, and it is thirty of the words in the last five minutes and it has taken me nineteen years to be able to say thirty words in five minutes in front of a room that does not need me."

Bess Ilk put her hand flat on the counter and left it there.

And nobody thanked her, and she had not come to be thanked, and about two hundred yards away in a lane of nineteen houses a woman of seventy-one who had been alive for seventy-one of the two hundred and fifty years of a stone on a bank was asleep, and did not know that any of it had happened.

---

Before she went she wrote the test out, on a sheet of paper out of Bess Ilk's drawer, with her own hand, and she made Bess Ilk take it and hold it up and read it back to her, which is a thing a woman does with a thing she is leaving in a place she is not going to be able to come back to.

> *A pint of the water in a glazed jar. An hour of boiling with the jar standing in the water on a ring. An hour in the sun. Look at the crust on the inside of the jar.
> *A white crust is the dissolved part of the water and it comes out of it when it boils. A crust that has run up the glaze in a ridge is worse than one that has not. A green crust is a different complaint and it has a different name and it is not the same thing.
> *This does not tell you whether the water is fit to drink. It tells you how the water compares with another water, and only another water from the same place and the same day, and nothing else.
> *It does not tell you where the dissolved part came from. It does not tell you how much of it there is. I cannot give you a number and I am not going to invent one in front of you, and anybody who gives you a number for this is guessing.
> *— Y. Sen.*

And then she put it on a nail in the bookshop door at Brant, beside the nail where the third copy of the count's figures is going to be on Sunday evening, and the two pieces of paper were the same width and they hung about a hand apart and neither of them had a number on it, and the right-hand one had a hole in it where a number should have been, and Bess Ilk looked at the two of them for a while and then went and got a third nail and hung nothing on it, because she is a publican and she does that sort of thing, and would not say why, and had said why in 1846.
