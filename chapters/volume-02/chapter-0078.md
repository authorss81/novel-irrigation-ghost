# Chapter 0078 — A Hole You Dig To Get Rid Of Water

It was Nesta Sarg who asked the question on the Monday morning of the twenty-ninth day of this month, in the front room of the shop at Brant, at about eight o'clock, and she asked it standing up and she asked it in about forty words and then she sat down and let twenty other people try to talk over it, which she had not done in thirty-two years and did not do again.

"I have been asked three times this month what I take out of a drain," she said. "I have been asked by a printer's woman, and by a woman from the city, and by a boy with a stick in a lane. I have not been asked once what is in one.

"The throat gives eight hundred and fifty gallons an hour. The plank gives a hundred and ninety. The difference is six hundred and sixty and it goes into the ground under a lane three hundred and forty yards above my pail, and I have had that figure since the second of this month and I have not said a word about where it goes, because I did not know, and because a woman of forty-one who says *I do not know* in a room like this one does not get asked the next question.

"The lane is on a clay. The clay is under the lower field. The lower field is nine hundred acres and it is drained by moles, and the moles empty into the Sallow Dyke, and the Sallow Dyke is the main drain of the lower field and it is two miles four chains long, and it is written in a Guild volume in a book that was in a chest for eight years as a land drain with no supply and no plate, and it is written on a printed slip in this room as a channel from the tank with the flume sluice.

"Nobody in this Reach has ever measured it. I have been asked what I take out of it nine hundred thousand times by a plank. Nobody has ever put a lath in it."

---

They were in the lowest field at about half past ten in the morning, at the second bridge, with a lath and a watch and a boy with a rule and a barrow and about eleven people, and there was a man of fifty-eight there because he has eleven acres of beans in that field and had no reason to leave them, and there was a woman of seventy-one there because somebody had told her at the gate and she had come.

The Sallow Dyke at the second bridge is a cut in a blue clay about a foot wide at the bottom and a foot deep, with a coping of two courses of stone laid by somebody who was not a mole man, and the water was in it and it was clear and it was moving.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Right," said Jory Vant. "How do we do this."

"You have told me," said Tarin Rusk.

"Two trials, with the lath twenty feet, and the watch, and a section, and the section first. Rule in."

The boy of fifteen put the rule in and brought it out and wiped it on his hand and put it in again.

"About a foot by about a foot," he said. "It is a foot and a bit over at the coping and a foot at the bottom on this side and eleven inches on the other, and I do not think you can call it anything except a foot by a foot, and a foot by a foot is a square foot, and a hundred and forty-four inches make a foot, so that is one square foot and it is a good thing that a thing comes out square, because I do not want to do a sum in a field."

"A cubic foot is seven and a half gallons in this basin," said Tarin Rusk, "and it has been seven and a half gallons in this basin since before anybody now living was born, and it is in a book at Culm, and I am not going to check it, because it is the only figure in this month that has never once been wrong and if I check it we will be here at dusk."

He put the lath in.

"Twenty feet. Go."

"Twelve minutes," said Jory Vant.

"Again."

"Twelve and a half."

"Both of those printed," said Tarin Rusk. "Do them again in a quarter of an hour, and both of them, and I will write them down and you will write them down, and neither of us is to be told the figure until both are in the book."

---

They did them again at a quarter past eleven and the second pair was twelve minutes and twelve and a half minutes, exactly the same, which is the one thing about a count that matters and which nobody has to be told.

"Square foot of cut," said Jory Vant, on the bank, with the figures on the back of his own hand in pencil. "Twelve minutes for twenty feet is a foot and two-thirds a minute. A foot and two-thirds a minute into a square foot is a foot and two-thirds of a cubic foot a minute. That is twelve and a half gallons a minute. That is seven hundred and fifty gallons an hour."

"And twelve and a half minutes is one and three-fifths of a foot a minute," said Tarin Rusk, "which is one and three-fifths of a cubic foot a minute, which is twelve gallons a minute, which is seven hundred and twenty gallons an hour."

"Seven hundred and fifty and seven hundred and twenty," said Jory Vant. "About four per cent apart. Write down seven hundred and thirty-five and be dull about it."

He had the lath out of the cut and in his hand and he was not looking at Tarin Rusk.

"Say that number again," he said.

"Seven hundred and thirty-five," said Tarin Rusk.

"That is what I said in a room in Brant on the eighteenth day of this month, at about half past eleven in the morning, and you told me in front of nine people that there was no seven hundred and thirty-five on it because there was no such measurement." Jory Vant put the lath down on the bank. "There was not. There is now. And I was wrong for eleven days and I did not know it, and I want that written down, because in about eleven years somebody is going to find a card with a number on it and no history, and the history is that a boy of nineteen said it eleven days early in a room and was wrong, and that is the only evidence in this basin that anybody ever gets for anything."

"No," said Tarin Rusk. "I am going to write down seven hundred and thirty-five and give the reason in a field where a woman of seventy-one can hear me, and the reason is not a fitter's reason this time, and I want it said that it is not.

"A fitter would take seven hundred and fifty, because seven hundred and twenty is the figure that is true and seven hundred and fifty is the figure that is generous, and a card is a generous thing and it has to be generous for thirty years after everybody in the room is dead. And she would be right about that, and I have never once in twenty-four years of watching her seen her wrong about it, and she is on a mountain road and there is nobody left in this Reach who is going to tell me I have chosen the wrong one.

"I am taking the middle one because there is nobody in this Reach left to check me. That is the whole of the reason and I am thirty-three years old and I have never had to say a sentence like it and I have said it once and I am not going to say it again.

"Seven hundred and fifty is a round figure and a round figure gets repeated at somebody. Seven hundred and twenty is the one that will be quoted as the small one for about thirty years. And the middle is the only one of the three that will still be true on the day that somebody is looking for a reason to say the other two were picked to suit somebody."

---

"Do the sum," said Nesta Sarg.

"There is a sum and I am not enjoying it and I am going to do it twice, and the first one is the one that matters and it is the one nobody in this field has thought of.

"Seven hundred and thirty-five an hour is twenty-four hours of it, which is **seventeen thousand six hundred and forty gallons a day.** That is a day and a night of it, in a field, in a Reach in a drought, going west.

"The fourth book puts the whole of the drinking of this Reach at four gallons a household a day for two hundred and ninety households, which is **one thousand one hundred and sixty a day.** Seventeen thousand six hundred and forty is fifteen times that. Every morning and every evening and every night, for the length of a day, this field has been passing under the reach of nine hundred acres of apple more water than the whole of this Reach drinks in a day, and the Reach's own assize, out of a locked press at Culm, in a hand that is a copy of a hand two centuries older, puts a house's season at ninety-four thousand five hundred gallons, and one fifth of that is eighteen thousand nine hundred, over four days is four thousand seven hundred and twenty-five a household a day, and two hundred and ninety households at one fifth for the season is **five million four hundred and eighty-one thousand gallons.**

"At seventeen thousand six hundred and forty a day, that season of five million four hundred and eighty-one thousand gallons goes in **three hundred and ten days.**" He looked up. "The season is not three hundred and ten days long. It shut on Wednesday the twenty-fourth, and the ground is like a bench, and the next sowing is not a thing anybody in this basin has a date for, and I have looked, and there is no date, and I am not going to invent one in a field. So this Reach has been spending three hundred and ten days of its own season a year, in a drain, on a field, for six years, and it is a very good season and it goes out of the reach, and the reach has never once had an account of it."

"There is a second sum," said Tarin Rusk, "and I am doing it because three people are going to ask me for it inside a week and I would rather have said it first.

"Seven hundred and thirty-five at the dyke, and a hundred and ninety at the plank, is **nine hundred and twenty-five.** The throat at the top of that lane is eight hundred and fifty. So I have made seventy-five gallons an hour out of nothing, and I want everybody in this field to have heard me do it and to have heard me refuse to fix it.

"I will not fix it. And here is the bound I will go to and no further. That tank has not moved an eighth of an inch in six years, and the whole of six years' variation in that tank is an eighth of an inch, and the only thing out of that tank is a six-inch pipe with a joint open in the crown of it. So the works pass about what the throat passes and not a millimetre more. Which means seven hundred and thirty-five and a hundred and ninety cannot both be coming out of eight hundred and fifty separately, and the only reading left is that they are the same water counted twice, the water going over the side of that pipe into a gravel bed under a lane and coming back out of the ground in a clay a mile and a half away.

"And I cannot prove it. I have got a rule and a lath and a watch and a ladle, and I have got no instrument in this basin that can tell me whether the water in this cut came out of the top of a pipe in a chamber nineteen feet down a mountain, and I am not going to stand in a lane with a ladle and call it the same. I have watched men do that in other counties. It is always the same water in the end, in the book, about four months after they have been wrong."

---

Nobody had said the thing yet and the boy of fifteen said it, standing in the water up to his boot, which he was not supposed to do.

"Where is it going?"

"There is a second cut at the bottom of the field," said Aldis Kerr, "and it has been going into the Brasse Fen since before my mother, and out of the Brasse Fen side cut into the Sere, and the Sere is not ours, and the Sere goes down to Cresset, and there has been a right of common on that side cut since about the time of the assize and nobody has used it in my life and nobody before me in hers."

"Will it ever be possible to get any of it into a field?" said Tarin Rusk, and he asked it properly, on the bank, in daylight, in front of eleven people, because it is the whole of the next four weeks and he wanted it in the record in that order and not in a room afterwards.

"No," said Aldis Kerr, "and I will tell you why in one sentence and then a fitter will tell you the other half of it better than I can.

"You cannot lift water. That cut is below the last stone of your flume. Your flume drops four feet onto a stone and the water is gone; the bottom of this cut is below the place where your water leaves the world. Anything you can put down that flume will arrive above me and go over me, and I have watched a man try to argue with that in the year I was forty and I am not going to sit here and watch it twice."

"Mr Kerr is right about the ground," said Tarin Rusk, "and the other half of it is mine and it is worse, and I am going to say it in a field and not in a room, and Jory Vant is going to write it down.

"**It is the Reach's own drainage.** That cut is not taking the water off that field. It is the field's own water table, coming up through a clay, and it is the moles that put it there, and the moles are there because a person dug them, and they were dug because a person wanted that field to be a field. The assize of the settling of this Reach says that a house in the lower field gets ninety-four thousand five hundred gallons a season, and every one of those houses is standing in nine hundred acres that is not a marsh, and it is not a marsh because that cut has been taking it away since before the assize was written down, and the Reach has been living on its drainage for as long as there has been a Reach.

"Take the water out of that cut and you do not get seven hundred and thirty-five gallons an hour for a crop. You get a lower field with no water table in it that has been drained for about six hundred years, and in a wet winter that field is a marsh by February, and a marsh is a marsh by June as well, and the Reach's season is not a single year's season. **The assize is a hundred and thirty years old and it is not a plan for this year. It is a plan for ever.**

"And the last thing, and it is the one that will not let any of this month be tidied up in a shed, is four miles up the Kerrow valley on the west shoulder of the service road, on the same main, and it is a cross joint in the crown of a hundred-and-eighty-one-year-old conduit, four and a half inches across, and there is ninety gallons an hour coming out of it into a field and there is a rind of lime on the lip the size of a thumb and the rind is the only thing in this basin standing between that joint and a face of stone the size of a doorway, and nobody has cleared it and nobody is going to clear it, and it says on a slate on a fence that it is not to be cleared and that there is nobody to catch.

"So we need that gate. We cannot feed it. And the reason we cannot feed it is a cut in a clay nine hundred acres of apple are standing in, and both of those are the same gate and neither of them can be moved this month. And on Wednesday the ground went like a bench, and the ground is like a bench until a day in a month that has not got a number in this basin yet."

---

Then Nesta Sarg said the sentence, and it took her about twenty words, and she said it to Tarin Rusk and not to a room.

"You have had a piece of paper in your coat since the eighteenth of this month," she said, "and you have had it in four books and a chest and a shed and a line at the bottom of a printed form about a door, and I am told you tore it up on Saturday and I am told the four lines are in the book anyway.

"So I am going to say the thing about your paper and then I am going to go and do my own work, and I am not going to be thanked and I am not going to be argued with.

"**A mole is a hole you dig to get rid of water.** That is all a mole is. That is what the word is. And you have spent a fortnight of a planting window building a road about a hole you dig to get rid of water, and you have written on it a figure of eight hundred and fifty gallons an hour, and a tank of six thousand gallons of room, and a mark in a wall, and two miles four chains of a channel, and every one of those is true, and the true thing about all of them is that they are about a reach this Reach has never had.

"And the Reach has got one. **The Reach has got a hole, and the hole is full.** It has been full for six years, at seventeen thousand six hundred and forty gallons a day, in a cut that two hundred and fifty years of this village's grandmothers have called the Sallow, and it goes west, and it goes to a river that is not ours, and it is the only supply this Reach has that anybody has ever measured and it is not a supply, it is a drain, and it cannot be shut because there is nothing on it to shut, and it cannot be used because using it is drowning the field it is in.

"You have printed the number of a hole you dig to get rid of water. And you did it honestly, and the honesty is the whole of the trick, and I have thought about it for an hour and I could not get round it, and I have been trying to get round men in rooms for thirty-two years."

---

Nobody thanked her and she did not want to be thanked, and Perdie Ammid wrote the whole of it in the second book, in her own hand, with the date, and under it she wrote four lines, because a statement can be improved on in an afternoon and a list has to be got right.

> *1. The Sallow Dyke, main cut, second bridge, in the lowest field: one square foot of section. Twenty feet in twelve minutes, and twenty feet in twelve and a half minutes, both taken twice, both pairs the same. Seven hundred and fifty gallons an hour and seven hundred and twenty gallons an hour, both printed. The figure is seven hundred and thirty-five and the reason is given on the page and is a fitter's absence and not a fitter's rule.*
> *2. Seventeen thousand six hundred and forty gallons a day, which is fifteen times the whole of the drinking of this Reach in the fourth book's figure of one thousand one hundred and sixty a day, and which is the Reach's own season of five million four hundred and eighty-one thousand gallons in three hundred and ten days.*
> *3. It is not available. It is below the flume's own outfall and cannot be lifted, and it is the lower field's own drainage, and the lower field is not drained in a wet winter without it, and the assize is a hundred and thirty years old and is a plan for ever and not for this year.*
> *4. The water was not lost. It was made blind, by one name, written down twice, in two places, in two centuries, in two hands, for two good reasons.*

At about four o'clock they came out of the field and the run of moles that they had walked back along to get to the bridge was soft underfoot for about a quarter of a mile, and nobody stepped in it, and there was a barrow for it.

And Jory Vant had the two figures and the two pairs of trials and the arithmetic on the back of his own hand in pencil, and the boy of fifteen asked him whether it was going on a card.

"No," said Jory Vant.

"It should go on a card."

"It should go on a card," said Jory Vant, "and there is not one, and the last one was cut on the Tuesday of the week before last by a boy who is not to be named, and the printing of it was in Culm and the last one has been on a post at the culvert and on a nail in a bookshop door and in a book in this village and one went up to a city sixty-one miles east on a cart on Friday, and there is no card and no nail and no press in this village, and I have got it on my hand, and a hand is not a place anybody can come and look at.

"That is a padlock," said the boy of fifteen. "That is exactly what a padlock is."
