# Chapter 0095 — The Last Stone

They went down the flume on the Sunday morning of the eighteenth because there was nothing else to do with a Sunday, and because the launder had not been looked at in six years and four hours and six minutes of it was going to arrive at eleven o'clock on Thursday night at three-quarters of an inch deep in a channel that had had six years of a field growing into it.

It is two miles and four chains from the tank to its outfall. The first mile is on the low side of the service road. Then it leaves the road and crosses a field on three stone piers and comes back and runs on down the side of a hedge for about a mile. Then it comes off the top of a bank on three more piers, and the last of them is about a foot and a half out of the ground and has a hazel growing out of the side of it that is older than every man on the flume.

Six inches wide, and the sides are coursed stone, and the invert is stone, and there is a film of silt in the bottom of it that a boot heel goes through. That is the whole of what six years does to a stone channel that is not on anybody's schedule: nothing dramatic, and everything.

They did not clean it. Sef Ordry said in the first hundred yards that they were not cleaning it and that anybody who wrote down that they cleaned it would be a fool.

"**We are going to rod it, we are going to break the crust where the crust is holding water, and we are going to clear the piers.** Two miles and a bit of a six-inch stone launder is four days' work for six men with bars and it does not need doing all of it to move four hours of water. What it needs is for the low places to not be low. There are four low places and three of them are at a pier."

He cleared the piers. That took until about half past three in the afternoon and it was the whole of the day's work, and five men and a boy did it, and four of them were going to be at three points on that launder at eleven o'clock on the Thursday night and were in a field four hundred yards away at eight o'clock that afternoon.

Jory Vant did the piers with a bar and a bucket and got the skin off two of them. Wick Venn did the piers with one hand and a bucket and could not have done it with the bar, and carried the buckets, four hundred yards each way, about forty times, and said nothing about the count and was counted. The boy of fifteen carried a lantern at the third pier because it was in a cutting and it was dark at four o'clock in a lane, and had been dark at four for a month, and was told by a man of thirty-eight to hold it lower and was told that a lantern held high is a lantern that shows the men holding it and not the stone, and lowered it.

And Tarin Rusk spent about eleven minutes of the Sunday doing anything to a launder. He walked it. He walked the whole two miles and four chains and back in parts, with a rule, and at four places he stopped and wrote a figure on the back of his own hand, and at the two piers over the long drain he measured the fall, and at the outfall he stood on the last stone and looked at the drop.

Four feet. Down onto the long drain, which is a foot and a half deep and in gravel and has been carrying a hundred and ninety gallons an hour at the plank for six years and has not been dry within living memory.

He wrote four numbers on his hand and gave the hand to Perdie Ammid, who was at the outfall because she is the only person in the Sallow Reach who is a party to anything this month and a party is a person who has to be standing where the water ends up, and she read them off his hand onto the back of the counterfoil, and the counterfoil is in a satchel with a strap, and the satchel is four days up a road, and there is nothing to copy them into.

"I have copied them out four times," she said, "on the back of the counterfoil, and the counterfoil is in a satchel with a strap, and the satchel is four days up a road, and I am going to say a thing about that which nobody in this valley is going to like."

"Say it."

"**The record of this flume left this village on Friday morning and we are standing in the flume.** The four fall measurements taken this afternoon are on the back of a counterfoil in a satchel on a mountain road, and there is no copy of them, and in about four years a man in a counting-house at Vellum can tell you that somebody measured four things in a launder on the eighteenth of this month, and he can tell you the numbers, and he will not be able to tell you who took them or with what or whether they were taken twice.

"I have been the person on this side of that sentence for thirty-one years," she said, "and I know exactly what it costs, and I have said the whole of it out loud to about six people and that is all I can do with it, and that is not enough, and I have done it anyway.""

---

Aldis Kerr was at the top of the lane at about eleven o'clock on the Sunday, and she was not asked to be there, and she came, because she is seventy-one and she is the oldest person in that lane and she has been coming down to the drain on a stick and a granddaughter's arm since the first of this month, and nobody sent for her.

Tarin Rusk asked her one question and it was not the question anybody expected and it was not a question about a number.

"What was it called," he said, "when you were eleven."

She did not answer straight away, and the lane was quiet, and the drain was a foot and a half deep and moving at about a hundred and ninety gallons an hour, and she looked at it for a while.

"**The flume,**" she said. "It was the flume then, and the flume went down to the fields and the fields were watered by it and the flume was dry in a summer like this one about one year in four, and it did not matter, because when it was dry you got up the wet common and carried it in pails, and a wet summer it ran and the low field never stood in anything.

"And the drain was the drain. It was the drain on this side and the Nether Sallow Cut on the other side, and that is two words for one thing and they have been two words for about four hundred years and the two villages have never once had a row about it, because you do not have a row with a man about a word when the water is going where it ought to.

"And they were two different things, and everybody knew it, and nobody ever wrote it down." She put her hand on the coping of the drain, which is the only part of this lane she has ever put a hand on.

"**A flume took water off a tank and put it on a field. A drain took water off a field and put it in a channel. That is the whole of what I know and that is all I am going to be asked for, and I am telling you the words and not the reason.**

"And I am going to say one more thing, and then I am going to be asked to go home.

"There is a sentence going round this valley since the fifteenth of the month before this one that says the flume sluice was shut because of a clause. It is a true sentence. It has a clause in it and a year in it. **And it does not say why, and about nine people have been saying it out loud for a fortnight, and not one of them has been asked why, and I have not been asked either, and I am seventy-one and I am not going to be the one who says why either.** If you want a why, you will have to find somebody who was there, and I was eleven, and nobody has asked me anything in forty years except what a word was."

Nobody in that lane thanked her and nobody said she was right, and a boy of fifteen wrote her words on the back of a shutter because there was nothing else to write them on.

"You will be asked in a room," said the boy of fifteen, on the Monday, at the shop. "On the Thursday. In the room, with the nine."

"Then they will not ask me," said Aldis Kerr.

"They will ask you—"

"They will not ask me," said Aldis Kerr, "and I am not going to be a woman of seventy-one who was got out of her own lane by a boy of fifteen and put in front of nine men, because in about four years there will be a note that says a woman of seventy-one was in that room, and it will be said kindly, and it will be the reason nothing was done. **You go and ask them in front of each other. And if one of them asks me, I will come, and I will not be thanked, and I have not been thanked once in this lane in my life and I am not going to start on a Thursday.**"

Nobody thanked her. About nine people in that lane have said since that they wished they had.

---

Nesta Sarg did the plank four times on the Sunday, and the fifth time at about six in the evening, and she did it alone, and she was asked by nobody, and the man from the mountain came down the lane at half past six and stood on the coping and did not offer and was told off.

"Six gallons in one minute and fifty-two seconds," she said, "and six gallons in one minute fifty-three, and the figure is a hundred and ninety, and both trials are on my own shutter and both are on a card at a culvert sixty miles up a road and there is a third rod on that card that says nothing measured and the reason is a padlock, and there is going to be a fifth rod on it after Thursday and I want it written down now, on the back of my hand, so that it is in the same hand as the date and not in somebody else's good one."

"What does the fifth rod say."

"**Fifth rod. The West Flume, third pier. Six inches wide. Not a drain. A channel. Depth read and not measured.** That is what it is going to say, because that is the one thing on that card that has never been in a book anywhere, and the third rod has been empty for six years because of a padlock, and the fourth rod is a drain that has been running since before the assize, and the fifth rod is the one we opened, and about half the men in this valley will say a flume is a drain if you let them, and then somebody in about four years will read that card and not be able to tell which of the two things came out of the mountain and which came out of the ground.

"And I will put the date on it, because the card is a count and a count has a date on it, and a channel that ran for four hours and six minutes on the twenty-second of this month and has been dry and chained and padlocked ever since is the first thing anybody in this basin has been able to measure about the flume in six years, and it is three-quarters of an inch, and it is not a figure anybody should lean on, and the ink on the card says so about the other one."

"Nesta."

"I have said the number and I have not divided it by anything and I am not going to, and there is a thing I want said and then I am going to bed. **Three thousand four hundred and eighty-five gallons is about three quarters of one household's day at a fifth, and the fourth book says four gallons a household a day, and those two figures are about a thousand two hundred times apart, and one of them is a house and one of them is a soil, and if anybody in this valley stands at a gate on Thursday and divides Thursday by a number that has no household in it, I will know, and I will not be able to do anything about it and I am saying it anyway.**"

"You will be at the plank," said Tarin Rusk.

"I will be at the plank at about eleven and I will be at the plank at about six minutes past three and I will write both figures on my own shutter and the man of thirty-eight will time both, and that is all I am doing and I am not going to stand in anybody's way and I have not been thanked once this month and I have said so before anybody asked and I have said it fourteen times."

---

The gate was not touched on the Sunday. A man of thirty-one put a bar into the slot and a can of grease into the groove at about four in the afternoon and took the inspection cover's padlock off and put it on again inside eleven minutes, and the chain was not off the gate at any hour of that day, and nothing was opened and nothing was moved, and the padlock is the reason printed under the third rod of a card and it has not been put on by anybody's order and it is the only reason in this basin that the third rod of that card is empty.

And on the Sunday evening a boy of nineteen sat in the front room of the shop at Brant with a bar across his knees and told him that he had said no.

"Not to you," said Jory Vant. "**To Wick Venn. In the yard, at half past four this afternoon, with two other people standing there.** And I have not told you and I am telling you now, which is not the same as telling you before, and you told me on Saturday that you were not going to take it back, and I am not asking you to take it back, and you are going to have to do a different thing on Thursday night at the third pier."

"You are going to do the pier."

"I am going to do the pier," said Jory Vant, "and I said no to a man of thirty-eight in a yard in front of two people because I had not been asked, and he did not argue with me about it and he has not told you and he told me at half past four that he had worked out who it was going to be by two o'clock and had gone and looked at a gate, and **that is the reason I am saying yes, and it is not a good enough reason and I am going to be careful for four hours and six minutes and I am telling you now so that if it goes wrong there is a record of what I did it for.**"

He put a bar down on the table.

"Write it in the book," he said. "Both halves of it. In your own hand, because I am not signing a book, and put the date on it, and put my name in it, because for once in this valley somebody is going to be allowed to sign something they did not get asked to do and they are going to sign their own reason."
