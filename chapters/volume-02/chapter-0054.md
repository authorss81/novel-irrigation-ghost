# Chapter 0054 — Where It Comes In, And Where It Goes

The pail went under the notch at ten past six on the Sunday morning and it was six gallons and it was the pail with the split in the handle repaired with a wire and a bit of pitch, because it is the only pail in Nether Sallow with a mark on the inside of it, and Nesta Sarg marks pails the way other people mark ledgers.

She put the watch on the coping stone and tapped the pail when it touched the stone and said, "Now," and her mother, who is dead, would have said *now* at the other end of the lane.

They did it four times over two days, because the first two times Jory Vant wrote the figures down and Margit Saye said they were no good, and the reason she gave was that a boy of nineteen writing at dusk in pencil is a boy of nineteen writing at dusk in pencil, and that a number about water that is written down in pencil at dusk is worth about as much as a number about anything else that is.

So the last two were done with a slate and a woman with a good hand, and Nesta Sarg held the pail herself both times.

The first was **one minute and fifty-two seconds.**

The second was **one minute and fifty-three seconds.**

"Six gallons in a hundred and twelve seconds," said Tarin Rusk. "That is a hundred and ninety-three gallons an hour. And a hundred and thirteen seconds is a hundred and ninety-one."

"One second is about two gallons an hour on this drain, and I have had a day of my life when I would have told you it was five," said Nesta Sarg. "And my grandmother used to say that a thing you cannot get to the right gallon in a second is a thing you should not be putting in a book, and I have believed her for forty years and I have watched about nine men in my life put a number in a book after one trial, and eight of them were wrong in the same direction, which is up, because a man is pleased with himself after a first trial and a second trial only ever takes it off him."

"That is the correct way round," said Sef Ordry, "and it is the opposite of the way this trade is done, and the reason it is the opposite is that the way this trade is done puts more work into the basin."

Nineteen households. Forty-three persons. A lane of two hundred yards and a lane beyond it of about a mile, and a cart.

"And this is the other half of it," said Nesta Sarg, "and it is why I have never put your number in my book.

"We take thirty-eight."

"Thirty-eight an hour?"

"Thirty-eight an hour for as long as the cart is there, and the cart is there about an hour and three quarters, and the barrel holds sixty gallons, and then the cart goes down to the Culm common and back up again and the next one starts, and it starts every morning except a Sunday, and there have been three Sundays this month, and in between we have what is in the butt.

"Sixty gallons in an hour and thirty-five minutes. That is thirty-eight. And I have had that figure since I was nine years old and I did not get it from a book, I got it from my mother, and it is right, and it is not a measurement, it is a habit, and the day you put it in a field book is the day somebody takes it off me and gives it to a clerk."

"Then what is in your book," said Tarin Rusk.

"Churns," said Nesta Sarg. "Forty-two churns since the first of the month, thirty gallons each, two a morning, and twenty-one mornings is what we have had this month with three Sundays in it, and I have got the day of every one of them because a churn is a thing you cannot lose without somebody noticing, and it is a better record than yours because it cannot be lost and yours can."

---

Aldis Kerr was seventy-one and she was the oldest person in the lane and she came down to the drain at about nine on the Sunday morning on a stick and a granddaughter's arm and she was not asked to come and she came anyway.

"I want to see the weed," she said.

The weed was thirty yards below the plank, at the bend, in about two inches of the standing flow, and it was a mat of it a yard long and a foot wide, and it was on the south side of the bed.

"That is wrong," said Aldis Kerr.

"It is weed on the side of a bend in a drain," said Sef Ordry. "That is where weed goes. It goes where the water pushes things."

"Yes, and it goes on the side the water comes *from*." She put her stick down into the mat and turned a piece of it over with the point, so that they could all see the roots. "Look at the roots. The roots are on the top of the stone and the stone is under the mat and the mat is on the south side. Water that comes down that lane and turns the bend at the plank goes along the north side of the cut for about a hundred yards, because the cut is straight for a hundred yards and then it bends, and when it bends the water leaves the outside and the outside is the north.

"When the water came down from the fields it came on the north side all the way, and it met this coming up at the bend, and where they met the water stood still in a wet spring for a fortnight at a time and there was a mat of weed in the middle of the flat, not at a side. And the mat went out in the summer. And the water that came up took the whole cut and the water that came down stopped being able to come down, and by the second year there was no water coming down, because there was nothing to come down, because the tap on that flume was shut and had been shut since I was thirty-one."

"How do you know the water came down?" asked Margit Saye.

Aldis Kerr turned round and looked at her.

"Because I was eleven," she said, "and I got my hand in it."

Nobody wrote that down, and afterwards Tarin Rusk thought that it was the only thing said in two days in the Sallow Reach that ought to have been, and that he had not written it because his hand did not have a pen in it, and that a man who has not got a pen has to be careful about what he concludes from a thing nobody wrote down.

---

On the Monday morning they turned the notice over properly, in daylight, at the top of the drain, with four people holding the frame off the post and a fifth reading.

There was writing on the back. There had been writing on the back for six years, in pencil, in a frame that had been on that post for about ninety years.

The first entry was at the top, in a small flat hand with a good nib, and it said a date and a number.

> *2nd day of the month, the year the sluice was shut. 1 hr 51 min.*

The next twenty-two entries were in the same hand and they were the same measurement done on the same day, once a week, and the numbers went down slowly and then in the third year they stopped and started again and the intervals between the entries grew from a week to a fortnight to a month.

The hand changed four times.

The last four entries were in Nesta Sarg's hand and they were within a month of the day they were standing in, and they said:

> *Sun. 1 min 52. Mon. 1 min 53. Sat. 1 min 49 and a half, which I do not believe and have entered anyway.*

"Nobody has ever turned it over," said Nesta Sarg. "It is a notice. It is a thing on a post. And in this lane a thing on a post is for reading and the back of a thing on a post is for a man who wants to make a mark with a knife, and I have read six hundred notices in my life and I have never once turned one over."

"Your grandmother started it," said Tarin Rusk.

"My great-grandmother started it," said Nesta Sarg, "on the card that was in that frame before this one, and the frame took a different card, and the practice stayed, and nobody has ever handed it on except by accident, because there is no notice that says you are to hand it on."

"Then that is the oldest record in this lane and it is on the back of a road notice signed by nobody," said Sef Ordry, "and I have four men who would each give a week's wage to have a copy of it, and I am not going to have one, and here is why."

He put his hand flat on the tin.

"Because the day there is a copy is the day somebody asks where the original is, and the answer is on a post on private ground in a lane that is not on any sheet, in a frame that a road man cut out of a bigger card about ninety years ago, and if I have a copy then I have made a man in a village either hand me the original or produce it in a room. And I have watched that happen four times. You do not walk into a village with a copy. It is the fastest way there is to lose a village and the slowest way there is to lose a fight."

Margit Saye put her hand on the frame as well, on the other side, and said, "Then the reading card at Vellum does not have it either."

"The reading card at Vellum does not have it," said Sef Ordry. "And I want that written down in a book somewhere, and by a man who is not me, because I have been asked twice this year by two men in a counting-house to put a Reach village into a return and I said no twice, and I would like the no to have a witness and I would like the witness to be somebody who is not on a wage."

---

Perdie Ammid's four books were on the table in a cottage in Brant on the Monday afternoon, and there were two other women in the room besides the two of them, and one of them was forty-one and the other was fifty-four, and they had not met.

That took nine minutes and it was nine minutes of nothing happening, and Nesta Sarg did most of it.

She said the lane was nineteen households. She said her grandmother's name. She said the drain was a drain and not a canal and the difference was not a matter of opinion. She said the cart was a cart and the horse was not theirs. And then she stopped and asked Perdie Ammid what a book was for.

"Answering that somebody was wrong," said Perdie Ammid.

"Right," said Nesta Sarg. "And it is not for you, then, because you are not going to be anybody's employer and you are not going to be anybody's office, and if it is a book for answering somebody wrong, it wants to be in a place where the somebody is."

And Perdie Ammid said, "It has been in a chest at Brant for twenty-six years and I have wanted it in a counting-house at Vellum for twenty-six years, and you are the first person in thirty-one years of my life to tell me the thing about it that I have not been able to say to anybody else, and I have been saying it to myself in the dark for a very long time and it never once got better in the dark."

And Nesta Sarg said, "Then do not put it in a counting-house."

"I am not going to."

"You are thinking about it."

"I am thinking about it," said Perdie Ammid, "and I have been thinking about it since a man in a city told me that a record with an owner can be closed, and I have thought about it every day since, and I would like somebody to stop me, and I would like it to be somebody in this room, and I am not going to be the one who asks you to stop me."

---

The splitting of the reach was the third thing the books were for and it took the rest of the afternoon.

It was in 1902. A canal had been called one thing for about four hundred years. A printed schedule of the Sallow Reach works was drawn up in that year, and the reach was given a name in it, and the name was taken off a return, and a clerk in a room in a market town copied the heading forward onto the next page instead of carrying it back, and wrote the new name in the column where the old one had been.

The canal had not changed. The water had not changed. The village had not changed. A word had moved in a book in a room, and it had been given to a different thing.

"Then the canal has got two names," said Tarin Rusk.

"The canal has got two names and one of them is in a drawer in Vellum and one of them is in a book on this table," said Perdie Ammid, "and neither of the men who did it ever saw the other one's work, and both of them were careful, and both of them did exactly what they were told."

"And which one is right?"

"Neither," said Perdie Ammid. "The canal was called *the Long Drain* on this side of the valley and *the Nether Sallow Cut* on the other, and they have been different words for about four hundred years and the two villages have never once in four hundred years had a row about it, because they did not have to have a row about it, because each side was using its own word and both of them knew what it meant.

"The row started in 1903 and it has been going for sixty-eight years, and neither side of it has ever wanted a drop of water out of it. It is a row about a word."

Margit Saye had been writing nothing for two hours and now she said:

"Then how do you write a head on it?"

"You do not," said Perdie Ammid. "That is the whole of what I have learned in sixty-eight years' worth of other people's rows, and it is why I have wanted the two names in one book for twenty-six years, and it is why I have never been able to do it, because the moment they are in one book somebody has to choose which one is the name, and the one I do not choose is the one my neighbour has been calling it since before either of us."

"Somebody said that to me on Sunday morning," said Nesta Sarg. "Aldis Kerr's mother used to say a road written on two maps is a road with a fork in it. That is not a book and it is not a record and it is not a schedule, and it is the only thing about this that I have never had to think about."

And then Perdie Ammid did the thing that made the afternoon, which was to say the four sentences.

"A name is for saying who decides.

"Two names is not two routes. It is one route and one argument.

"An argument can be carried. A route cannot.

"And nobody has to be right. That is the whole of it. I have been trying since I was twenty-eight to make a book in which nobody has to be right, and I did not know until about a year ago that that was what I was trying to do, and I have not been able to do it, and I have told nobody that I have not been able to do it, and there is no reason that you should know either, except that you have both said a thing to me today and I would like somebody in the room to be able to check me later."

The cottage was quiet for a moment.

"And the fifth box," said Nesta Sarg.

"The fifth box is *witness*," said Perdie Ammid. "And I have not filled it in the notice and I am not going to, and you have just told me the day after I told a yard in a city why I would not, and I would like you to say it out loud anyway, in this room, so that there is one person in the basin who has said it and did not say it to me."

Nesta Sarg thought about it for a while.

"A witness," she said, "is not somebody who watched. Every one of us has watched. A witness is somebody who will still be drinking out of it when the argument is finished, and there is exactly one of those in this lane and she is seventy-one and she has told me this morning that the water used to come the other way, and I am not going to ask her, and I am not going to ask her this afternoon, and I am going to ask her in about four years, and that is the number I would put in the box if there was a number to put in it."

"Four years," said Tarin Rusk.

"She is seventy-one," said Nesta Sarg. "I am forty-one. I have a brother on the Kerrow lane who is fifty-eight and who has not spoken to me since a paper changed a name, and I would put four years on the witness box and I would put the box in a book and I would not fill it in until there is a person in it."
