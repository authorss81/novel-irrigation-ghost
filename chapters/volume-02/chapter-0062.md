# Chapter 0062 — The Book That Is Not A Toll Book

The toll bar was half a mile below the shed at the foot of the embankment, and Tarin Rusk walked down it and back on the Friday morning of the fifth day of this month with Sef Ordry and the boy of fifteen, and the other three went down to Culm with the empty carts.

Margit Saye gave him one instruction at the shed door, and it was the whole of what she wanted.

"Ask him questions," she said. "Do not ask him for the book. He has told a wagon full of strangers on the second day of this month that he will not read it out and he will answer if he is asked, and both of those were said in front of me and one of them is a promise and the other is a condition, and a man who says a thing like that in front of a fitter is not going to be badgered about it.

"And one more thing. The head at that gate is seven point three one metres and there has never been a rod on it in my working life, and there is not going to be one on the fourteenth, and I am telling you now so that neither of you spends the next nine days looking for a way to put a lath on a shut gate, because you will both try it, and it cannot be done, and it must not be done on the morning the count is taken."

"And if I get nothing."

"Then you have lost half a mile and a morning," said Margit Saye, "which is the cheapest thing anybody has done in this Reach in eleven days, and I would rather you had it than not, and go on."

---

The hut at the bar is a hut the width of a cart and nine feet deep and it has a stove in it and a bunk and a boy of about nine asleep on the bunk in the middle of the day, and the book is on a shelf above the sill and the box for the farthings is on the sill and the two of them have never been within a foot of each other.

The man was sixty-one. He had been on that road about forty years and he had a dog that did not get up and a way of standing in the doorway that had the effect of being in the road.

"Two days," he said.

"Two days and one day. I was here on the second day."

"You were here on the second day going up. I did not see you going down because you went down in the dark and a man does not count a thing he did not see." He looked at Sef Ordry and then at the boy. "You are the fitter's four and you are the boy. I know all of you and I have said nothing about any of you, and I am not going to start, and that is not a threat, it is a habit."

"I am not here about the road," said Tarin Rusk. "I am here to ask you four questions and I am going to ask them one at a time and you are going to answer or not answer and I am not going to ask you the fifth."

The man looked at him for a while.

"All right," he said. "But I am going to do the thing I did on the second day, first, and then you can ask me your four, and it will take a minute and you will not like it."

He took the book off the shelf and opened it on the sill with his own hand and turned it so that it faced away from Tarin Rusk, and read down a page with his finger going along the lines, and did not make a sound, and shut it, and put it back on the shelf.

"That is the whole of my day and you have heard about four fifths of it," he said.

---

Question one was about an inspection.

"How many times in the six years since the sluice was shut has a man gone up that road with a writ on him."

"None," said the man.

"How do you know a writ on a man."

"Because I have been on this road forty years and I have counted them on three other roads I have stood on," he said. "A man with a writ on him does not get out of a cart at the bar. He gets out of a cart at the bar and he stands in the road and he looks up and down the hill, and he says good morning to me, and then he asks me the name of the works and how far it is and who keeps it. And then he writes it down in a book he has already got out. And I have never once seen one go up this road. Not one. Not in six years, and not in the six years before that, and I would not know the difference in the six years before that either, and I have told you that and I have told you the reason I can tell you."

"And what is the reason."

"I keep a book," he said, and then stopped, and then said, "That is the second half of that answer and you did not ask for it and you are not getting it."

---

Question two was about the tank.

"How many times in six years has anything gone up that road that was not lime."

The man did not have to look at the book for this one and Tarin Rusk understood that he had counted them without the book, and had been counting them for six years, and would have said so if he had been asked.

"Eleven," he said. "Nine of them are the works' own and I know the difference because the works' cart has a bad wheel and it comes up the last quarter mile slow. There is a churn of water on a handcart on the Monday of every week and that is the driver's own and he is not paid for it and he brings it up for the works because they let him have the use of the bar."

"And the other two."

"One of them was eight years ago," said the man, "and it was two men with a rod between them and a boy with a lamp, and they went up and they came down the same day, and they were the only two men I have ever watched go up that road and do anything at a thing instead of going past it."

"And the other one."

"The other one was six years ago," he said, "and it was a man with a chain and a padlock and a card, and he went up in the afternoon and he was not on the bar again for two hours because he had to walk, and he came down at dusk and he did not stop and I did not speak to him and he did not speak to me and I have thought about it since and I would not have spoken to him either."

---

Question three was the one Tarin Rusk had walked half a mile and a morning for.

"What is the last thing you wrote down."

The man looked at him.

"That is not a fair question," he said. "That is the fourth question wearing a hat, and you have told me there are four questions and you have asked me three, and I have answered three."

"It is the third one again," said Tarin Rusk. "You answered the same question twice because you did not know it was the same one. You told me there was a man with a chain and a padlock and a card and a time of day, and you did not tell me the time of day, and a time of day is a number and it is the only number in this conversation that is in a book in a hut and not in a shed or an office or a schedule."

The road man took his hand off the sill.

"Ten minutes past two in the afternoon," he said. "And it was raining a little, the sort that gets into a collar and stops. And he walked. And the padlock was new, because new is the word for a padlock and an old one has rust on it and I have been looking at them for forty years, and I could see it on his arm at ten paces, and I have not told that to anybody in six years, and I am telling it to a man with a bad hand and no office because he asked me the right question and I have been waiting since the second day of this month for somebody to ask me the right question."

---

Question four was the one Tarin Rusk had not thought of and he asked it because the room had gone quiet.

"What is the road for?"

The man did not answer for about four seconds.

"Two things," he said. "I always thought it was two things, a lane to go to the works on, and a place for a man to stop who is not going to the works, and I have been that man twice a week for forty years and nobody has ever asked me why, and the answer is that you cannot get up a hill in this weather without somebody knowing where you stopped, and a man who stops where somebody can see him is a man who is behaving, and there is not enough of that in the world."

"And that is the road," said Tarin Rusk.

"That is the road," said the man. "The road is the bar and the hut and the wheel that is bad and the rain on a Tuesday in a lane nobody uses, and the chain and the padlock and the rod and the two men, and the card, and the fact that a card is a card and not a record and anybody can write what they like on it and it shows about as much as the notch in a stick.

"A road is a road. A *route* is a road with somebody at every end of it who has to be answerable for it, and the somebody has to open it and shut it and look at it and be asked about it, and the looking and the asking are the two things that are not a mechanic, and the two things nobody can leave out, and I have been on this bar forty years and in six years of those forty I have not once seen a man go up this road to look at it or to be asked about it, and there is not a writ on this road, and there is nobody on this road whose job it is to ask."

He stopped, and then he said the last thing, and he said it without any weight at all, which was the only way it could have been said.

"You have all been up there for eleven days, and you have had a shed door and a card and a book with two hundred and thirty-nine lines in it and a chest of books in a village, and I have had a hut and a book and a mile and a half of road, and mine is the only one of the five of them that has got an entry for every single thing that has gone past this bar in six years in it, and nobody has ever sent me a question about anything, and I would like it noted that I would have answered."

---

The boy of fifteen had been standing at the sill the whole time with his hands behind his back, and he had not asked anything, and at the end of it he said the only thing anybody said in the hut that Tarin Rusk did not think of.

He said it to Tarin Rusk, not to the road man.

"You could have brought a book up here," he said, "and copied it, and put it in the book at the shop, and it would have been a copy of a thing in a hut and nobody would ever be able to check it, and you did not, and I do not think that was because you thought of it."

"No," said Tarin Rusk. "It was because a man in a shed told me on the twenty-fourth day of the month before that the day there is a copy is the day somebody asks where the original is, and the answer is a post on private ground in a lane that is not on any sheet, and I heard it."

The boy waited for the rest of it and there was no rest of it.

"Ask him about the water," he said.

Tarin Rusk asked him about the water.

It came up the road once a week in a churn on a handcart, and the road man had boiled it for six years, and there was a jar on the shelf behind him with a white crust about the width of a thumb inside it.

"That is what it does," he said. "You cannot drink it without boiling it and you cannot boil it without that. My girl went to the dispensary at Culm about it and the woman there said that was what the water in her district was doing too and that her district had a name for it, and I have a name for the bar."

---

Margit Saye's letter went up the road on the Friday afternoon on the Bexhill cart, and it was a page and a half, and it went in a Guild envelope because there was nothing else in the shop at Brant, and it said four things.

The first was that nothing had been taken out of the specification book and that she would put her hand to that in front of a magistrate if anybody ever asked.

The second was that a stranger had stood beside two hundred and thirty-nine lines of it for three days and had not asked to copy one, and that she had not told him he could, and that she had not told anybody she had not, and that if her master chose to come and look, she would be glad of it, and that she would rather he did not.

The third was the weep.

It said that the weep on the west shoulder of the service road at the fourth mile above the lime kiln is a break in the crown of a dry-stone conduit and not an overflow, and that the opening is four and a half inches across and there is a second weep eighteen inches above it and two courses open above that, and that the figure is ninety gallons an hour taken twice on the fourth day of this month and the trials are on the card at the culvert, and that there is a lime rind on the lip of it that is the only thing standing between that joint and a face of stone the size of a doorway, and that when the rind goes the face will go, and that the face is the west shoulder of the only road anybody can bring a plate up, and that the head on the joint is the head the Office has had at 7·31 metres since before I came, and that there is no instrument in this basin that can be put on the other side of that number.

The fourth said:

> *I am not going to guess. I have had six years to guess and two men in a counting-house to guess at and I have not and I am not going to now, and if you have it in your own hand then you have it in your own hand and I would rather you wrote it out in full than that you sent it to me in a piece at a time. There is a boy of fifteen on that hill with me whose knees I would not swap and whose name is on no paper, and he will be nineteen in a year and I have taught him the throat and he has taught me a bar of cold chisel at fifteen, and neither of those things is on any list in this basin, and one of them is the only reason there is a figure at all.*

Then, underneath, in a smaller hand and as though it had been added afterwards and not sealed:

> *One more thing and it is the only thing I actually want answered. Is there any instrument in this basin that can be put on the other side of a shut gate in that position. There is one at Ostre and it is a hundred and forty years old and it belongs to a man of eighty-one and Sef Ordry has written to him once and the once was this year. If there is a second one I would like to know before the fifteenth and not after, and I am not asking you to send it. I am asking whether to write to him again or whether to say to him in a village that somebody will write to him again.*

The cart left at two on the Friday and it is a fortnight cart, and it comes down on the second Wednesday after it goes up, which is the seventeenth of this month, and I am telling you the day because the whole of this letter is about days, and because in four years somebody is going to ask when the answer came and the answer is *none*.
