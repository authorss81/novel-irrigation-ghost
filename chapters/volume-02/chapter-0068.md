# Chapter 0068 — Three Rods And A Reason

The count was taken at eight o'clock on the Sunday morning of the fourteenth day of this month, in a cold that had come down with the night, and it took four and a half hours, and one of the three rods had nothing to measure.

**At the tank**, at eight, Tarin Rusk held a staff against the mark and Jory Vant wrote.

Two feet six and a quarter inches, and the same again at a quarter past eight, and Jory Vant wrote it on the tank card in his own hand, and Margit Saye looked at the two figures for a moment and then at the wall.

"That is the mark," she said. "Not a figure off it. The mark. A line cut in a wall with a chisel by a man with no date on it, and it is the highest figure in six years of Sundays and an eighth of an inch above the lowest one, and the whole of the variation for six years is an eighth of an inch, and that is the entire story of that tank and it does not mean a thing. If it were a foot I would be writing a different word. An eighth of an inch in a drought is the water going round a bend."

**At the plank at Nether Sallow**, at eight, Nesta Sarg put the pail under the notch and a woman with a good hand to write, who was not Nesta Sarg, stood on the coping with a slate.

Six gallons in one minute and fifty-two seconds.

Six gallons in one minute and fifty-three seconds.

A hundred and ninety-three and a hundred and ninety-one, and the figure on the card is a hundred and ninety, and the two trials are printed underneath it because Nesta Sarg would not have it any other way and she has not moved an inch on that since the twenty-fourth day of the month before.

"That is the same pair of numbers I gave a man in a yard on the twenty-fourth," she said, when it was read back to her. "The same two, to the second, three weeks on, and I am not surprised and I would not be, and I want it said in this lane that I am not surprised, because a count that comes out different is a count that has to be looked into and a count that comes out the same is a count that is worth more than one that comes out different, and if I had got a different figure this morning I would have gone for it before I put it down and I would not have come to Brant at four o'clock."

**At the flume sluice**, at ten, when Wick Venn had walked a barrow four hundred yards in the cold with a man in it who was not supposed to be there, the padlock was on and the chain was on and the seal was on, and there was nothing whatever to measure.

Tarin Rusk stood in front of it with a lath in his hand for about two minutes and then put the lath down.

"No," he said.

Margit Saye said, "Say the whole of it in the yard this afternoon, and say it now, because I am going to write a card and I am not going to put a figure on it and I am not going to put a word on it either unless you tell me which of those two you want."

"The whole of it is this," he said. "I am not writing zero. Zero means a man measured it and it was not running. It was not running because the gate is shut, and the gate is shut because it has been shut for six years, and it is shut lawfully, and it is chained, and there is a padlock on it and a paper seal on the cover, and the seal is broken at eight o'clock on Monday morning with two men standing at it and the sheet behind it read out first.

"So the number that would be on this card is not zero, and it is not a dash, and it is not nothing, and if I write nothing on a card then in a year a man in a counting-house reads nothing on a card and thinks nobody came.

"Write: *Nothing measured. The gate is shut, chained and sealed, and is not to be opened before the fifteenth day of this month, and is opened on the fifteenth with the seal broken and two men present and the sheet of the sixth year read out first.* And then under it, in a line of its own: *The figure that would stand here is not zero, and it is not the same as zero, and it is on a padlock.*"

Margit Saye wrote it out in full on a slate, and read it back twice, and said, "Then that is what goes on the card, and it is the longest sentence anybody has put on that post in six years, and I have no objection at all to the length of it."

And one more thing was on that card, and it was not measured on the Sunday. Eight hundred and fifty gallons an hour at the stone throat, with the two trials of eight hundred and thirty-three and eight hundred and sixty printed above it, and the printed words *not a figure anybody should lean on*, and the difference of six hundred and sixty an hour against the drain at the plank, and the six hundred and sixty-seven that the same subtraction came to on the twenty-ninth day of the month before with one trial at each end, and the reason the two sums differ. All of it went on that card on the second day of this month and it is on the card this morning and it was not done again this afternoon.

---

There were about four hundred people in the yard at the shop at Brant at four o'clock on the Sunday afternoon.

They were not on a platform. There was no platform, and the front room was open to the street with the boards up, and the people stood in the yard and about a hundred of them stood in the street because the yard took about two hundred and a half and there were four hundred.

Perdie Ammid took the card out of her apron and read it out in the order that had been agreed on the Saturday morning, and she read it slowly, and she did not read it herself to the end.

The tank: two feet six and a quarter inches, twice, against a mark in a wall.

The drain at the plank: a hundred and ninety gallons an hour, with one hundred and ninety-three and one hundred and ninety-one printed underneath it, and the two trials and the two times.

And then the third rod.

And then she stopped, and she did not go on, and she stood in a yard with four hundred people in it and waited until the yard had gone quiet all the way to the street, which took about nine seconds, and then she said:

"The count is short. There are three rods on this card and two of them have a figure on them and the third one has nothing, and nobody has been asked to say that and I am saying it because I asked for the count.

"There is nothing to measure at the flume sluice. There has been nothing to measure at the flume sluice for six years. It is shut and it is chained and it is sealed, and the seal is broken on Monday morning at eight o'clock with two men standing at it, and the sheet behind that seal, which is a record of the last time anybody in this basin ever looked at that gate, is read out loud before the wax is touched.

"So the reason that there is a hole in this card is a padlock. That is the whole of the reason. It is not the drought and it is not the field and it is not a man in a counting-house. It is a lock, and a piece of paper over a slot, and both of them put there by men who were paid to put them there.

"And I want the four hundred people in this yard to understand what a short count is, because there is not one person here who has been in a room where a short count was read out, and I have been in two, and in one of them a man read a figure that was wrong on purpose because the short one was inconvenient, and in the other one a woman read a figure that was right and was not thanked, and I would like this one to be the second kind and not the first."

She turned the card round and put her finger under the last line.

"Anybody may come and look at the tank and the plank and the gate this week. The card is on the post at the culvert, on a nail in this doorway, and in the second book in this village, and the fourth copy goes up on the cart on Friday for a place sixty-one miles east where a boy of twenty-two has been keeping a count by himself for eleven days, and he is not on this card, and he is not in this yard, and he is owed a count, and I have put it in a cart and I have not written to him, because he asked me not to write to him in writing, in front of two people, on the second day of this month."

Nobody in that yard had known that he existed. About nine of the four hundred knew, later, from four hundred other yards.

---

There was a question from a man near the wall about the tank, and it was the question that everybody in the yard had wanted to ask and one man asked, and it was a reasonable question.

"How much is that in gallons an hour."

"Nothing," said Tarin Rusk, and he said it loudly enough that two people at the back heard it, and about four hundred people heard the whole of it.

"I am not going to give you a rate for that tank and I am not going to put one on that card, and I want the reason said in this yard because in eleven years somebody is going to ask it again.

"That tank does not flow. It is being passed through. There is a mountain line into it that has never failed and there is one outlet out of it and that outlet is a six-inch pipe with a joint in the crown of it that is open, and the joint is not a gate and there is no gate in it, and the water goes over the side of the pipe and falls nine feet into a throat. So a level in that tank is not a rate, and a rate is not a level, and if I give you a rate this afternoon then in three weeks you will have a number about a tank in a book in a village and a man in a counting-house will compare it with a plate in a shut gate four days away and one of the two of us will be a fool, and I would rather it was neither.

"And there is a second reason, which is that a rate in that tank would be the most useful number in this Reach and there is nobody in this basin who could check it, including me, and I have spent ten days finding that out at a cost of about a day and a half-mile of road a day, and I would like to say so in a yard once, in daylight, and then not say it again."

Then the man at the back, who had a barrow and one hand and who had not spoken all afternoon, said the thing that the rest of the yard had been thinking about for about ten minutes and that nobody had wanted to put first.

"The field," he said.

That is Wick Venn, and what he said was this, and it took about forty seconds and it is in the second book in his own hand and it is the most useful thing anybody said in that yard on the fourteenth day of this month, and nobody thanked him for it, and he did not want to be thanked for it and said so.

"You have all been talking about Monday as though Monday is a thing that happens to a reach. It is a thing that happens to a man.

"The window is on the fifteenth and the sowing is the ten days from the fifteenth. Those are the same ten days. So a man with nine hundred acres of apple does not go and stand at a flume sluice on the Monday morning of the first week of a planting window, because on the Monday morning of the first week of a planting window he has to be in a field, and if he is not in the field then the sowing is not set that week, and it does not matter how much water comes down the flume on the Monday, because a crop is a thing that is done with a body.

"And I have watched four of you work this out in the last two days and not one of you has said it out loud, and it is the only thing in this yard that I have not been able to stop thinking about, and I have been trying to say it since June, and what I have been trying to say is that in the field from nine in the morning to one, and down the lane from three till dusk, there and back, and a man cannot get water and be somewhere else at the same time.

"That is not an argument against Monday. It is the argument for today. A count is a day and a window is a morning. Every village in this yard can give a morning and not one of them can give a day, and that is why there are six names on a roster and not forty, and that is why I am on a barrow at a flume sluice this morning instead of in a field, and I have been in a field every morning since the second of this month at seven o'clock, and I have lost eight days, and I have told nobody, and I have not told anybody because there was no notice that anybody had to be told."

A man in the second row said, "You should have said that on the Wednesday."

"I should have said it on the Wednesday," said Wick Venn, "and I did not, and I have said so, and it is in this yard and that is the first time it has been in a yard. Do not thank me. I have got a barrow to get back to Brant with before the light goes and I am not going to be standing in a yard being a man who said something."

---

They nailed the card up at the culvert at about ten past six in the evening, in the frame that is ninety years old and was cut out of a much larger card for something else, and the boy of fifteen nailed it because he was on the roster and the roster is the only thing in this Reach that has been kept to, and the frame is a hundred yards from the plank and three hundred and forty yards from the throat and four hundred yards from the flume sluice, and anybody who wanted to stand at any of the three on that card could walk to all three of them inside two hours and would be welcome.

And across the top of it, on a strip of paper an inch and a half wide, because the card was already full, in the hand of the boy of fifteen who does not want to be named, because his grandmother could not write and his mother could not write and nobody in his family has ever written a date down:

> *The fifteenth day of this month. The gate is opened at eight o'clock. Any person may stand at the plank and bring a pail.*

Nobody took it down. It was still on that post on the eighteenth day of the month, with the third rod empty and the reason under it, in a lane of nineteen houses, and the water in the long drain at the bottom of it was a hundred and ninety gallons an hour and had been since before anybody now living, and the count was short, and the reason was printed under the hole, and the padlock was thirty-two miles away on a mountain with a paper seal on it that was going to be broken lawfully on Monday morning by two people, one of whom was a woman of forty-six and one of whom was a boy of nineteen, in front of a copy of a sheet of paper that six years earlier had said *the chain is to be renewed and a keeper named*, and there was still no keeper, and the third rod was still empty, and the count had been printed anyway.
