# Chapter 0067 — Two Miles And Four Chains

The card came back from Culm on the Saturday morning of the thirteenth day of this month with everything on it except the three figures.

It was printed in two colours, which cost the villages four shillings, and it was the size of a sheet of writing paper, and it went on the post at the culvert in a frame, and it went on a nail in the bookshop door at Brant, and the third copy went into the second book on the page with the roster on it, and there was a fourth copy for the man in the blue steps sixty-one miles east because somebody in that lane had been holding a count by herself for eleven days and was not going to be left out of a card.

The order of reading was the only thing left to settle, and it was settled on the Saturday morning in about twenty minutes and it was the hardest thing on the card.

Tarin Rusk wanted the throat first.

"The throat is eight hundred and fifty and it is the number this whole of it has been about since the twenty-ninth day of the month before, and it is the number that is going to be argued about for the next ten years, and if it is read second then the room remembers the first thing it heard and the first thing it heard is a level in a tank."

Perdie Ammid said no, and gave the reason in one sentence, and then gave it again at greater length because she could tell that one sentence was not going to be enough in a room with six people in it.

"Because a level in a tank is the one figure on this card that nobody in this Reach can argue with. It is a line cut in the inside of a wall with a chisel and no date on it, and there is a mark on that wall at two feet six and a quarter inches, and it has been on that wall since before I came, and it is not a figure anybody took. It is a mark. And a mark is the hardest thing in the world to be wrong about, and if it is read first then a room spends the whole afternoon arguing with the drain at the plank, which is the one figure in the set that two people who do not trust each other have both measured.

"Read the tank. Then the plank. Then the rod that has nothing in it, and it has to be last, and it has to be read by a person who is not the man who asked for the count.

"And the throat is not read at all. It is not one of the three rods. It is a number taken on the twenty-ninth day of the month before and on the first of this month with the two trials printed above it and the words under it that it is not a figure anybody should lean on, and it is on this card already, and I will not have a figure read out in this room that was not taken in this room, and if anybody wants that number read in a yard then somebody may go and stand in the throat and put a lath in it, and I have done it twice and it is thirty-four degrees and I would rather somebody else did it the third time."

She then put the order to the room, which is a thing she does, and which nobody has yet thanked her for.

---

Six names were on the roster and three rods were going to be read at eight o'clock on the Sunday morning, and a rod is two people, because a person cannot measure a thing and write it and also be argued with, which Nesta Sarg said in a cottage on the twenty-fifth day of the month before and would not give way on.

Nesta Sarg on the pail at the plank, and Jory Vant on the lath, because he is the only man in the party who has been shown how to hold a watch in both hands.

Bess Ilk on the trough in her own yard, and a fifteen-year-old.

And the tank, and the flume sluice, and Wick Venn on the barrow, and there was one man left, and the man was a man of thirty-three with two live charges who could not vote in any assembly in the Reach.

"There is your problem," said Bess Ilk. "You are the sixth and there are three rods. You cannot be at two of them at eight o'clock and you have known that since the Monday you wrote the roster, and every one of us has known it, and it has been sitting on that page for nine days."

"I can do the tank and the sluice," said Tarin Rusk. "The tank is a level and the sluice has nothing in it. They are a mile and a half apart and there is a road between them and I can walk it in twenty minutes."

"You can walk it in twenty minutes," said Bess Ilk, "and you cannot read a staff and write a figure at the same time, and by the time you have walked it back the tank figure is two hours old and the sluice figure is three, and we are not printing a card on the Sunday with two hours on one end of it and three on the other, and the first person who comes along that road at ten o'clock is going to ask you what you have been doing and you are going to have to say that you are an engineer on a roster, and that is the sentence I would like to keep out of this village if it can be kept out of it."

Tarin Rusk said, "Then I am not on the Sunday."

The room took about four seconds over it and then Wick Venn said the thing that solved it, and he said it the way he said everything, which was as though he were reading a price off a wall.

"Two of the six are the same man," he said. "You. And you can be the tank at eight, and the tank does not want a man on a lath, it wants a man to hold a staff and a man to write, and the sluice has nothing in it at eight o'clock and it can be read at ten when the tank has been read twice. So: tank at eight, sluice at ten, and I take you up to the gate at a quarter past nine in a barrow and walk you back down to the tank for the second reading, and it is four hundred yards and a shed in between, and I have got a barrow, and I would like it noted that this is the only reason anybody has let me come to a count and that I have known that since the second day of this month and have not said so."

"You have not said so," said Perdie Ammid, "because you have been trying to say something else since June and you have not got to it yet."

"I have not got to it yet," said Wick Venn.

---

He went down the flume at seven in the morning with a slate, a stub of chalk, a length of tarred twine, a bucket and no instrument of any kind, and he is fifteen, and he does not want to be named, and the second book says *a fifteen-year-old* in a hand about a hundred and thirty years old on a page that was ruled for something else.

The flume is two miles and four chains from the tank to its outfall, and the first mile of it is on the low side of the service road and then it leaves the road and crosses a field on three stone piers and comes back and runs on down the side of a hedge for about a mile.

He was told not to go past the second mile.

He came back at about ten past four, and he had been out from seven in the morning, which is nine hours and a quarter, and the bucket had the flume's silt in it and his knees to the thigh, and he had eaten two things that Perdie Ammid had put in his hands at the head of it, and he came into the front room of the shop and put the slate on the table and did not sit down.

He read it out, and he read it in the order he had written it, and nobody interrupted him, and about half of it was Margit Saye's four places and the eleven feet of missing invert and the thing about the oak post, and he had written it down in her words, which she noticed and said nothing about.

And then he came to the last four lines, which were his, and which are the only lines in this account that were found by somebody who was not looking for them.

> *A mile and a half from the sluice, on the piers. The launder is straight there and true and there is a plate on the side of it that I could not read because I do not read.*
> *Where it comes off the piers into the field the invert is a foot and a bit higher than it was at the sluice and I do not understand how that can be and I have written it down because I have walked it.*
> *At the sluice I put my hand in the launder with the gate shut and the water came up to here, which is the second line on my wrist, and the stone above that was dry and warm.*
> *At the outfall the water goes off the end of the last stone into the long drain and it is a drop of about four feet and the drain is in gravel and I stood in it up to here and the water was cold and it was moving and there is about two feet of it in the drain and it goes to the plank and past the plank and it does not stop at the plank.*

The room was quiet for a moment.

"The second line on my wrist," said the boy. "I do not know what that means. I have put it down because you have all been writing things down and I did not want to be the only one that did not."

Margit Saye got up off the bench.

"It means the flume's own floor at that gate is nine inches below the mark on the tank wall," she said. "Nine inches. It is the only thing anybody in this basin has measured this month that I have wanted for four years and it was done by a boy with a bucket on a Saturday because I did not send him and he went anyway.

"And he is right and I want to explain it, and it is one sentence, and the sentence is that a flume is not a tank. A tank is a vessel and you look at the water in a vessel. A flume is a hole with a stone bottom in it, and the only level in a flume that means anything is the level of its own bottom, and the flume's own bottom at that gate is nine inches under the tank's mark on the wall, and when the gate comes up on Monday the water is going to run down a stone floor that is nine inches below the level of the water in the tank.

"Nine inches of head on a six-inch launder that is about three-quarters of an inch deep is nothing. Two inches on a smooth one would be enough. Nine inches on a stone one that has not been relined in a hundred and eighty-one years is what a fitter would ask for, and that is the whole of what Monday is, and it is not a favour from anywhere. It is arithmetic and a wet stone and I would like it noticed that we have been frightened of Monday for a week and Monday is a fall of nine inches and a hole in the ground."

---

Perdie Ammid paid him on the Saturday evening, in the room over the shop, in front of nine people, at five shillings the day for two days, out of the villages and not out of any water account, and she entered it in the second book in the column where the wages of a hired man had not been entered since about 1841.

> *A fifteen-year-old of Brant. Two days. Five shillings the day, and a tenth of a shilling the day for the Monday, and his name is not to be written down and it is not to be asked for.*

And Bess Ilk, behind the counter, where the tea is, said that the boy had worked seven hours on a Saturday up five miles in thirty-six degrees with a bucket, and that five shillings was what the notice said and the notice was not wrong, and that he was to have the cup too and was not to be argued with about the cup.

And Tarin Rusk, who had been in this Reach ten days, said nothing at all about any of it, and wrote in the blue book under the heading **Kerrow lane**, which had had one line under it since the twenty-second day of the month before and had by the Saturday got a great many, the one thing he had learned in ten days, which was that the man who finds the number is never the man who can use it, and that the boy of fifteen had gone two miles and four chains in a heat and nine hours to find out where the flume's own floor was, and had not been asked, and had not wanted to be named, and had been paid in a book.
