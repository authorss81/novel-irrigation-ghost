# Chapter 0073 — Four Documents On A Table

On the Monday morning of the twenty-second day of this month a man of thirty-three carried a table into the front room of the shop at Brant and put a chair on each side of it, and the table is the table a card gets nailed to outside, and it took him two trips.

Perdie Ammid had the chest brought out of the back room at about eight, and the chest is a deal box with a hasp and a padlock and twenty-six years in it, and the padlock is the key she has had in her apron since before the war, and she opened it on the table in front of everybody rather than in the back room, on purpose, and said so.

"You have all seen this chest," she said. "I have said in this room that it was a book of customs and not a record and I have said it twenty-six years and I have been right every time and it has cost me something every time and you may all go and look at what is in it."

There were five things in it and Tarin Rusk laid them out in a row, and counted them twice, because the count is the thing he has spent this month on.

**One.** Four field books in four hands, of which the second is the oldest and the first fifty pages of it are not in her hand.

**Two.** The Reach's own copy of the schedule of the works of the Reach, bound, printed in this village in 1902, with the flyleaf, and the amendment on it.

**Three.** The card on a nail in the shed door at the foot of the embankment, which Sef Ordry had brought down in his coat on the Sunday evening and put on the table without a word, having carried four hundred yards of a locked gate's own paperwork in his good coat because a card is not to be carried in a barrow.

**Four.** The Guild's own volume: *Sallow Reach maintenance*, counting-house at Vellum, shelf C, volume 44, bound in half calf, a hundred and eighty-one years old, and eight years old in this village.

And that is four documents, and there is no fifth, and the reason there is no fifth is that there has never been a fifth.

"Three of those have been in this chest," said Perdie Ammid, "and one has been on a nail in a shed at the foot of an embankment since about the eighth year, and none of the four of them has ever known that the other three exist. That was true at seven o'clock this morning. It is not true at about ten past nine. And that is the first thing that has changed in this Reach in twenty-six years and I have not had a vote on it and neither have you."

---

The fourth document was the argument.

"It came down in a cart eight years ago with a return," said Perdie Ammid. "It is not mine and it was not given to me. It came to the shop with a letter asking this village to check a length, and the length was wrong by about a mile, and I checked it with a man and a rule and we sent the answer, and the volume went on lying in my back room because I had nowhere to send it and no way of asking.

"And then four years went by, and then four more. And I have never opened it. I want the room to understand that, because I have said in this room as recently as Monday that a book of customs is not a record, and that is the truth, and it is also a thing I have been using as a reason for not reading a Guild volume that is the only one of the four of these that anybody outside this valley has ever had in their hands. It has been eleven feet from my own bed for eight years. I have been right for eight years about a box and I have been using the rightness of it."

"Then open it," said Tarin Rusk.

"It is open," said Perdie Ammid, "and you are looking at it, and you did not ask me for it and you did not ask Bess Ilk and you did not ask anybody, and I have noticed that in the same way I noticed the other thing on Saturday, and I am going to let it stand."

---

The volume is in a hand of about 1890 and it is very good and it is the only one of the four that anybody has ever had to be responsible for, and about nine pages in, on the right-hand leaf, in two hands, is the entry for 1903.

Tarin Rusk read it out. About twenty people were in the room and eleven of them took it down and Perdie Ammid would not let anybody call it minutes.

> *Kerrow Water. Sallow Head. Works of the Reach. Return for the year 1903.*
> *The long drain from the wet common at Nether Sallow, measured 4 m. 2 ch. by two men and a chain, on the eleventh day of the month, the sluice at its head found shut and sound, the channel below the second mile not walked.* — **hand the first**
> *The Sallow Dyke, from the head of the low fields to the Brasse Fen, 2 m. 4 ch. Land drain. No supply. No plate. Not maintained. Cleared in the winter of — and no entry after that winter.* — **hand the second**

Two hands, and the two men who wrote them are both dead, and the volume does not say which half either of them wrote, and it does not say that there were two, and nobody in 1903 was asked to check the other half because the volume is a volume and a volume is what somebody wrote down.

"Read it again," said Sef Ordry. "Read the second one again, the hand and the number."

> *The Sallow Dyke, from the head of the low fields to the Brasse Fen, 2 m. 4 ch. Land drain. No supply. No plate. Not maintained.*

"Two miles four chains," said Tarin Rusk. "It says two miles four chains."

"And the print on the pin in this room says two miles four chains," said Perdie Ammid, "under item two, written above item one in ink, and item one says four miles two chains with the sluice at its head of the long drain, and the whole of the print is in this village and the shop that printed it is this shop and the family that printed it is behind that counter."

"Yes," said Tarin Rusk. "Both say two miles four chains. And they are about two different channels. That one is a stone flume on the west side of a mountain road, two miles and four chains from the tank to a four-foot drop into a drain. This one is a cut in a clay, from the head of the low fields to the Brasse Fen, two miles and four chains, and it is the main drain of nine hundred acres of the lower field."

"Then the flume and the dyke are the same length."

"The flume and the dyke are the same length, and they are not the same thing, and neither of them knows the other exists, and that is not a coincidence anybody in this room is entitled to explain, because the honest answer is that a figure is a small thing and it goes where it is put."

He took the printed slip off its pin and put it flat on the table and put his finger on item one and then on item two, and did the thing that is the middle of this month.

"Read the numbers out, and then read them the other way round, and say the difference between those two sentences and a correction.

"Four miles two chains. Two miles four chains. They are not two versions of one figure. **They are the same two figures with the places swapped.** A man who has found a length wrong does not put the four and the two the other way round. A man who has found a length wrong writes a new length. A man who is carrying the wrong line down from the line above him copies the figures, because he is not correcting anything, he is continuing, and the figures are in his hand from four lines higher on the same page.

"And it is not a conspiracy. There is no man in this. There is a print shop in this village in 1902 with a compositor and a boy, and a schedule of the works of the Reach to reprint, and item one is a drain four miles two chains long with a sluice at its head, and item two is a supply of some kind that the Reach has never had, and the compositor has carried the figures down, and the thing that has been wrong for a hundred and twenty-four years is not the number. It is the **heading**, and nobody has ever seen the heading because headings are not on pins."

Margit Saye said, "That is what a fitter would say about a plate specification."

"That is what a fitter would say about a plate specification," said Tarin Rusk, "and the specification book at Bexhill is the back of it, and I have stood next to two hundred and thirty-nine lines of it for three days and copied none of them, and the reason there are two hundred and thirty-nine of them is that somebody once found that a plate of a stated bore was not a plate of a stated bore, and wrote it down, and the writing it down is the whole of the company."

---

Then he dated it, in daylight, in front of the room, which is what the room had been brought there for.

"Three dates, and they are on this table, and this is the twenty-second day of this month, and I am going to say them in the order they happened and nobody is to write a speech out of it.

"**Nineteen hundred and two.** A printed list of the works of the Reach is drawn up in this village. A line is struck through in ink and a different line is written above it, in the same year, by a compositor, and the figures go the wrong way round.

"**Nineteen hundred and three.** Two men each carry half a page into the Guild's own volume, and neither of them sees the other's half, and that is not unusual and it is not a fault and there is a reason for it, which is that the second man's half was a land drain and the first man's half was a drain with a sluice at its head, and they are different works and different trades, and a volume is very properly kept by men who do not read each other.

"**The sixth year.** A keeper's name is struck out of the keeper column on a flyleaf in a chest in this village, and *supply district 14, North Sallow* is written in its place, in a counting-house index hand, and it is written on both copies, and it is written lawfully, and nobody in this locality was asked.

"So it is a hundred and twenty-four years old in the method and six years old in the instance. And the method is the same both times. The method is: **a name, an address, and a record, written down twice, by two people, in two places, in two different centuries, in two different hands, for two good reasons.**"

He put his hand flat on the open volume.

"Two good reasons. That is the part nobody in this basin wants. A compositor carrying a line down, and a pair of clerks each doing their own half of a page, are not men doing a thing to anybody. And the result of them is a reach that has been read two ways since 1902, and the two ways do not agree about what the Reach is, and one of the two has never been measured by anybody in a hundred and twenty-four years.

"And I am not going to say what the method is called. There is a word for it, the word has been used in other basins about other things, and I have got no evidence for it in this basin and I have got four days of a planting window, and a man who explains a thing in a room like this is the man who gets thanked for it, and in four years somebody will be sitting where you are sitting."

---

Perdie Ammid opened the fourth book on the Monday afternoon at about four o'clock and put her finger on a line in pencil near the back, in a small hand that was not any hand in the room.

"Seventeen lines," she said. "In pencil. About the Sallow Dyke, and the bend at the second bridge, and where the weed is. And it is dated nothing and signed nothing and it is in a hand about eleven years old, and I have had that book for thirty-one years and I have read it every year of those thirty-one, and I put it there myself when I was about nine, off a woman of seventy-one who showed me the page."

Aldis Kerr was in the room, and had been since about eleven, at the back, and she is seventy-one and she had said she would not be asked.

She was not asked for the box.

The question was put to her by Tarin Rusk, in daylight, with the fourth book open on the table and about twenty people in the room, and it was not the question anybody in this Reach has wanted to put to her for a month, and it was one word.

"Mrs Kerr. The line in the fourth book says the water in the dyke takes the other side of the bend from the weed. Is that still true."

"It is still true," said Aldis Kerr.

"Nobody has asked you about the box and I am not asking you about the box."

"You are not."

"Will you tell us the reason for the weed."

Aldis Kerr looked at about twenty people in a room over a shop for a while, and then she said it, and it took her about ninety words, and nobody thanked her and she did not want to be thanked, and she has never wanted to be thanked for anything.

"Waterweed grows where the water was, and not where it is, and that is the whole of what a weed is and it is why a man in June reads a bend off the weed and is wrong. There is good weed on the inside of that bend at the second bridge and none on the outside, and the water takes the outside, and it has taken the outside since before my mother, and my mother took her hand in it at the bend at eleven and I have a note of that in a different book and no more of me in this than what is written.

"Two things and neither of them is a number. The dyke carries. My mother's people did not call it the dyke. They called it the Sallow, and they called it that because it took the water out and it went west, and everything in the low fields was built to throw water away from it, and a field that throws its water away from a dyke is a field, and a field that throws it into a dyke is a marsh. And that is the fourth thing the word means and it is the oldest of the four and it is the only one of the four nobody in this room has ever said out loud, and I have been waiting sixty years for somebody to ask me about a word instead of about a box and I have not been thanked once and I am not going to be now."

She sat down.

Perdie Ammid wrote the whole of it in the second book, in her own hand, with the date, and the name of the person who said it, and the fact that she was asked a question about a word and not asked for the box, and that she had refused the box in a room of forty-one people on the twenty-seventh day of the month before, that she said *put it in the diary and do not ask me*, that it is in the diary, and that the number that would go in the box is still four years, and that nobody has asked her since and she has not offered.

---

On Tuesday morning the table was still in the room, because the table is the table a card gets nailed to outside and it is a fixture, and four people had slept badly and nobody had gone home.

And the bind was put on the table with the four documents, and it was put there by Perdie Ammid, and it took four minutes, and it is the sentence the whole of this month has been running at.

"You have got a form," she said, "and you have got four documents, and you have got a word in a fitter's memory and a note in my book in a hand of eleven and a girl of seventy-one who will not be asked again, and you have not got a party, and you are not going to get one, and I am going to say why in four sentences and then I am going to go and do my own work.

"The oath says the takers are to be named so that a man who is thirsty can come to a door. The form says the same in print. A door is where a man is. A man is where a party is. A party is a person and not a village, and this Reach is a reach and not a district and never has been one, and supply district 14, North Sallow, is a district in a city sixty-one miles east with no acting commissioner, and a district cannot come to a door because a district has got nine hundred and forty doors in it and nobody at any of them has ever been to this valley.

"And the man it would have to be is you. And you told a room of thirty-four people on the twelfth day of this month, in front of a boy of nineteen, that you cannot be named, and you gave four reasons, and every one of them was a fact about you and not a excuse, and I wrote them all down and they are in this book on a page of their own with the date on it.

"So you have a road with nobody on it. And you have told me once, on Saturday, in a doorway, that you are not going to be able to leave that sheet in your coat, and I have watched you for four days and you have not left the village and you have not sent it and you have not torn it up, and I am fifty-four and I have been watching a man hold a piece of paper for four days and I have run out of ways of saying it that you would enjoy."
