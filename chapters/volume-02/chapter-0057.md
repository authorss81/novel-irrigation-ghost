# Chapter 0057 — A Thumb In A Brass Ring

They opened the intake chamber on the Friday morning at seven and it took four hours and six men, and the six men were Margit Saye, Sef Ordry, Jory Vant, Wick Venn, a boy of fifteen from Brant that Bess Ilk sent because she does that, and Tarin Rusk, who was the sixth and who was there because Sef Ordry had said he would be and would not argue.

The chamber door is a plate of iron in a frame and the frame is on the level of the tank's overflow, and the last person through it had a lamp and a scraper and a boy of fifteen holding one end of a thing, and the door was shut behind them and the water came up through the floor in about four minutes, which nobody remarked on because everybody had expected it.

Nineteen feet deep, by the staff, from the sill to the top of the door.

Silt at **nine feet two inches** above the floor of the chamber. The card in the shed door has said *silt 2 ft 4* at every clearing for six years, and the two feet four inches is the layer in the outlet channel below the sill, which is a different place, and which is the layer that cannot be scraped because the pipe runs in it. The man who wrote the sixth line measured that and not this. This is a hundred and eighty-one years of catchment and there is no line for it anywhere, because it has not been looked at since the sixth year of the standard.

"That is not silt," said Jory Vant, from the bottom, about ten minutes in. "That is about two feet of it on the top and then it changes."

"It changes because it has been a road for a hundred and eighty-one years and the top two feet is what falls in off the road every time it rains, and the seven feet and two inches under it is the catchment," said Margit Saye, "and I have seen it in nine places in this basin and it is always the same and I have never once seen a man look at it twice."

It took four hours because silt does not come out of a chamber with a bucket, it comes out with a bucket and time. A wheelbarrow went up the ladder on a rope with a boy of fifteen on the other end, and the barrow went down empty every time it went up full, and the barrow held about four gallons and there were two hundred and something barrow-loads by the time they had a floor.

At about eleven they had a floor and the plate and Tarin Rusk went down the ladder to the silt and stood on it with a lamp, and the silt took him to the ankle, and it was cold, and it smelled of a wet road.

The ring was in the wall at **eleven feet above the sill**, in a brass plate a foot across, and the plate was in it, and Margit Saye went up to it with a cloth and came back down with it in her hand.

"Half an inch," she said. "Worn at the rim where the float has been riding it. Three-eighths would have had another quarter of an inch of brass on that face and I would not have been able to seat my thumb in it without a rag, and I have had a rag every time for nine years and I have thought about it every time and I have not written it down because I could not prove it and I do not put a number in a book I cannot prove."

She held it out and he took it and it was heavy for its size and cold.

---

He had his thumb in the bore of it before he had decided to, because that is what a man does with a plate.

> **The Gauge:** The water is not under the road. It is being carried under it.

He took his thumb out and put the plate down on the sill and neither of them said anything about it for a while, and neither of them said anything about it afterwards, and it is in this account because it happened and because Jory Vant was on the ladder above them at the time and heard it said in the voice Tarin Rusk used when he was reading a level off a staff.

"That is not under the road," Jory Vant said, after a moment, from above. "Say that again, because I want to write it down and I want to know if it is a measurement or a joke."

"It is neither," said Tarin Rusk. "It is a thing I have just been told by a piece of brass and it is the fourth time in two months and I have not been told anything by a piece of paper in my life and I am not going to start. Do not write it down."

"I am going to write it down," said Jory Vant, "in my own book, and I am not going to show anybody, and you cannot stop me and you could have said it to a different man and I would not have heard it."

---

The branch was found at half past two in the afternoon by Jory Vant with a scraper, and it was found because Margit Saye told him where to look, which was not by looking.

"Every one of these chambers has got a low-level pipe in it," she said, "because the maker always put one in, to empty the chamber, and he put it in at the bottom of the wall in the corner where the floor slopes. Nobody has ever used one in nine years because they silt up in a fortnight and after that nobody can find them again. Get your scraper into that corner and go along the wall at about a foot above the floor."

The floor of the chamber is not the floor. It is nine feet of it, and Jory Vant went into the corner of the wall with a scraper at about a foot above the real floor, at the depth a man can stand at with his head four inches under the water.

"I have got iron," he said. "I have got iron and it is round and it is about the size of my wrist and it is a pipe and it is going into the wall and I cannot see where it goes."

"How big."

"About that." He held his two hands about seven inches apart. "Six inches, maybe. It is full of stuff. It is not full of silt, it is full of a sort of gravel that has set like a mortar, which is the stuff that comes out of a pipe joint when the joint is old and the water has been going in and out of it for a hundred years."

Margit Saye went down the ladder two rungs and looked.

"Clear it," she said. "Two feet. Do not clear more than two feet, and if it starts to move on its own, put the scraper down and get up the ladder, and I will not have anybody tell me afterwards that they thought about it."

They cleared it for fifty minutes with a bar and a bucket and two men taking turns, and at about ten past three the water came out of the crown of the pipe.

It did not come out of the mouth. It came out of the **top** of the pipe, out of a joint on the crown, in a thread about the width of two fingers, and it ran along the top of the pipe away from them, up the slope of nine degrees that the pipe had been laid at for a hundred and eighty-one years, and went about eleven feet along the crown of it, and then came off the end of the silt in the corner, and dropped the foot and a half back onto the floor of the chamber, and made a hole in the floor, and ran out through the hole and away under the door.

The whole chamber heard it.

"That," said Margit Saye, "is why a man with a rod at the low end of that culvert finds nothing. The water is not coming along the channel. The water is coming out of the top of it and going over the side of it. A rod is the worst instrument in this trade for finding a thing that is doing that, and I have used one about four hundred times and I have never once put it against the top of a pipe, and there is a rule about pipes and I did not know it."

"It has been doing that for how long," said Sef Ordry.

"It has been doing that for however long that joint has been open," said Margit Saye, "and the joint has been under about nine feet of water for about a hundred years, and water finds the top of a pipe before it finds the end of one, and I would not be surprised if it has been doing it since before the flume was shut, and I would not put that in a book, and I have said it out loud so that it is in somebody's ear and not only mine."

---

Tarin Rusk put his hand on the pipe.

The thread was cold and it was moving and it went over the side of a six-inch pipe that was full of a hundred-and-eighty-one-year-old gravel mortar and had about nine feet of head on it, and it went uphill for eleven feet because the pipe went uphill, and it fell about nine feet into a hole in a floor, and somewhere on the other side of a wall and nine feet of road it came out of a stone throat into a plank with a notch cut in it and a pail under the notch.

He took his hand off and he said, out loud, to five people, at about ten past four in the afternoon, in a chamber with a lamp in it:

"I am not going to open it."

Somebody said something. It was Wick Venn.

"Listen to me, and then you can go up the ladder and you can all say whatever you like about it in the shed, and I will not be in the room and I will not answer it. I am not going to open it because there is nobody standing next to me who has been asked. That is the whole of the reason and it is the reason I gave in a shed on the Tuesday in front of a woman of forty-six, and it is not a better reason today because I have a lamp and a body of water in front of me.

"And I would like it written down that I put my hand on it and did not open it, and I would like Sef Ordry to write it, and I would like it written that Jory Vant was in the chamber and not on the ladder, and I would like the date on it, and I would like it to say that the pipe was open on the crown and that I did not open it and that I do not know what happens if anybody does."

"You cannot write that," said Sef Ordry. "There is no form for it."

"Then write it on the card," said Tarin Rusk, "on the back of the card in the shed door, under the seventh, and put my name and yours and the boy's, and if anybody comes in here in ten years and wants to know why a thing in this basin was left alone, they will turn the card over, because that is what people do with cards, and the whole of this trade is people turning cards over."

Sef Ordry looked at him for a while and then said, "That is a filthy thing to do to a card and it is the right thing to do to a card, and I will do it, and I am going to do it in my own hand and not yours, because I have four men whose names I would rather not have on a thing in a shed."

---

The stone throat on the west side of the embankment is nine feet below the crown of the road and four feet above the long drain, and it is a slot in the end of the rising main's outfall wall about the width of a hand, and Margit Saye went down to it on the Friday evening with a tape and a length of lath cut to a point and a watch, and Wick Venn held the watch because Wick Venn is the only man in the party who can be trusted with a watch.

The slot is **two inches wide and an inch and a half deep** at the lip, and the lath went twenty feet in **thirteen seconds**.

"Twenty feet in thirteen seconds," said Margit Saye, and wrote it on a plank with a bit of pencil, and then did the sum in front of everybody, slowly, twice, and made Jory Vant do it a third time on the back of his own hand with a nail.

"A cubic foot is seven and a half gallons in this basin and anybody who says otherwise is on a schedule that was made in a wetter year.

"Two inches by an inch and a half is three square inches. Three square inches is a forty-eighth of a square foot, and I am going to leave it as a forty-eighth, because a man of nineteen cannot divide by three and come out with a foot, and I have watched him try.

"Twenty feet in thirteen seconds is one and a half five hundred and thirty-eight thousandths of a foot a second. Divide that by a forty-eighth and you get a shade under a thirty-first of a cubic foot a second, and a shade under is what we are, and I have said under twice because a man who says over once is not doing the sum.

"A thirty-first of a cubic foot a second, and a cubic foot is seven and a half gallons in this basin, is about fourteen and a half gallons a minute and about eight hundred and seventy gallons an hour. A shade under a thirty-first is eight hundred and sixty-five gallons an hour. That is a hundred and sixty-five, and a card that has to be true in a shed for ten years is written in tens, and that is the figure, and I have not got to announce it. That is a cubic foot a second, roughly, for every thirty-one seconds, and I have known men who would have said nine hundred on that with a straight face and been four per cent out and never found out."

"Wrong," said Jory Vant.

"It is wrong because a lath in that slot is a very bad instrument and it is the only one we have, and because the lath ran slow at the lip on the last two feet, where the slot is a shade narrower, and because a man in a hurry takes his section at the lip and not in the throat. I have taken the benefit of both of those, because both of them are the way a man measures when he is in a hurry, and if I had taken it the other way I should be reporting a thousand and forty gallons an hour out of a slot you could put a finger in, and there is a very great deal of room between a true figure and a useful one." She scratched it out and wrote under it.

"There. **Eight hundred and sixty gallons an hour.** And I would put that in a book if I had a book and a different job."

Eight hundred and sixty gallons an hour.

A hundred and ninety and three was what came under the plank at Nether Sallow, at one plank, on a good day, with a pail and a watch and two women.

The number went round the lane that evening the way numbers do. Somebody said it was three times as much. Somebody said the culvert was a mile off. Tarin Rusk took a bit of paper out of the bench book and wrote both figures on it and, underneath them, one line, and read it out to nobody in particular at the top of the embankment in the dark.

"The channel is two feet two inches at the top and the water in it is an inch and a half, and the bed under this lane is gravel and has been gravel for four hundred years, and the Reach's water table in a dry summer is about a foot below the invert at Nether Sallow.

"So eight hundred and sixty comes out of a stone throat three hundred and forty yards above that plank, and a hundred and ninety and three goes past the plank, and the difference is six hundred and sixty-seven gallons an hour, on one trial at each end, and the difference has gone into the ground under a lane.

"It has not been lost. I want that said, and I want it said carefully, because it is not lost and that is not the same as it is safe, and it is not lost and it is not found, and the ground under that lane has been quietly swallowing a rising main for six years and nobody has ever once been asked where it comes out.

"And I am not going to open that pipe, and I am not going to be the man who finds out."

And on the back of the card in the shed door, on the Friday evening, in a sluicewright's hand, under a line that said SEVENTH and stopped there eleven years ago, there is a second line, and it says:

> *Seventh, and after. Pipe found open on the crown, in the corner, below the floor, by S. Ordry, J. Vant and M. Saye, on the twenty-ninth day of this month. The crown joint is weeping. It was not opened by us and it is not to be opened by anybody in my company without a man with a witness standing at the ladder head. I have written it here because there is nowhere else to write it and because the seventh has been empty for eleven years and an empty line reads like a clean chamber.*
> *The Rusk has put his hand on it and has not opened it. Enter that, and the date, and let nobody take it out of this card.*
