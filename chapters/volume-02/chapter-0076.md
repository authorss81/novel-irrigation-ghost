# Chapter 0076 — The Fifth Line

The count is read on the last Saturday in the month, and the twenty-seventh day of this month is the last Saturday, and the four of them went to the shop at Brant at about eight in the morning instead of to the three rods.

That is the whole of the outside of it and it is not nothing. The roster said eight. Perdie Ammid had ruled the Saturday off in the second book on the Friday morning without asking anybody, in the way she rules things, and the tank card and the plank card and the empty third rod and the reason under the empty third rod were to have been gone over at the tank at eight, and the tank and the plank are three hundred and forty yards apart and the flume sluice is four hundred yards from the tank, and a barrow and two pails and a lath and a watch will do it in four and a half hours as it was done on the fourteenth.

Nobody in the front room of the shop at Brant said the word *count* for about three and a half hours.

---

What happened, and it took about nine minutes, and there were about fourteen people in the room and two of them had not come to look at anything.

Tarin Rusk took the sheet out of the inside pocket of a coat at about a quarter past eleven and unfolded it, because it had been folded twice since the Thursday of the eighteenth and the creases had gone soft in a pocket, and he put it on the table face up, and the four lines were in his own hand and Jory Vant had a clean copy of the four in his own hand on a separate sheet, done on the Friday afternoon at about one o'clock, correctly, in about nine minutes.

The fifth line had a rule under it and nothing above it.

"All right," he said. "I am going to do it now and I am going to say the names out loud as I write them, because my hand is not what it was and there is a boy in this room who has to know what is being written, and that is the whole of the reason and it is not the real reason."

He wrote the first one.

"Sef Ordry, sluicewright, of Bexhill, of no district, entered by me as a taker of the water of the Sallow Reach on the day of this return."

He wrote the second one.

"Jory Vant, apprentice, of Bexhill, of no district, entered by me as a taker of the water of the Sallow Reach on the day of this return."

He wrote the third one.

"Wick Venn, of this Reach, of no district, entered by me as a taker of the water of the Sallow Reach on the day of this return."

He put the pen down and looked at the room and said, out loud and in the ordinary voice, in the order in which he had written them.

"Sef Ordry. Jory Vant. Wick Venn."

Nobody said anything. Jory Vant said, about four seconds later:

"That is three."

"It is three," said Tarin Rusk.

"There is a fourth line."

"There is a fourth line," said Tarin Rusk, "and there is no name to go in it, and a return with an unfilled line in it is not a return, and the small type on that sheet says that a return subscribed by a person without lawful authority in the matter is nothing and is worse than nothing because it may be read, and I have had this sheet in the inside pocket of a coat since the Thursday of the eighteenth, and I have not taken it out in front of one human being since the Thursday of the eighteenth day of this month at about eleven o'clock in the morning, and I was going to tell the four of you at eight o'clock this morning, and it is a quarter past eleven, and I did not come at eight."

---

Sef Ordry put both hands on the bench. He is thirty-one and he has a bad way of not moving.

"I am going to ask you four questions," he said, "and I am going to ask them in order, and I have never in nine years in a shed asked anybody four questions in order, and I asked them in a yard in July of the year before last and I shall want that entered, and I would like them put down."

"Put them down," said Perdie Ammid, and put her hand flat on the book.

"How long have you had that sheet in your coat."

"The Thursday of the eighteenth. About eleven in the morning."

"And the three names."

"Since the same Thursday. About eleven in the morning. I did not know them on the Wednesday and I had them by about half past nine on the Thursday and I wrote the four lines out between ten and eleven and I read the whole of it out twice in this room with nine people in it and I did not say the fifth line was empty because a form with an empty fifth line is a form that has not been made out, and a form that has not been made out cannot be anybody's fault."

"You had Margit Saye in this room on the Thursday," said Sef Ordry.

"I had Margit Saye in this room on the Thursday and the Friday and the Saturday and the Sunday and the Monday and the Tuesday and she went home on a cart at ten o'clock this morning, and she read the fifth line out of a bundle of forty forms in front of nine people last Saturday, and she told me to ask you about it before anybody signed anything, and I did not ask you, and she told me to and I did not, and that is a thing I have to hold and not a thing you have."

"She did tell you to," said Sef Ordry.

"She did."

"Then the fourth question," said Sef Ordry, "and it is the only one of the four that matters and I am not going to get angry in a room with fourteen people in it, and I want it noticed that I have decided that in advance, because if I get angry in this room then in about eleven years there is a story about a man of thirty-three who lost his temper over a piece of paper in a shop, and the story will be better than the return, and I have watched that happen to a shed in another county and it took the shed eleven years to be a shed you could work in again.

"**Why did you not ask us.**"

And Tarin Rusk answered, and it took him about four minutes, and it was the right answer, and it was not a good enough one, and he said the second half of that himself before anybody else could.

"Because on the Friday morning of the nineteenth I sat in this room and read out four lines and read the whole of the sheet out twice, and Mrs Sarg read out in this room on the Saturday the one line on that form that was not about the water, and she told me to ask you before anybody signed anything, and I did not ask you. And I did not ask you because I had known the answer since about half past nine on the Thursday morning, and the answer was no.

"You would have said no. You said no to a witness box in a room of thirty-four people on the twelfth day of this month with a woman of forty-six standing behind you explaining why, and you were right, and the reason she gave was a debt for the rest of a life, and a name on a printed form about a door at a flume is the same debt with a rule attached to it instead of a box attached to it, and I have heard that argument in this room from a better person than me and I could not have answered it and I could not have gone round it.

"And if you had said no I should have had a form with an empty line in it, which is nothing, and I should have had it in a coat for a week with nobody in the room able to say a word about it, and by the twenty-fourth I should have sent it up the road with a line in it that somebody could not be named and had been entered by me without being asked, which is exactly the thing the small type warns about, and worse than nothing, because it may be read.

"So I did not ask, and I am telling you that I knew at eight o'clock this morning that I was not going to ask, and that is the whole of it, and it is not a reason. It is a reason with the end taken off it. If I had been asked on Friday afternoon what I was going to do I should have said *ask them on Monday morning over a table with the form on it and let them write their own names or not write them at all*, and I should have believed it, and I should have come at eight o'clock this morning instead of a quarter past eleven, and I did not, and the reason I did not is that a man who has got a thing in his pocket and a week in his hand is a different man from the one who made it on Thursday, and I have known that about myself for fourteen years and I have never once written it down."

---

"There is a line on that form," said Sef Ordry, "that says a man who is thirsty comes to the door of the man who has the water."

"Yes."

"Whose door."

Tarin Rusk put the pen down again.

"Sef Ordry, of Bexhill, who does not live in this Reach and has never voted in it. Jory Vant, of Bexhill, who is nineteen and has never been outside the foot of a service road. Wick Venn, of this Reach, who is the only one of the three of us who is a person in the sense that the form means and who is the only one of the three of us who has a house a man could be sent to.

"And the fourth is a boy of fifteen of Brant, who has earned eleven days at five shillings a day out of a wages column in a book and who is not named and is not to be asked, which is in writing in a column in a book in this village in a hand a fitter does not own.

"That is the whole of the fourth line and there is no name in it and there cannot be one, and that is why the sheet has been in a coat for a week."

Jory Vant said, from the wall, in a voice nobody expected:

"You said that in a shed on the eleventh."

"I did."

"You said the takers and you said the reasons. You said a man of thirty-three with no office and no district and eleven pence and two live charges and a name in four buildings. You said a man of thirty-eight with one hand. You said a woman of forty-six from Bexhill who was here on four days' notice. And you said a boy of fifteen on a roster who is not named."

"Yes," said Tarin Rusk. "In that order."

"You had them ready," said Jory Vant. "On the eighteenth you had them ready. You had them ready in a room with thirty-four people in it nine days before and you have just written them on a form."

"Yes," said Tarin Rusk.

"Then you did not find them on Thursday," said Jory Vant. "You found them in this room on the twelfth, and you wrote them down that night, and you have been carrying them about for nine days."

---

"I gave him a reason at four o'clock yesterday afternoon," said Sef Ordry, "in that yard, and he asked me to say it again at eight o'clock this morning and I said no, and I was right to say no, and I want the second question put in the book as well: **Sef Ordry was right to say no on Friday night**, and it is the only thing in this whole business that I have been right about, and I have not been thanked for it and I am not going to be, and I would like it written down that I have been right about exactly one thing and I have said so out loud on the first available morning.

"And I am not resigning off a rope. I am not putting the bar down. I am going to be at a gate bed on Tuesday and he is going to hold the other end of it, and I would prefer it that way, and if either of us ever decides he is not helping, then one of us says so and the other one stops, and there is no argument in a shed about it, and that is how it is done.

"And I have one thing to ask for and it is not the sheet, because the sheet is his.

"When it is all finished — and it is not finished, and the window shut yesterday — somebody is to write down that I did not ask, in my own hand, and that I was right not to ask, and that I said the thing out loud on Friday afternoon to a man who asked me to keep it, and that I have had a day of it and I have not had a week, and a rule that lasts a day is worth nothing and a habit that lasts a day is worth nothing either, and if a man of thirty-three wants to write down what a habit is worth he can come and watch me on Monday at eight o'clock for as long as he likes."

---

The sheet was torn up at about four o'clock in the afternoon, in the front room of the shop at Brant, in front of Perdie Ammid and Bess Ilk and Jory Vant, and the four lines that Jory Vant had copied in his own hand on the Friday afternoon stayed where they were, in the second book, under the returns, with a signature at the foot of them.

And that was the part that could not be got out again, and Perdie Ammid said so and nobody argued with her.

"I have kept four books for thirty-one years and wanted them to be a record for twenty-six," she said, "and a man of thirty-three has put four lines into the second one of them that he has admitted in front of fourteen people were a part of a return, and a return that was not a return and is not a return and cannot ever be a return, and the four lines are in there and they cannot be got out and I would not take them out if I could, because they are true.

"**Four true lines about four different reaches**, in a book in this village, under a boy's signature saying he copied four of the five and was not asked to copy the fifth. And the fifth line is not in the book. And the sheet is in pieces in this stove and there is no return in this basin and there is no party and there is no name at any door, and the whole of what a piece of paper has done in this Reach in a month is that it has said one true thing and had it taken away again in the same room."

---

At about two o'clock in the afternoon on the same Saturday, a mile and a half down the Kerrow lane, in a barrow, at the flume, Wick Venn was told by a boy of fifteen who had been sent to the smithy for a nib at about half past eleven and who had come back at half past two.

The boy said three names and then said that there was a fourth line and that there was no name in it.

Wick Venn put the barrow down, and looked at about eleven feet of a stone launder with the gate shut and chained on it and nothing whatever in it, and said:

"Then the count was not taken today."

"It was the last Saturday," said the boy. "Perdie ruled it off on Friday morning in the book."

"Yes it was," said Wick Venn.

He did not say anything else about it, and he did not go up to Brant, and about nine hundred acres of apple in nine hundred acres of field were three days past the day the ground went like a bench, and a man of thirty-eight with one hand and a barrow was the only person in the Sallow Reach who could get anything anywhere, and he had been in a barrow instead of in a field for about five and a half hours on the last Saturday in the month, and he had told nobody on the Friday and he told nobody on the Saturday either, and there was no notice that anybody had to be told.
