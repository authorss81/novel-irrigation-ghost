# Chapter 0081 — A Card And A Return

The ration review of the lower terrace sat on the first day of next month at nine in the morning in a room over the terrace road, and it is a standing body and it moves, and on the sixteenth day of the month before it set a cut of a fifth on everything it serves and wrote a ruled line under the schedule saying that the city does not know where a bounded supply came from.

There were about twenty-two people in the room and two of them had not come to sit.

Tobias Cray, thirty-four, in the post four years, had the bundle of returns on the table because it was his bundle and he carried it up the stairs himself, and in it were the returns of supply district fourteen, North Sallow, for six years, quarterly, twenty-three of them, and he had never had a reason to open that particular one and he opened it at about twenty past nine in the morning on the first of next month and found a line in it that he read four times.

"The source of supply," he said. "That is what this column says. Supply district fourteen, North Sallow. Source: **the works at Sallow Head.** In twenty-three returns, six years, quarterly, and the source has never once changed and I have never once asked."

Alderman Tarrant, who had set the fifth himself and had said out loud twice that he would rather not, said, "Read it out."

Cray read it out. It took him about eleven seconds and then he read the line under it, which was a printed line and not the district's, and which said that the water is taken off the works at Sallow Head under the Kerrow Water and is credited to the district in accordance with the schedule, and that any person having the care of that work may be asked at the works.

"May be asked at the works," said Alderman Tarrant.

"That is what it says."

---

Then the second document came into the room, and it came in because of a boy.

Niko Venn was twenty-two and had been keeping a count since the first day of the month before by himself for no wage, and he could not read, and on the morning of the first of next month he was in this room because the district's own return for the week was on the table and his count was the only count of that district anybody had, and the count was four weeks old and in chalk on the back of a shutter and was going to be gone by the weather.

He had the fourth copy of a card in his coat. It had come up the mountain road in a cart on the Friday the nineteenth of the month before under a board so that it would stay dry, and the driver had said that a card with a hole in it was the only honest thing he had carried that year, and Niko Venn had not told anybody at the bar what the driver had said, and he had carried it for thirteen days and had not known what to do with it and had not been asked.

Tobias Cray asked him for it. That is the whole of how it came in, and Cray has said since that it was the ordinary course of a morning and that he would have asked anybody.

It is a card in two colours about the size of a man's hand, printed, with three rods on it and two figures and a hole, and a strip of paper an inch and a half wide along the foot, and a date. It was cut by a boy of fifteen in a village four miles from a mountain works and it is nailed to a post at a culvert where anybody may look at it.

Cray read it out, and the room heard it.

> *Tank: two feet six and a quarter inches, twice, against a mark cut in the wall. The mark is the highest figure in six years of Sundays and the whole of six years' variation is an eighth of an inch.*
> *Plank: one hundred and ninety-three gallons an hour, and one hundred and ninety-one, off six gallons in one minute fifty-two seconds and in one minute fifty-three seconds, figure one hundred and ninety. Both trials printed.*
> *Flume sluice: nothing measured. Zero is refused in print. The reason is under this line and the reason is a padlock.*

Nobody said anything for about four seconds.

"A padlock," said Oriel Fenn, who had been a minute clerk for nineteen years and had come out of habit and was not on the review at all.

"A padlock is not a reason," said Cray.

"It is the reason printed under the line," said Fenn. "I have read it twice. It is not a reason in the sense of a cause. It is a reason in the sense of an obstruction. Read the next line."

"There is no next line. It is a strip of paper and it is a date and a sentence."

"Then read the sentence."

> *Brant is a party to the act of the fifteenth. Brant did not ask for the water. Brant was asked and signed and is a document. — P. Ammid.*

---

What happened in the next forty minutes is a paper collision, and both parties are behaving correctly.

So the review had a return saying, in twenty-three quarters of six years, that nine hundred and forty properties in the north of this city take their water off works called Sallow Head, in a valley sixty-one miles west, on a schedule they had never seen. And it had a card, printed in that valley by a boy of fifteen and nailed to a post at a culvert where anybody may read it, saying that the mark in the wall of the tank has not moved an eighth of an inch in six years, and that a stone throat there passes eight hundred and fifty gallons an hour into a drain, and that the flume sluice has been shut and chained for six years with a padlock on the inspection cover of it.

Both are true. Cray established that in about nine minutes by asking four questions, three of them answered by a return already in the bundle and one by a card, and no single person in the room knew about both documents until the boy put his coat on the table.

And then Alderman Tarrant did the only thing a chair can do, which is to read two documents into one record and ask whether they can both be true.

They can both be true. That was the finding, and it took about six minutes, and the room did not like it.

"A tank that has not moved an eighth of an inch in six years is a tank that is being passed through and not one that is being emptied," he said. "A throat that passes eight hundred and fifty gallons an hour into a drain is a discharge and not a supply, and a shut sluice with a padlock on it is a work that is out of service, and a work can be out of service and still be the source of a district's water for six years, because the water leaves the tank by a road nobody has a return for, and I have had a case of that in this city for six weeks and it is in four buildings and it is not about anybody's villainy.

"**So we have a district supplied off a works, and a works that supplies nobody, and both of those are in this bundle and neither of them is a lie.** And I am going to say the thing that matters and I am going to say it once and I would like it read back. We may not cut that district on this return, because the return's source is not accounted for. And we may not raise it either, for the same reason and with more reason, because if you may raise a ration on a source you cannot account for, then you may cut it on one, and there are nine hundred and forty properties in that district and about half of them have never had a full one.

"That is a chair's ruling and it is the whole of what I have got, and I have not got a seal, because the office of this city has had no acting commissioner since the sixteenth day of the month before and there is a determination in a book and nothing to put a seal on. So it goes in as a paragraph under the schedule and it goes in the book with the date on it and a man will read it in about four years with no idea what I was frightened of."

---

Then Oriel Fenn read out a line, and it is the reason anybody in that city now knows a valley exists.

"There is a line at the foot of the form," he said. "Under the columns. Smaller type. It is the form's and not the district's, and it is on every quarterly return of that district I have ever handled, and it has been empty on all twenty-three of them, and I have never once asked for it, and I am asking for it now because the clerk has read out a card out of a valley and I want to know what our own forms have been saying."

> *Takers, named, so that a man who is thirsty may come to the door of the man who has the water.*

The room had about twenty-two people in it and it went very quiet in the way a room does when everybody in it has just understood the same thing at the same moment and nobody is going to be the first to say it.

"That is the form's line," said Cray, in the voice of a man reading a printed line on a form. "That is not a district's line. That is the company's line and it has been on the form since before I was in the post and it has been empty on every return of that district for six years, and the reason it is empty is that it wants nine hundred and forty names and nobody has ever asked nine hundred and forty people anything."

"Read the line again," said Alderman Tarrant.

Cray read it again.

"Then there is no door in that district," said Tarrant.

"There is a pump," said Cray. "It is on a list, in a street with houses on it, and there is no keeper and there has not been one since before I came, and the key is in a box and the box is opened on a Thursday by a man who is not paid for it."

"Then that is the whole of what the form has been asking for for six years," said Tarrant, "and the answer is that the answer is a name, and I am not going to put a name to a form in a room of twenty-two people at half past eleven in the morning, and the reason I am not going to is not a chair's reason. It is that a name on that line is a debt for the rest of a life, and the reason I know that is that a man said it to me in a yard in this city in the spring and he was right."

And a boy of twenty-two at the back, who could not read and had not understood a word of it, said:

"There's a door."

---

Nine of the twenty-two looked at him and he said it again, standing up, the way a boy of twenty-two says a thing in a room when he has been the only one in it who has ever stood at a door with a pail in his hand.

"There's a door at the low end of the blue steps," he said. "There's about a hundred and twenty-six people in it and the office has got no row number for us and it is inside district fourteen, it is inside the district, it's under the district on the sheet, and it has got no row, and every one of those nine hundred and forty properties has got a row and a door and a name against it, and we haven't, and we have got a cistern with six thousand seven hundred gallons in it and it has been at the coping since the twentieth of the month before last and it is the same water that has been coming out of that mountain since the seventh of the month before that, and I know it is the same water because a physician stood in a yard in this city and said so in front of a chair of this Assembly and a clerk wrote it down and read it back four times, and I was there, and I am not a witness and I am not going to be one, and I would like the row number left empty."

Nobody spoke for a while.

"What is your name," said Cray.

"Niko Venn. And it is not to go in the takers line, because I have no water and the line wants the man who has it, and I have a pail and a piece of chalk and four pebbles in a tin and I have been doing a count by myself since the first of the month before and nobody has paid me and nobody has asked me to stop, and that is the whole of what I have got and it is not a supply."

---

What Niko Venn got, in the end, was not a row number and was not the takers line, and it took about four minutes and it was against Cray's advice and over Cray's objection and into the return anyway, at the direction of a chair, and Oriel Fenn read it back twice.

It was his count. Four weeks of it, in chalk, on the back of a shutter, in a lane that is on no map in this city: two figures a morning for every morning since the first of the month before, and the figures are the depth of the water in a yard cistern and the day of the week, and a boy who cannot read has had somebody read them back to him and write them on a slate every morning for four weeks.

"Enter it," said Alderman Tarrant, "as a count of the blue steps, with the dates on it, in the column for the district's own return, and enter that it is kept by a person without a row number and without a wage, and enter the reason in the ruled line under it, and the reason is that the district's return has had no count in it for eight weeks and this is the first figure anybody has entered against that water since the twentieth of the month before last."

Cray said that a count kept by a person with no row number and no wage was not a return and could not be checked against anything and would be in a book in four buildings in a fortnight.

"Then it is in a book in four buildings in a fortnight," said Tarrant, "and in about four years a man is going to ask what the water of the blue steps was doing on the first of next month, and there will be a figure and a date, and that is one thing more than there is now. Clerk, read it back."

It was read back. And the paragraph went in under the cut of a fifth with the date on it, and the paragraph said, in the terms of the office and without anything added to them, that the source of supply stated in the returns of district fourteen is not accounted for at this date; that the works at Sallow Head is on no row of this office and has no keeper; that a printed card at a culvert on the mountain service road, of which a copy is held in this building, states that the mark in the tank of that works has not varied in six years and that the flume sluice of that works is shut and chained; and that this body can neither raise nor reduce the ration of that district on a return whose source is not accounted for.

And at the foot of it, on a separate line, because Oriel Fenn would not have it in the paragraph, it said that the form's line at the foot of that return, headed *takers*, has stood empty on twenty-three returns in six years, and that this body has noted the line and has not filled it, and does not know of a person in that district to whom it may be addressed.

It was a Thursday. The room emptied by about one. A man in a good coat came in at four in the afternoon, looked at the cistern at the arch, wrote nothing down, and went out again, and the office has no acting commissioner and had sent nothing, and the count at the arch was on the back of a shutter in chalk and was the only figure in that city that anybody had entered about that water in eight weeks, and a boy of twenty-two who cannot read had carried that card up the mountain road in a cart and then in his coat for a fortnight to find out what a card was for.
