# Chapter 0097 — What Item One Was For

The assembly of the villages met in the room over the shop at Brant on the Tuesday morning of the twentieth at eight o'clock, which is the hour a man of fifty-eight named on the fifteenth in the second sentence of the fourth paragraph of a refusal, and about twenty people were in the room, and nine of them were the assembly, and the other eleven knew which nine they were.

The nine were a man of about seventy from Cold Ashton, who had written one sentence on the Wednesday; a clerk of Marl, who had asked for a copy; a man of forty-four from Dray, who had said nothing when the sheet went round and had come; a man of thirty-one from Fenn's, who had come although his village had refused and had brought the refusal with him in his coat; and five others. The ninth was a man of fifty-eight with eleven acres of beans in the lower field, who came in at about ten to eight and put his hat on the table, and his hat was the only hat in the room.

There was nothing else on the table. A man who is asked to sign something has to be able to read it. Sef Ordry stood by the wall. Tarin Rusk stood at the back. The man of thirty-eight with one hand sat on the stairs. The two boys were on the floor by the door. The man of sixty-one at the toll bar was on his feet by the window with his book under his arm, having walked four miles again, and having come on the day a man of fifty-eight named. Nesta Sarg was not in the assembly, because there is no form in this basin that would put her in it, and she had said so in this shop on Thursday in front of eleven people, and she was in the room anyway, at the back, on a stool.

---

It took about two hours and it was not a debate, and that is the thing about it that four people said afterwards to each other and not to the room. Nine men sat at a table for two hours and at no point did anybody say a thing that had been prepared.

Perdie Ammid read the request of the fourteenth out twice, and then put her hand flat on the declaration and said it was written in the hand of the man of sixty-one and that she had written it out from what he had said in this shop on the Friday, and that he would say whether she had got it right, and he had asked for that in advance in writing and it was the only condition he had made.

Then she read it out.

> *Entered by the assembly of the villages of the Sallow Reach, in the room over the shop at Brant, on the twentieth day of this month, being a day named by A. Sarg, one of the nine, in his own hand on the fifteenth of this month, and accepted by this assembly.*
>
> *Item one of the schedule of the works of the Reach, printed at this village in 1902, is not amended by this sheet, and nothing in this sheet is an amendment of it, and item one stands as printed.*
>
> *Item one was made in the year 1902 to forbid the taking of the water of the fields of this Reach out of those fields into the long drain. The West Flume is not the water of the fields of this Reach and is not taken out of any field of this Reach. The West Flume is a channel from the works at Sallow Head to the fields of this Reach, and the long drain is the drain of the fields of this Reach, and these were two different things in the year 1902, in the words used by the men who wrote them, and they are two different things now, in the words used by the people who have used them, and this assembly says so in those words and not in its own.*
>
> *This assembly does not resolve, and does not need to resolve, and does not admit, whether the six-inch waste pipe at the works is a drain of the fields or a channel from the works. No person shall take anything in this sheet as an admission, or a concession, or a precedent, upon that pipe. Item one continues to forbid whatever it forbade on the sixth day of last month, and whatever it forbade has not been altered by this sheet.*
>
> *And this assembly consents to one window and to no other: that on the twenty-second day of this month, from eleven o'clock at night, for four hours and six minutes, the West Flume sluice at Sallow Head may be opened, and the flume run, and the water fall where the flume's own outfall is, and the chain put back on before daylight, and the sluice shut, and no other water whatsoever admitted to the long drain at any time by reason of this sheet or of any act done under it.*
>
> *This consent is given for one night. It does not survive the night. It is not a precedent and it is not a rate and it is not a supply, and it creates no right in any district and no entry in any return, and no person is appointed by this sheet to anything, and any person may read this sheet and take a copy of it.*
>
> *Witnessed by nine. Signed by eight.*

She read it twice, slowly, and then said that it was narrower than what was asked for, and that this was on purpose, and that the man who wrote the words in it had asked her for that.

---

Then the man of sixty-one said she had got it right, and then said she had got two-thirds of it right, and that the third was coming out of her apron and not out of his head, and that he would not mind if the room were patient for about eleven minutes.

He did not sit down. He put the book on the window sill and kept his hand on it.

"You all think item one is about the company. It is not about the company. The company had been there since 1843 with a schedule of its own a hundred years long, and he was not writing a company's schedule and he would have been insulted if you thought he was.

"He meant **ours.** He wrote a fence round the fields so that nobody could take their water out of them and sell it. Every drop that has been in this Reach since 1902 and is not rain and not a well is ours to divide or ours to get rid of, and a field can only give it up to a drain, and a drain can only give it up to the channel. **The flume is not a field.** A flume is a thing a man put between the tank and the fields in order to divide ours, and a thing that divides ours is not ours, and in 1902 he knew that, and I have never in forty years met a man who was alive then.

"And the second thing, which none of you will want.

"**The tank is not the Reach's.** It was built in 1843 by people who had a book nobody in this valley can get hold of, and the Reach has never had a copy of that book and has never asked for one. We have taken water off a works that belonged to somebody else for longer than this valley has had a name at all, and called it ours because it is the only water there has ever been, and the reason we can put a flume sluice on a company's gate is that a company has never once in a hundred and thirty years come up this road to look at one.

"I said that once before, in a hut, to about four people, and a man from a village wrote it down and carried it about, and I did not tell him to and I would have been ashamed if he had asked me to."

Nobody in that room said anything for about eleven seconds, which is how anybody in the lane knows it was eleven.

"That is all. **I am not asking you to do anything about the second thing, and I am sixty-one and I am not going to be a man who puts up a second thing at seventy.** Put it in the minutes. Do not put it in the declaration. A narrow thing can be got through nine men in a morning and a wide thing cannot, and I have watched this valley lose a good idea in a month for exactly that reason."

Then he asked her to put it in the minutes and not to read it out and not to let anybody hear it that day, because the man who wrote it was four days away and would want to know it was said and would not be able to answer it.

Perdie Ammid wrote it on a sheet of paper and did not read it out and put the date on it, and then she stopped, and the man of sixty-one waited about nine seconds.

"I have put **copy** at the top of it in my own hand," she said, "and I have done that four times this week and I have said the reason out loud to about nine people each time, and I am going to say it to you because you are the only man in this room who is owed an explanation and you are not getting one about the flume.

"**The minutes of the assembly of the villages go in the record of the assembly of the villages. The record of the assembly of the villages is four field books. The four field books are in a cart with a board across it and they left this shop on Friday morning at nine o'clock and they are four days up a road.** So the minutes of this morning are on five sheets of paper in my apron, and one of them is in a boy's coat, and every one of them says *copy* at the top, and there is no column in this village any more and there has not been since Friday morning.

"You told me to put it in the minutes and I have put it in a sheet. And I would do it again, and I want it noticed that I would do it again, and I would like somebody in this room to feel how small that is."

Nobody said anything about it, and the man of thirty-one at the window did not look at her, and she did not look at him, and that is the whole of what happened between them about it.

"The requisition goes up on the Tuesday cart and it will be in a counting-house at Vellum on Friday," she said, "and I am going up the lane myself on Tuesday afternoon with the sheets in the same pocket, and I shall be in Culm on Wednesday morning, and in about four years there will be a man in that building with a bundle and a sheet of paper in it with the word *copy* at the top of it, and he will be right to doubt it, and he will not be able to do anything about it, and that is the whole of what my bill was worth, and I priced it in four seconds in a room on the fifth of this month and I did not know what I was buying."

---

Then the eight signed and the ninth did not.

A man of about fifty off the two villages on the Kerrow lane read the sheet twice with his finger and put it down.

"I am not signing this, and I am not sending it back, and I want it in the minutes that I was asked and refused and said no in front of nine men, and I would like anybody in this room who thinks that is cowardice to say so now while I am sitting here."

Nobody said so.

"My reason is my own and it is not a rule. **A declaration of what a thing is is not an amendment, and I have read it twice, and I do not believe it.** I have lived on that lane sixty-one years and I have seen two people say out loud what a thing was and about four others say it the other way, and I do not know which of them was right, and I am not going to put my name to a thing I do not know. And I will say this too, because not saying it would be cheating: **there are nineteen houses at the end of that drain, and I do not think anybody has the right to put three thousand four hundred and eighty-five gallons in it in a drought, whatever it is called.** I have read it and I do not believe it and I am not going to pretend I do, and I am sorry, and there is my reason."

Perdie Ammid entered it as the second refusal in a fortnight, in her own hand, with the date, and read it out, and nobody argued with it, and the man of thirty-eight with one hand said afterwards that it was the only honest thing anybody said at that table, and that it cost that man more to say it in a room than the eight men would ever pay, and that neither of them was thanked.

And then the man of fifty-eight with eleven acres of beans took off his hat and signed.

He signed for himself. His sister was not asked, because there is no form in this basin that would put her in it, and she had said so in this room on Thursday in front of eleven people, and nobody asked her and nobody suggested it. He did not look at her. She did not look at him. **Two people in a room of about twenty, in a village of about two hundred, four miles from a toll bar, four years of not speaking, one signature, and about nine witnesses, and neither of them said one word to anybody and neither of them was thanked and neither of them was asked.**

She said one sentence later, at the back of the room, and it was to nobody.

"**My brother has signed a paper and I have not been asked to sign a paper, and that is a fact about the assembly and not about us, and I am not going to help anybody in this room make it into anything else this month.**"

---

At about ten past ten Tarin Rusk came off the back wall and said four things, and they took about nine seconds, and nobody understood any of them except one woman in the room and she did not say so.

"The nine have done a thing that I have been in this valley fifty-nine days trying to do with sentences, and I want it entered that a sheet of paper in nine hands is worth a month of me in a room, and I have been the second one since I was thirty-one, and this morning I was not even the second one. I was at the back by the door with a boy of fifteen.

"And I am going to be told in about four days' time by a man with a bar that this is the fourth thing this month that I have been right about and got nothing for, and he will be right, and I would like it entered now so that he cannot be told he is wrong.

"And **I did not get it.** Nine men gave me what a flume is, and it is smaller than the window and it is the only thing that could have been got out of a room in a morning, and I asked for the wrong thing for fifty-nine days, and I would like that written down as well, and by me, and not by the man of thirty-eight.

"And there is nobody in this room to thank and I am not going to look for one, and I have looked for one for fifty-nine days, and that is a habit and not a virtue, and about nine people in a month have told me so and I have stopped arguing with them."

And then he said the fourth thing, and it was not to the room.

"Nine men have just done the fourth thing," he said, to Perdie Ammid, quietly, in the corner by the stairs. "You said it in a room in May and I have written it down and carried it about and never once known what it meant, and I think I know now, and I would like you to tell me if I am wrong. **A physical trace, and a witness, and a record, and a legitimacy.** Three of those have been in this valley since the second day. The fourth one has a date and nine signatures on it, and it was signed forty minutes ago by a man with a shovel for a hand who will lose a trench across his own ground for it, and I have not got a name for what that does to me and I have got three in six weeks, and I am not going to use them on a thing I do not understand in a drought in a valley that has had enough of me."

"Write it down," said Perdie Ammid, "and I will tell you in about four years whether you were right, and I have not thanked you."

On the Tuesday afternoon of the twentieth of this month, before she went up the lane, on the fourth sheet, in her own hand, with the word *copy* at the top of it, a woman of fifty-four wrote:

> *The twentieth day of this month. The assembly of the villages met, nine men, and signed eight, and refused one in the room, and the refusal is on this sheet and it is the best thing said at that table, and the man who made it has not been thanked and was not thanked and I would like it noticed that nobody tried.*
>
> *Item one of the schedule of the works of the Reach stands as printed. A window of four hours and six minutes is consented to for one night by a sheet that says what a flume is and says nothing whatever about a waste pipe. A man of sixty-one at a toll bar said a second thing, and it is on a separate sheet, and it is not read out, and nothing has been done about it, and it is that the tank is not the Reach's and the Reach has never had a copy of the book of 1843 and has never asked for one.*
>
> *That sheet is in an apron and it is going up a lane this afternoon in the same pocket as a requisition, and nobody in this basin can do anything about it this week, and the window is in two days.*
>
> *It has been asked whether the record of the assembly of the villages is a book. It is not, at present, and this is a copy, and the word is at the top of it in my own hand, and I have not asked anybody to excuse it.*

And the flume sluice was shut and chained, and that chain had been bought with a man's own money and the man had not told anybody, and the third rod on a card at a culvert was empty with a padlock printed under it, and that card was in a press at Culm and would be back on the post on the Friday, and a man from Cresset was on his fifty-ninth day in a valley, and on Friday night at eleven o'clock four men — three of them asked and one of them not — would stand at three points on a six-inch stone launder two miles and four chains long, and a boy of fifteen would stand on the coping of a tank with a level staff and a watch and a slate that belonged to him and nobody else.
