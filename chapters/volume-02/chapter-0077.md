# Chapter 0077 — It Lasted A Day

Sef Ordry came down the bank on the Sunday morning of the twenty-eighth day of this month at about ten o'clock on foot, with the bar over his shoulder and a lath in his coat, and there was nobody else on the road, and there was no cart going up it until the Tuesday.

Tarin Rusk was in the shed door. The card was on the nail. There was a hundred and eighty-one years of a shed, a stove, two benches and a smell of oil.

"I have not come to cut anything," said Sef Ordry. "I have come because I have got a thing to say that I would rather say on a Sunday than on a Monday, and I have thought about it since about four o'clock yesterday afternoon and I could not get it into a shape that fitted a Monday, and the reason it will not fit a Monday is that on Monday I am going to be a man working, and this is a man talking."

"Say it."

"I want to say the bill first, because you have not asked for it and you are not going to, and I have worked for eleven years under a master who has told me about nine hundred times to enter the cost, and there is no cost on this and that is the problem. Put your coat down."

Tarin Rusk put his coat down on the bench and Sef Ordry took out the bar and turned it over in his hands, which he does when he is reading a thing that is not wood.

"Here is the bill and it is a professional bill and it is not a grievance and I am not going to have anybody in this basin tell me it is a grievance, because a grievance is a thing you take to a man with money and I have not got one of those.

"On the fifteenth day of this month I stood in this shed at a quarter to eight in the morning with a disc of red wax in my open hand and I did not touch anything, and I put my hand out for about two hours and a half and a quarter while a sheet was read out and copied in two hands and signed, and I did that on purpose, and I have said out loud in three rooms in this month why I did it, and it was so that in about four years there would be one man in this basin who could stand up in front of a bench and say that he did not break a Guild seal. I bought that. It cost me two hours of holding my hand out and the skin off both forearms, and I have not regretted it and I never will.

"And on Saturday morning at a quarter past eleven, a man of thirty-three put the name **Sef Ordry, sluicewright, of Bexhill, of no district** into a printed line on a form, without asking me, as a man who has the water, so that a thirsty man can come to my door.

"Both of those are in a row now and there is nothing in this basin that will ever separate them. Not the shop, not the book, not the shed door. In about four years a clerk in a counting-house is going to lay out a card that says the Rusk has put his hand on it, and a copy signed by a fitter and a boy, and a destroyed plate in a barrow, and a return with three names in it, and that clerk is going to read them in an afternoon and put them in a row, because that is what a clerk does with four documents about a man. And he will be wrong. And it will not matter that he is wrong, because there is no form in this basin in which a man may object to a sentence in a margin, and I have known that since I was twenty-three and I have used it about other men twice and been ashamed of it once.

"That is the bill. It is a real one and it is payable in about four years and not today, and I have not asked anybody to pay anything, and I have come to say it out loud in a shed on a Sunday so that it is said."

"Thank you," said Tarin Rusk.

"Don't," said Sef Ordry. "I have not given you anything. I have written down a thing about myself and I would like you to hear it and I would like you not to be grateful, because in about four years a man being grateful about a professional cost is a man who has not understood it."

---

"I have read the thing you wrote in the second book," he said, "and I want to take a piece of it out and put it somewhere else, and I want you to stop me, and I want you to know that I am not asking you to be nicer about it.

"The cost you have entered is about my books. Perdie Ammid has entered it and initialled it and it is on a page of its own and the page after it is going to be the reason there is a page after it, and that is hers and it is correct and I would not touch it.

"There is a second cost and it is mine and it is not in the book because I would rather say it than enter it.

"**You have had my reason since four o'clock yesterday afternoon and you did not use it.** You used my name. Those are two different things and I have spent a night turning them over and I have got to this and I would like you to have it in one paragraph and then I will be at a gate bed on Tuesday and you will hold the other end of it.

"The reason I gave you in that yard was a habit and not a rule, and I said so, and I said I would not say it twice because the second time it is a rule, and you asked me to say it again this morning and I said no, and you did not press me and I want it entered that you did not press me and that not pressing me was the correct thing and that it cost you the whole of Saturday, and I do not think you know that it cost you the whole of Saturday.

"So the reason is gone. It lasted a day. It was in the room on Friday afternoon and on Saturday morning at eight o'clock it was in the room and at a quarter past eleven it was not, and the reason it was not is that a man who has got a sheet of paper and eight days and a habit in his head can carry all three, and he carried three, and one of the three was mine.

"That is not a thing you did to me. I want that said in a shed and I want it said in those words. I gave you a thing on Friday on purpose, knowing it was a habit and not a rule, and I said it to one man and not to a yard, and I have been in a shed nine years and I have never given a habit to one man in my life, and I gave it to you because I was going to be standing in this room on Monday and I wanted there to be one reason in it that was not a rule.

"And there was, for about a day, and now there is not."

---

"Give it back to me," said Tarin Rusk.

"No."

"You do not know what I am going to do with it."

"I know exactly what you are going to do with it and it is the right thing and it is the reason I am not going to let you.

"You are going to put it on the record in place of my name. I have watched you do arithmetic like that all month. You have got four true lines in a book and no fifth line and a man of thirty-four in a black coat in a counting-house who is not going to look at four true lines and no fifth line, and you have worked out in about a day and a night that there is a sentence in a man of thirty-one's mouth that would do the work a name does, and you are right, and it will not go in.

"A name goes in a book. A reason does not go in a book. That is not a complaint about the book, it is what a book is. A name is a mark, and a mark can be looked at, and a name can be said out loud in a room in eleven years and be checked against a man. A reason is a sentence, and a sentence in a book about a route is an argument, and an argument can be carried from room to room for a hundred and thirty years and a route cannot, and I have never in nine years seen a man carry a route in a sentence.

"And I will say to you that I have listened to a woman of fifty-four say that in a room in this village on the twenty-seventh day of the month before and I did not agree with it then and I agree with about two thirds of it now, and the two thirds is the part about a mark and a book, and the third is that people do carry things in sentences, and what they carry is not water."

He put the bar down on the bench.

"You cannot have it back, and you were not having it in the first place, and the reason you cannot have it back is that it was never a substitute for anything. It was the only thing in this month that anybody has given anybody for nothing, and I have given it to you, and I am not going to be relieved of it by having it entered in a book as though the entering of it were the end of it.

"And I will tell you the thing I would take most comfort from out of all of it, and it is a small thing, and it is the only one available.

"You asked me to say it again this morning. You did not ask me what was on the sheet. You did not ask me whether I had read it. You did not ask me whether I had seen your coat. You asked me to say a thing twice, which is the only one of the four questions a person might ask that has an answer that is not a document.

"You are not a fool. That is what is wrong with you. I have met fools and I have met careful men and I have never met one that was both."

---

At about eleven the boy of fifteen came up the road on foot, on the Sunday, by himself, because the roster said the last Saturday in the month and the last Saturday in the month had gone by with four men in a room in a village and he had come on the Sunday as well.

Perdie Ammid paid him the Saturday at five shillings, and a tenth of a shilling for the Sunday, out of the villages, and entered both in the second book in the wages column, and did not enter the Sunday as a day of the count because the count had not been taken.

"Nobody has asked me what is on the form," he said, at the door of the shop, on the Sunday evening, with his coat on. "I have not been asked and I am not to be asked and I would like it written down that I have not been asked and that I did not offer, and I would like it written down in the book next to my column and not in a speech.

"And I told a man on Friday night that he had a sheet in his coat and that I had noticed and that I was not to be asked, and he did nothing about it until Saturday, and that is in the book because I have said it and I am not sorry I said it and it did not help and I am not going to be the man who says it helped."

And Perdie Ammid wrote it in, and put the date on it, and put his own initials beside it, because the boy is not to be named and the fact is to be kept.

There is a thing that happened in this month and nobody in the Sallow Reach has put it in a book, and it is in this account because it happened.

Margit Saye is on a mountain road four miles from a village with a case, and she is the only person alive who can read the hand in the third book, and she has said in two rooms that she will not name a man in it, and she has said in a third that the hand is a desk and not a man and that the Guild keeps no sample of its clerks' hands and that the Guild's own good rule is the reason the question in this Reach cannot be answered.

She refused to carry the specimen slips out of the counting-house, which was correct. She wrote the hand out from memory in her own hand on her own paper and gave it away, which was generous. And on Saturday morning she got on a cart at ten o'clock with a specification book in an oilcloth case and a rule and a lamp and a bottle and a tin and a roll of string and an eleven-inch canvas book of her own, and she took a way of reading a page with her, and there is now no person in this Reach who could be shown a document in a counting-house and say whether the hand on it is the hand on a flyleaf in a chest in a village.

In about a year somebody will take a page out of a drawer in a counting-house at Vellum and put it beside that flyleaf, and it will be a very ordinary afternoon, and there will be one person in this country who could settle it, and she will be in a shed at Bexhill, and she will be right, and nobody will have asked her.

That is the second bill, and it is the larger one, and it is not payable in about four years. It is payable whenever anybody next wants to know.
