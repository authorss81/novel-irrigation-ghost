# Chapter 0090 — What He Did In The Sixth Year

The second letter came down on the Tuesday morning of the thirteenth day of next month on foot, with a man from Bexhill who had walked from the toll bar with a satchel and nothing else in it, and it came three days after it was written and two days after the answer came down the road, because the answer was done in a morning by a desk and this was written by a man.

It is three sides of a sheet. The first two are in the hand of Iven Marrow, which is a bad hand and gets worse at the end of a page. The third side is worse than the first two and there are two words struck through on it and not inked over, and he has left them struck.

Perdie Ammid read it out in the front room of the shop at Brant at about eleven in the morning to about nine people, and she read it out in full, and when she came to the two struck words she said that she was going to read the letter as it stood and that anybody who wanted the words that were not there could go to Bexhill and ask him, and nobody went.

---

> *To P. Ammid, at the shop, in a village of the Sallow Reach. From I. Marrow, master, of the Open Channel Guild, at Bexhill. The tenth day of this month.*
>
> *I am going to write this badly and in the first person throughout, because a man's own hand is the only kind of hand that means anything in this letter, and you are a woman who has spent twenty-six years finding out what a hand is worth and you will know that I am not flattering myself about mine.*
>
> **One.** *Your counting-house sent back a form dated the seventh of this month in a hand that is not mine and could not be mine, and the desk that wrote it did the whole of it in a morning, and it is dated the day it was done, and it says four things and three of them are the company's and one of them is the rule on the front of the form. I got a direction into the same sheet with one letter at the bottom of it, and a one-letter initial is not a signature, and a man who cannot be signed for cannot be answered for, and I am not going to have it reported in this valley that I said a thing. So I am saying it here, in my own hand, and nobody can add this to a card.*
>
> **Two.** *You have asked me, in a room, by a fitter, and by a bookkeeper, and by a boy with a bar, and I am going to answer the only question in this letter that anybody actually asked, which is why a gate at Sallow Head has read dry for six years, and my answer is about me and not about a county four days away and not about a man in it.*
>
> *It has read dry for six years. The reading is true. The plate in the ring at the intake is a half-inch, and the plate it replaced was a half-inch, and both entries are in my own bench book, six years apart, and neither of them is a note, and that is the whole of the iron and nobody has touched it.*
>
> *And the head on the printed slip of 1902 is a figure that no person in this basin has ever been put in front of, and the only instrument that could is a hundred and forty years old at Ostre and belongs to a man of eighty-one.*
>
> *So the water is not coming. I have had that for six years and I have had no reason for it, and I have had a plate and a card and a true reading, and that is all I have had, and I am not going to make a sentence about the sluice and I have not made one and I have had six weeks and a great deal of reason to make one and I have not made it, and the reason I have not is that I am about to tell a village in my own hand that a man who has not been at a work ought not to be signing about it, and the sluice is a work and I have not been at it in six years.*
>
> *I have written a fourth time to the man at Ostre and the answer is silence, and I will say again, for the fitter's hearing, what the fitter said to me and what I agreed with and what I have not stopped agreeing with: the right reading of silence out of a man of eighty-one, with an instrument of a hundred and forty years and no obligation to anybody in this basin, is not that he will not help. It is that nobody has ever asked him in a way that a man of eighty-one could answer.*
>
> **Three.** *Here is what I did in the sixth year, and this is the part of this letter that will cost me and I am going to say it plainly because I have found out in the last four days that a thing is only worth what the hand is that wrote it.*
>
> *I signed a return. The return said the reach had no keeper, and it was true, and I was the party having the care of that work and had been for nine years, and I signed it without going up the road, and I have not been up that road in six years.*
>
> *And in the same year, in the Reach's own copy of its own schedule, in a village, a keeper's name was struck out and the words supply district 14, North Sallow were written in its place, in a hand that was already in that printed book from the second page to the end, which means the man who wrote it had the book in front of him and wrote the other pages of it in the same week with the same pen.*
>
> *Two documents. Both true. Neither one wrong. They do not agree, and there is nothing in either of them to catch out, and between them they are the whole of the trouble of the Sallow Reach, and I wrote one of them, and I did not know for eight years that the other existed, and I sent the one I wrote to a village in a cart with a letter asking them to check a length, and the length was wrong, and they checked it, and they sent me the answer, and I have it in a drawer, and I did not act on it, and there is no version of the next six years in which anybody could have told me I should have.*
>
> *Enter it the way it is. Not a plot. Not a theft. **A man in an office copied a line out of a book, and a village checked a figure he was sent, and between the two of them a reach was read two ways for six years, and neither of them knew the other existed, and both of them were honest, and the honesty is the whole of the trick, and I am the man who signed the one in the counting-house.***
>
> **Four.** *And this is the sentence, and it is the only one I am going to make this year, and I am making it in a letter to a village because there are about forty men in a shed at Bexhill on a Tuesday morning and a letter has one man in it, and I want it in a book sixty-one miles from here in a place where nobody has ever been thanked for anything.*
>
> *The company has kept a reading card over four hundred works for a hundred and thirty years, and the card is what a plate says, and a plate is a permanent thing and a card is a yearly thing, and a man in a counting-house who has never been up a mountain reads a card and thinks about the works. **A company that keeps a record of a work it does not go and look at has not got a record. It has got a habit.** And the habit has a man's name on it and the man is me, and I have held the Sallow card for six years and have not been up that road in six years, and I sent a fitter up it for three days with a bundle of forms in a month and did not go with her, and I had a plate and a true reading and a paper full of my own trade and no single reason to go, and not going is not a decision anybody made. It is just what nobody does.*
>
> *And a journeyman of mine of nine years standing said in a shed on Wednesday of this month that the good practice of my company is the reason the counting-house at Vellum cannot be opened from outside, and that there is a difference between a firm that can be asked a question and a firm that has never once been asked one, and that he has spent nine years keeping us out of rooms and is proud of it. I have got the sense of it right and the words wrong, and I have had three days and I did not go and find him and ask him to say it again, which is the whole of the fault and the whole of the sentence. I am putting it in a book. That is not a theft either, and if I leave it out then in four years two of us will have said the same thing in two places and one of us will be believed, and it will not be the one who was there.*
>
> **Five.** *What I am not going to do, and I am putting it in the same letter so that there is no room in this valley for anybody to think it was overlooked.*
>
> *I am not going to name a man. Three men in a counting-house write that hand, and a fitter of twenty-four years' standing has said in front of a hundred and forty people that she will not name one of them, and she is right, and I have had the specimen slips in my own company's case for thirty years and have never taken them out and am not going to start at fifty-four. And here is the reason in one sentence, and it is the reason and not an excuse: **a man who has been wrong in print for six years has no business handing anybody a name, and if I put a man's name in a letter to that valley, then in four years there will be a story that the master of the Guild settled the Sallow question, and the story will be believed because I am a master, and masters are believed, and I have been wrong in print about a gate and about a plate and about six years and about the best practice of a company of three hundred men, and a man who is wrong in a book is the last man in this basin who should hand anybody anything.***
>
> *And I have not touched the question of the sluice, and there is no sentence of mine about it in this letter, and I would like it noticed that there is no sentence of mine about it in this letter, and not that I could not have written one.*
>
> **Six.** *The offer has not moved. It is the offer of a man of thirty-three in a store on a hill a month ago, in this order: work, access to the reading card for the whole of eleven thousand four hundred leaves, and eleven weeks of a bench at Bexhill on Tuesday mornings. The price is the same price and it is the price and I have not been asked to say it and I am saying it because this letter must not be read as a complaint, and the price is that the blue steps is not in a report, and I have not moved it and I am not going to and I would rather it was a worse price than a moving one.*
>
> *The offer does not expire. The bench is on a Tuesday. **Nothing in this letter is part of the offer, and nothing in this letter is an appeal against anything, and I have had two men in two rooms ask me this month whether I have finally been got at by a village, and I would like it entered that they asked it and that I said what I am saying here.***
>
> *— I. Marrow, master.*

---

There were about nine people in that room and it was the largest that a piece of paper has been in for a month, and four of the nine had read it before it was read out, because the man off the road read it to Bess Ilk at the counter at about half past eight and she made him read it again, and Bess Ilk has never in thirty-one years made anybody read a thing twice at her counter and told him why.

Nobody thanked him, and it was said in nine words by the only person in the room entitled to say it.

Sef Ordry put the bar down and said: "He has written in his own hand that the company does not go and look at its works, and he has written it to a village, and the company is three hundred men, and about forty of them will be in that shed at Bexhill on a Tuesday morning, and every one of those forty will now be able to say, correctly, and in front of apprentices, that the master says the company does not go and look at works. He knew that on the Wednesday, in the shed, with the door open. He has known it since. **He has not moved the bench.**"

"And he is not in this room," said Tarin Rusk, "and he is four days away and he cannot be sent for, and nothing in this letter asks us for anything, and that is the first thing sent into this valley in a month that has not asked us for something, and I would like somebody to notice it, because the last four documents that came up this road all wanted something and one of them wanted three men's names."

"What did it cost him," said Jory Vant, from the wall, in the voice he uses when he has a figure he cannot check.

"It cost him three hundred men and a bench he did not move," said Nesta Sarg. "And it cost him the right to hand anybody a name, and he gave that up in a letter to a village and did not have to, and I have spent thirty-two years saying the price of a thing and I have never once been able to price that, and I am not going to try this month, and I am not going to be thanked, and I have said that eleven times now."

---

The cost is entered in the second book on the afternoon of the thirteenth, in Perdie Ammid's hand, in the column headed *what it cost*, and it is the second entry ever made in that column, and she read it out and would not let anybody take it out of the book.

> *The thirteenth day of this month. Entered from a letter in the hand of the master of the Open Channel Guild, at Bexhill, four days by road from this shop, unasked for, in answer to nothing, and copied out in full in the second book on a page of its own in the hand of J. Vant, apprentice, of Bexhill, of no district, who was not asked to copy it and copied all three sides.*
>
> *1. The record of the Sallow Reach, for the six years from the sixth, is a habit and not a record, and that sentence is in this book and is not in any book of the company, and it was put into a company of three hundred men by a village, from a letter, without the company.*
> *2. A journeyman of nine years standing said the same thing in a shed on the seventh of this month, to a master and not to a book, and the master has put it in a book and taken the credit and has said so, and the journeyman has not been thanked and was not thanked in this room this morning either.*
> *3. The master is not in this room and is four days away. Nothing was decided in his absence and nothing was decided against him, and he is not owed a hearing and has not asked for one, and the thing that has happened to him has happened in a letter, and I am the only person in this basin who is going to have to write to him, and I do not know what I am going to say, and I have got a fortnight to find out.*

And Jory Vant signed the copy, and the signature says that the three sides above are copied in his own hand and are a copy and not the original, and that the original is in a counting-house at Bexhill, and that I. Marrow is a signatory of no book in this village and of no sheet in this room and is not a party to anything here.

---

And at about four o'clock on the same afternoon a man of thirty-eight with one hand priced the week in a yard, in about four minutes, in days, and it went into the column on a fifth line, and the column now has five lines in it and there is still nothing at the bottom of it.

"The card went up the road on the Friday the ninth and it comes back printed on the Wednesday the twenty-first, and in those twelve days there are two Saturdays and two Sundays in it, and a printer of sixty-eight does not work either, and I have priced that in, and it is four days I had not counted, and it is nobody's fault, and it is in the column.

"The requisition went up on the Tuesday evening cart of the thirtieth of the month before and came back on the Sunday evening of the eleventh, and the answer was done in a morning by a desk and dated the seventh. That is **four days there and four days back and a cart both ways, and four days standing in a yard at Vellum with a wet coat on the seat, which is the part nobody ever puts in a column and which is most of it.** There is a man on the forward cart and a boy of nineteen on the up cart and a man off the road with a satchel on the Tuesday, and that last one walked from the toll bar and charged nobody anything.

"Twelve days. And I want it written down, because this is the column and the column is to be added to and not taken back, and **twelve days is the whole of that requisition and there is no iron at the end of it, and the iron is the cheapest thing in this column and it is the only thing anybody in this basin has actually asked for in six years.** Four and a half, five and a half, four and a half, one, and twelve. That is twenty-seven and a half days, and a boy of fifteen has a wage out of it and a man of thirty-one has given a day and a half of a Saturday, and in about a year there is going to be a number at the bottom of this column and I want to be alive to see it, and I do not think I am going to be the only one who is."

Perdie Ammid entered the fifth line. It is a Tuesday, and the count card was on a post at a culvert sixty-one miles away with a third rod empty and a reason under the hole and a fourth rod in a boy's hand with a signature under it, and the card would be printed on the twenty-first, and the flume sluice was shut and chained, and there was no keeper, and a man of thirty-three was on his fifty-second day in a valley and his fourteenth week ends in the month after next, and nobody in the Sallow Reach had thanked Iven Marrow for anything and he had not asked.
