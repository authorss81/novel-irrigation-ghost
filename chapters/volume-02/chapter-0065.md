# Chapter 0065 — What Is Inside The Cover

Sixty-one miles east, on the tenth day of this month, Niko Venn put a chalk on the coping of a yard cistern for the eleventh morning running and wrote down the same number he had written ten times.

He was twenty-two. He had a piece of string, a stub of chalk, a tin with four pebbles in it on a shelf in his aunt's old room, a tin of sixty-eight gallons of water at the back of the dispensary that he had not been asked to guard and did guard, and no wages, and there was no line in any budget in four buildings for him, because the work he was doing was a boy with a stick, and a physician had said out loud in a yard in this basin that a boy with a stick is not an instrument.

The cistern had stood at its coping since the afternoon of the twentieth day of the month before last, a district of a hundred and twenty-six people was on a ration cut by a fifth, and the district appeared in no return at all. On the morning of the fourth of this month a man in a good coat had come into the arch, looked at the cistern, written nothing down, and gone out again, and the office has no acting commissioner and had sent nothing since.

Nobody in the Sallow Reach knew that Niko Venn existed. He had not been asked to come west and had not asked. He had said on the Friday of the month before *then I will hold the count and you will not write to me about it, because I have got it*, and Tarin Rusk had not written to him since, in a village two days from a man with a lath, and that was the correct procedure and it was also the loneliest thing in the month, and no man in any room in the Reach was to mention it.

---

At the foot of the embankment on the Wednesday morning there were six men and a stove and the shed door open on the hillside, and Margit Saye had the flume sluice's inspection cover in her hand before anybody had said anything.

It is a flat iron plate about the size of a man's palm, let into the frame of the gate with four screws and a slot in the middle of it about the width of a thumb, and the slot is what the sheet is behind, and across the slot there is a strip of paper and a disc of wax with the Guild's device on it, and the wax is red and has been red for six years and has the weather on it and the print of somebody's thumb in the middle of it where somebody pressed it six years ago.

"That is the seal," said Margit Saye. "And the wax is not the only thing. The paper is gummed and the paper is gummed because that is what the rule says, and the rule is that the sheet behind the slot cannot be got at without breaking the wax, and that is on purpose, and the reason it is on purpose is the only reason anybody has ever cared about a bit of red wax in this trade, which is that a sheet nobody can get at is a sheet nobody can have altered and nobody can have taken out, and that is a thing you can stand in a room and say."

"What is inside it," said Tarin Rusk.

"That," said Margit Saye, "is the entire question of this week, and you are asking me it in a shed door on a Wednesday and I am going to answer it in a shed door on a Wednesday, in front of six people, and I am going to answer it in about forty words and then nobody is to ask me again until Monday."

The wax came out of the slot whole. It is a thing that has been done nine thousand times in this basin and it is done with a small iron and a bottle of water, and the paper lifts off the slot after about a minute, and it does not tear, and there is a sheet behind it in a folded state about the size of a playing card.

It had been folded and unfolded and folded about four hundred times and the folds had gone through it and it came apart in two places along them and Margit Saye did not try to make it worse. She put it on the bench.

> **Open Channel Guild. Inspection of gates, sluices and stops. Kerrow Water, Sallow Head, West Flume.**
> *Of the tenth day of the month, being the sixth year of the present standard.*
> *Item: the West Flume sluice at the head of the flume, with its chain, its lock and its inspection cover.*
> *Found: shut, and chained, and the chain sound, and the lock sound. A padlock is on it which is not the padlock this sheet was begun for, and its number is not entered, and there is a note in the margin that the padlock is to be brought into the next return with its number.*
> *The flume from the head to the second mile: clear, and the invert true, and about two feet of silt in the first four hundred yards.*
> *The channel below the second mile: not walked, on account of the state of the ground, and to be walked at the first opportunity.*
> *Remarks: the chain is to be renewed and a keeper named.*
> *Signed, W. Hesketh, fitter. Countersigned, the master.*

Nobody in the shed said anything for about eleven seconds.

"There," said Margit Saye. "Now the forty words, and then I am going to sit down and I am going to have a cup of something and I am not going to be asked anything else about a piece of paper until Monday.

"That sheet is six years old and it is the only record that this basin has that anybody ever looked at this place. Not a card. A card is what people turn over. A sheet is what somebody signed, by a fitter and countersigned by a man who had read it, and the countersignature means the master of the Guild saw the words *the chain is to be renewed and a keeper named* and signed his name under them.

"And in six years the chain has not been renewed, there is no keeper, and the second mile has not been walked, and this basin has now got a sheet that says all three of those things in the handwriting of two men who were both paid to write them.

"That is what a sheet is for. And from the moment I take that wax off in a shed door on a Wednesday afternoon, this is not a record. It is a folded piece of paper in a shed that a man can alter, and the moment somebody says a number was wrong, or a date, or a fitter's name, then there is nothing whatever to look at."

---

"Then do not do it," said Tarin Rusk.

"Which of the three ways," said Sef Ordry, from the door, who had not said anything for six minutes.

"The three ways," said Sef Ordry, and came over and put his thumb beside hers on the bench without touching the paper. "You cannot open that gate with the chain on. And the chain is on with a padlock on it, and there is no key to that padlock in this basin except at Bexhill, and Bexhill is four days, and I am not going to pretend to anybody that a fitter's letter at Bexhill is a thing you can count on, and we both know what happens to a request that comes from a stranger's acquaintance in a shed.

"So it is the chain or it is the padlock or it is the seal, and the chain can go back on and the padlock can go back on, and both of them are bolts and a lock and a man can do that with a bar and a chisel in about nine minutes and a shed gets two broken things instead of one.

"And you have asked me about the seal twice since Friday and I have let you, and here is the answer to both of them, and I am saying it in daylight in a shed with six witnesses, which is the third time in three weeks I have said something like it and I would like the third one recorded.

"No. Not casually. And I am not being proud. If that wax comes off in a shed on a Wednesday because a man asked, then the next thing anybody in this basin will do with a sealed cover is take it off because a man asked, and there are two hundred and thirty-nine plate specifications in this basin and I do not know how many sealed covers, and in eleven years somebody will take a wax off because a man asked and there will be no sheet, and the reason there will be no sheet is a Wednesday afternoon in a shed, and the reason there is no sheet is that breaking it was easy.

"It is not easy. It is done properly, or it is not done. And done properly means the purpose is stated and written down before the wax is touched, and two men are standing there, and the sheet behind the slot is read out in full, and copied, in two hands, while the wax is still whole, and the two men both sign the copy while the wax is still whole, and the date goes on the copy, and then the wax goes.

"And then the copy is a record. Not because it is better than the sheet. Because two men were standing at a slot in a gate with a red disc of wax in their hands and both of them put their names to it before the wax was broken, and in eleven years a man in a counting-house can ask a fitter what that means and the fitter will have to say."

Margit Saye had not moved.

"That is the procedure," she said. "That is the procedure as it is written and I have it in a book, and I want it noticed that a book of what a plate ought to be has got the answer to how a wax comes off, and that is a sentence I would not have believed in my life a fortnight ago."

---

The boy of fifteen asked the question, at the end, in the doorway, and it was the question Tarin Rusk had not thought of.

"If it is done properly on Monday," the boy said, "then is the copy as good as the sheet."

And Sef Ordry answered it, and did not soften it.

"No," he said. "And anybody who tells you it is will be telling you a thing that makes him comfortable. The copy is a copy. In eleven years there will be a sheet in a shed and a copy in a village, and a man will be able to alter the sheet and he will not be able to alter the copy, and that is worth having, and it is not the same as the sheet, and the difference is between a thing two men saw and a thing a man wrote down afterwards.

"And I am going to be one of the two men standing there, and I am not going to be one of the two men who picks up the pen, and I could have both, and here is why I am not.

"If a fitter of twenty-four years standing with a company breaks a Guild seal in a shed, then in four years every man in this basin says the Guild was careless with a seal. If the master of the Guild breaks his own seal, then in four years it is a master who did a thing in a temper, and I have watched a man do that in a shed in a different county and it took the shed eleven years to be a shed you could work in again.

"So Margit reads it and copies it and the boy copies it, and I stand at the slot and I do not touch it, and in four years nobody in this basin can say that Sef Ordry broke a seal. They will say the plate fitter did, in a shed, properly, in front of two men, and there are a hundred and forty men in the Guild and about nine of them would have done it that week and most of them would have done it wrong."

Jory Vant said, "I can read the hand."

"You can read the hand," said Sef Ordry, "and that is why you are doing it, and you are nineteen, and in about nine years' time somebody is going to say a boy of nineteen copied a Guild sheet and your name is on it twice in this basin and I do not care, and I have been in a shed long enough to know that the person whose name is on a thing is the person somebody is angry at in about nine years."

---

They walked the top two miles of the flume in the afternoon, all of them. The flume is a stone launder on the low side of the service road for about five miles, and then it leaves the road, crosses a field on three stone piers, and comes back.

Margit Saye found four places in two miles and had them marked with a bit of chalk on a stone before she showed any of them.

"Here the outer course has moved an inch and a half and it is going to move again, and when it moves the launder is a channel and not a pipe. It will not come down tonight, and I will not be able to make anybody believe me about that in a book in eleven years, and the chalk is the only thing I have got."

"Here the crown has come off two stones and there is a hole in the invert about a foot across, and the water will go into it and find its level, and it will be dirty for the first quarter of an hour and then it will be a launder again."

"Here is a post of the road. The flume is against the post and the post is moving, because the post is a bit of oak in the ground since about 1861 and it has the flume's weight on it. I would not build a launder against a post, and I am not going to say who built it."

"And here," she said, and stopped before she said it, and this one she had not marked and marked in front of them, "is the one where it has happened.

"Eleven feet of the invert is gone. The stones are on their sides in the silt. And there has been water in this flume about once in eleven years, which is the year it was laid, and once in the last eleven days, if the thing on a fence in a field is true, and it will be on Monday.

"That is not going to be fixed in a day by a barrow and a bar. And it is not going to stop the water either, because the flume is on a fall of about one in two hundred and the water will fill it and go over, dirty, and in a week it will be a launder again with a barrow-load of stone in the middle of it.

"Write that down as a thing that has happened, not as a thing that has been done. If anybody writes it down as a thing that has been done, I will take the pen off them."

---

The choice was made in about four minutes and it was not a hard one, and Tarin Rusk made it out loud in the shed door at about five o'clock with the light going, and he made it in the wrong order on purpose and then corrected himself.

"There are four things available and one of them is not anybody's idea and I want it said out loud first so that it is in the room. We could not open it. We could count three rods and publish two and a hole, and the hole is the padlock, and that is honest and it is a document and it costs a day, and I am not ruling it out and I would not be ashamed of it.

"The other three are the chain, the padlock and the seal, and the chain goes back on and the padlock goes back on, and I am going to take the seal, and I am not going to take the chain, and the reason is that the chain is a bolt and the seal is a piece of paper, and after Monday there will be no sealed cover on the West Flume sluice in this basin and there never will be again, and in eleven years the only way anybody will find that out is by turning a card over in a shed door, and I would rather the one piece of paper in this basin that says *a keeper is to be named* survive in a village in a chest than die in a shed in my hand."

And then he said the other half of it, which was the part he had actually come to say.

"And I am not one of the two men. Not because I am the wrong man for it. Because I have two live charges and a name in four buildings, and if a man with two charges and a name in four buildings breaks a Guild seal then those two things stop being two things in about nine days, and in eleven weeks the Reach will have a count and a refusal and an amendment in a book and a story about the English engineer who broke the seal, and the story will be better than the count."

Nobody argued with him about that either, and he noticed that as well.

---

The copy was drafted on the Sunday, in two hands, in the room over the shop at Brant, with the date left blank, because the date is the part of it that cannot be written until the wax is off, and Sef Ordry would not have it drafted with a date on it. It is four lines long and it is signed by two people and it says at the top, in Tarin Rusk's hand and under his own line, the thing that is going to have to make itself true in eleven years:

> *This is a copy, made from the sheet behind the sealed inspection cover of the West Flume sluice at Sallow Head, on the fifteenth day of this month, at the breaking of the seal, by two men, the seal being whole at the time of writing and each of them signing while it was whole. The sheet itself is destroyed with the cover. What is copied here is what was read out. It is a copy and it is not the sheet, and the difference between those two things is the whole of the procedure and I am writing it down because in eleven years somebody will need it and I will not be here.
> *Read and signed. MARGIT SAYE, fitter.
> *Read and signed. JORY VANT.*

And the original went back into the split cover in a japanned box in the shed on the Monday afternoon, because the Guild keeps no second cover and there is no form for a destroyed one, and Margit Saye wrote *destroyed on the fifteenth* on the lid in chalk and then wiped it off and wrote it again on the lid in ink and said, "There. Now somebody can find out what happened to it if they ever want to know, which they will not, for about nine years, and then they will all at once."
