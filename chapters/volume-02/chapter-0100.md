# Chapter 0100 — The Day After

The card went back on the post at the culvert at about eleven o'clock on the Friday morning of the twenty-third and a boy of fifteen nailed it there himself, and about two hundred and forty people in that valley could walk up to that post and look at it, and anybody in this basin may look at it.

The third rod was empty. The reason printed under the third rod was a padlock. Under the third rod there was a line with a heading on it and nothing under the heading, and at the foot of the card, in a printer's hand, it said that there was no figure for the line above and that he would not print one.

The fourth rod was at the bottom in a boy's hand with a signature under it, and the signature said that the figure was taken on the third of this month by four persons and a boy of fifteen, and that it was not measured, and that there was no instrument in this basin that would measure it.

And the padlock was still on the inspection cover of the flume sluice, and the chain was on the gate, and the gate was shut.

Nobody in that village took a figure off that card. About nine people in the shop at Brant said out loud, on the Friday, that they had looked at it and that nothing had been taken off it, and one of them said that a man of sixty-eight in a town of about four thousand people had put a sentence at the foot of a card in a valley he had never been to, and that the sentence was the reason there was nothing to take off it, and that he had done it for nothing.

---

The drop came up from Cresset on the Friday morning on a cart with a boy of twenty-two on it who could not read.

The board on the terrace road had a second figure on it under the first one and the second one was smaller, and the sheet the boy carried said in about four lines what the first figure had been, which was the figure for the night, and what the second one was, which is the figure for the morning of the twenty-third, and that any person in the district might come and read it and that the difference was what the Reach's one night had cost nine hundred and forty properties.

"**The North Sallow branch draws off the same tank as the flume.** That is the whole of the mechanism and it is on the flyleaf of a card anybody can walk up to, and the cisterns on that branch have been filling more slowly since about six o'clock this morning and they will go on filling more slowly for about a day and a half, and the board has the drop on it and the board is the only place in this city where anybody has written either figure down.

"There is no figure for how much a household has lost in gallons," said the boy of twenty-two, "because there is no such figure in this basin and there has not been one since the assize, and I have asked for one in two offices this month and been told twice that it is not a form anybody has, and **I have written the two figures side by side on the back of this sheet in chalk and put the date under them and nobody has signed either, because there is nobody in this district who can be made to sign anything, and that is still the truest thing anybody has told me about this city in eight weeks.**"

"Was it a big drop," said the boy of fifteen.

"It was a drop," said the boy of twenty-two. "I cannot tell you what it was, and I have stood in front of it for about a quarter of an hour trying to work out whether I could, and a man of about sixty came out of a building and read the two figures to me twice, slowly, and then said that the difference between them was the whole of the cost of a window in a valley sixty-one miles west, and then said that he was sorry, and then went inside."

---

Nesta Sarg did the plank at eight and at noon and at four on the Friday and the figures were a hundred and ninety and a hundred and ninety-one and a hundred and ninety, and the two trials on each are on her own shutter, and she did not divide them by anything and she did not come to the shop.

The boy of fifteen had been up to the post at the culvert at about two in the afternoon and came down and said that the fifth rod was not on the card, and that a woman of forty-one had told him at the plank on the Sunday that there was going to be a fifth rod on that card after Thursday, and what it was going to say, which is the West Flume at the third pier, six inches wide, not a drain, a channel, depth read and not measured, and that she had said all of it in her own words and that he had written it down on the back of his hand, and that he was not going to cut it in a day and a half with a press four days up a road and a declaration in a book that does not exist in this village.

"She is not going to ask anybody to do it for her," said the boy of fifteen. "**She said it in her own words and she is going to do it in her own hand and she is going to wait for a day when there is a sheet in front of her instead of a shutter, and she has told me that if anybody in this shop cuts that rod for her this week she will not know who did it and she will not be able to be angry at the right man.**"

And Aldis Kerr was seventy-one and was at the drain on the Friday morning on a stick and a granddaughter's arm and nobody asked her to come to a room, and nobody has asked her for anything since the Sunday except for a word in a lane, and there were about nine people in this valley who would have said that the witness box should have been filled on Friday morning, and about four of them said so out loud, and she was not asked, and she was not thanked, and she is seventy-one and she has not been thanked once in this lane in her life and she said on the Monday that she is not going to start on a Thursday.

---

Perdie Ammid's letter to the master of the Open Channel Guild at Bexhill was three lines long and it was on a sheet in a chest in a room over a shop and it was begun on the Friday the sixteenth of this month and it was unfinished on the twenty-third, and there were four days left of the fortnight, and she said out loud on the Friday afternoon that she was not going to finish it on the day after a window, and that a woman of fifty-four has written to a man four times in thirty-one years and three of them were about a book and one of them was about a book that was not hers, and that she was not going to learn a new letter in a week.

---

Wick Venn priced the last two lines in a yard at about four o'clock on the Friday afternoon and it took him about six minutes and it went on the seventh sheet.

"The sixth line is the window and it is five and a half days of four men, and the seventh is the day after and it is a day and a half, and I have priced the barrow and the two-mile walk twice and I have priced a man of nineteen sitting on a stone in the dark from midnight until half past two, and I have priced eleven people in a field from about ten at night until about six this morning, which is a day each and nobody has offered to pay for it and nobody is going to.

"**Four and a half, five and a half, four and a half, one, twelve, five and a half, a day and a half. That is thirty-four and a half days.** And I have written it on a sheet, because the column is in a cart, and there is a number due at the foot of that column in about a year, and that number is in a cart too, and in about a year somebody in a counting-house at Vellum is going to look at a column with seven lines in it and nothing at the foot of it, and I have been thinking about that since about half past four this morning on a bank in the dark and I would like somebody else to have to think about it as well."

---

And then a man of thirty-one came down the bank at about five o'clock in the dark with a day-book under his arm, and it was the shed day-book from the foot of the embankment, nine inches by six, written in from both ends, and he had brought it out of the shed and had not opened it in the shop and he stood by the door.

Perdie Ammid read the entry out, because a man with a bar over his shoulder does not read a day-book aloud in a shop in a valley in the dark.

> *The twenty-second day of this month. The West Flume sluice at this work was opened at eleven o'clock at night and shut at six minutes past three in the morning, being four hours and six minutes, at the figure of eight hundred and fifty gallons an hour, being three thousand four hundred and eighty-five gallons, being the figure agreed by this Reach and not the figure of any other person. The gate was found shut, and chained, and padlocked on the inspection cover, and is left so, and the chain on it is a new one bought by me with my own money, and the chain that was on it for six years is in this shed, and I record both of those things and I am not asking the company for a chain. I have been the party having the care of this work for nine years and the company has not been up this road in six years.*
>
> *The men at the works, at the piers and at the outfall on the night were: S. Ordry, sluicewright, of Bexhill, of no district, at the gate. W. Venn, of Brant, at the outfall. J. Vant, apprentice, of Bexhill, of no district, at the first pier. T. Rusk, of no office, of no district, and in no standing in this Reach, on the coping of the tank with a boy of fifteen and a level staff.*
>
> *A boy of fifteen, of no district, on the coping of the tank, with a slate of his own.*
>
> *— S. Ordry. The twenty-third day of this month, ten in the morning, in the shed.*

Nobody thanked him and he did not ask, and the day-book went back into the shed on the nail-hook it had been on for a hundred and eighty-one years.

And Perdie Ammid wrote a thing on the seventh sheet that afternoon that is the last entry in her hand in this valley for a long time, and it is four lines, and she read it out once.

"**His name is in two books, in two hands, in two places, and neither of them is a record of a route.** One of them is a column headed *days lost, and by whom, and to what* in the four field books of this Reach, in my hand, and the numbered sheet that goes with them in the same satchel, and between the two of them they say one day, taken by T. Rusk, of no office, out of a Saturday morning, and it is in a cart. The other is a day-book in a shed at the foot of an embankment, in a man's hand, and it says four men at three points on a launder and a boy with a slate, and the two places are four hundred yards apart and the two books are going in opposite directions. And that is the whole of it. Nobody has thanked him for either and he is not going to be thanked for either and I am not going to be thanked for writing it down, and I have said that five times this month and I am counting."

---

At dusk on the Friday, the twenty-third of this month, at the top of the lane at Nether Sallow, a man was standing in a field of turned earth with a six-gallon pail and a watch and a roll of tarred twine, filling a butt at the plank.

The butt stood on the edge of the turned earth with its foot in the hole the notch had been dug, and it had been standing empty since the spring and it had a crack in it that had been cemented. The pail was six gallons and it was Nesta Sarg's pail, the one with the mark inside it and the split in the handle repaired with a wire and a bit of pitch, and it is not lent.

The water came off the notch into the pail and the pail went into the butt and the butt filled at the rate of about a hundred and ninety gallons an hour, and a butt is about two hundred and forty gallons of room, and it took about an hour and sixteen minutes and forty trips up a bank, and it will be filled again in the morning.

He had a watch in his left hand and did not use it, because the plank is a plank and the notch is a notch and a man does not need a watch for that.

The roll of tarred twine was on the pail's handle, wound round twice and tucked under. It had been on the pail's handle since the Saturday morning, when it had been given to him in a shop by a boy of fifteen who had got it off a stake at a culvert, where it had been round a tin frame nailed at a right angle to the post, in a frame ninety years old and cut out of a much larger card for something else, and the frame had carried a notice that said **CLOSED. No water. No entry. Under penalty.** and had named no office and named no person, and had been the reason a village's water had been a secret for six years, and it had been cut off that stake, and turned over, and the tin cut out of the frame, on the Tuesday evening of the second of this month by six hands in daylight, and the frame had gone back on the post because a frame is ninety years old and a card is six.

The twine was not cut off. It had come away whole when the notice came away, and it had been put on the pail's handle because a pail needs a handle and a man needs something to do with a length of tarred twine, and it had stayed on the handle through a count, a window, a chain, a card and a declaration.

A man is going to do that every evening now. That is what the butt is for, and it is not a window and it is not a service and nobody has a schedule for it and there is no figure for it anywhere in this basin, and a butt is a day and a half of drinking water for three people, and there are nineteen houses in that lane and forty-three people in them, and nineteen butts is one butt each and nineteen butts is not enough, and the water in all nineteen houses comes out of a butt, and a butt is filled by a man.

There is a day when somebody has to say his name.

Tonight nobody had to. Tonight there was a butt and a six-gallon pail and a roll of tarred twine and a man standing in turned earth at the top of a lane above a drain, and the light going off the top of the hill behind the lane the way it had gone off it on an evening in the middle of this month when nineteen households came down to look at something they had not been told about, and the twine is where the notice was, and the notice is dated the fifteenth, and it was nailed over a plank with a pail under the notch of it for six years, and the pail was not full and not empty, and the water came up out of the notch the whole while.

**And it is the twenty-third, and the fifteenth was a day like this one, and there is no rod on the card at the culvert for a butt in a field, and there is going to be a fifth rod on that card and it is not this.**

He could not leave. There is a butt to fill every evening at about six o'clock until the ground gives a crop or the winter does, and a roll of tarred twine on the handle of a pail that is not lent, and his name in two books in two hands in two places four hundred yards apart, and neither of them is a record of a route, and there is no form in this basin in which a man may say that he is going somewhere he is not going.
