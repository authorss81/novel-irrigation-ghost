# Chapter 0055 — Solid

The shed at the foot of the embankment was thirty-one feet long and it had a bench in it, a stove, a bench-book shelf, and a slate, and it smelled of brass filings and old lamp oil and the particular damp of a stone building that is on the wrong side of a hill.

Sef Ordry put the Guild return of eight years ago on the bench in front of Tarin Rusk and put his own hand flat on it, the way a man does who has read a thing four hundred times and knows exactly which two lines are going to be argued about.

> **Open Channel Guild. Return of works, Sallow Head, Kerrow Water. Eighth year of the present standard, being the year before last.**
> *Item: the culvert at the embankment, carrying the tank overflow under the service road, at the fourth mile.*
> *Found: stone, single, laid dry. No void. No through flow, at either end, to the depth of nine feet with a rod.*
> *The channel is solid to the rod at both ends and to remain so.*
> *No plate is fitted at this item. No instrument is required.*

Sef Ordry let him finish and then said, "That is a good return. I have read a great many bad ones and that is a good one. It is dated. It is signed. It gives a depth, it gives an instrument, it gives both ends, and it gives an instruction, and the instruction is that the thing is to stay as it is, and a man reading that in eight years' time is not being lied to about anything in it."

"It is true," said Tarin Rusk.

"It is true."

"A nine-foot rod in a stone culvert will find stone. That is what a rod does. And the culvert is single-laid dry stone, which has no invert channel, and it has no void, and water cannot come up through nine feet of laid stone. Every one of those five statements is true and the return says all five."

"Yes."

"And it is wet," said Tarin Rusk.

The shed was quiet for a moment. Jory Vant, who was nineteen and had not said anything for an hour, shifted his weight from one foot to the other.

"It is a culvert under a road," said Sef Ordry. "It is on a rising main."

"It is what?" said Margit Saye, from the other end of the bench, with the oilcloth case at her feet.

"It is a rising main," said Sef Ordry. "I have known that for four years and I have never written it down, and I have told two men in a counting-house and been told to put it in a return in plain words, and I have not, and that is the first thing you are going to ask me about and so I am going to say it now, in a shed, in daylight, in front of the man who is going to be asked about it later.

"It goes under the road to get to the high side. You cannot lift water under a road with a culvert. I have built four of them in nine years and I have never once seen it done and I do not believe anybody has. The tank is on the low side. The high side is eleven feet higher. Whatever is coming up under that embankment is being *pushed*.

"And I cannot tell you what pushes it, because I have never had a rod in that chamber and never sent a man into it and never paid for a man to go into it, and the reason is on the card inside this door, and we will come to the card."

He put the return to one side and brought out a second paper, which was a leaf torn out of a bound book with a stub of ribbon still in it, and the leaf was covered in a small upright hand in a column.

"That is the specification book," said Sef Ordry. "Two hundred and thirty-nine plates in this basin. Bore, date, gate, and the man who set it. It goes where the fitter goes and the fitter is the one who has it, and it has been at Bexhill and it is here now, and it is going back on the wagon on Saturday and it is not yours and it is not his, and I am going to say that now, at the top, so that nobody in this room can be accused later of not having known."

Margit Saye put her hand on the case without opening it.

"Situation," she said. "The Sallow Head."

She opened the case and took out a leaf and put it on the bench and turned it round so that it faced the man standing at the other end of the bench, and Tarin Rusk read it, and it said:

> *Sallow Head, intake plate. Three-eighths. Set in the year 40. Set by W. Hesketh, fitter.*

"Three-eighths," he said.

"Three-eighths," said Margit Saye. "And I have been in the ring at the top of that intake on four occasions in nine years, and there is a plate in it, and I have had my thumb in it every time, and I have told my own men it was the wrong plate every time, and I have never once been able to say with certainty that it was the wrong plate, because a plate has a number on the rim and the number on the rim got rubbed off in the fourth year and has been rubbed since.

"But it is half an inch. I am a fitter. I know the feel of a three-eighths and I know the feel of a half, and that is a half, and it was put in by somebody without an entry."

"That is not the interesting part," said Sef Ordry. "Say the interesting part, Margit, because he will get it in a minute and I would rather he got it from you."

Margit Saye took her hand off the case.

"A plate is either in water or it is not," she said. "That is the whole of a gauge plate and a plate fitter will not have anybody tell her otherwise. If a plate is in water, it reads a number. The bigger the bore, the bigger the number, and that is the whole of the difference between one plate and another and it is why your friends in the lower fold write down what a plate says and are telling the truth. There is no head in this basin at which a plate reads dry.

"So when the plate at the Sallow Head reads dry, there is no water at the plate. And the plate is in the ring at the top of the intake chamber, eleven feet above the floor of that chamber, and there is nowhere else for that ring to be, because I have been in it and I have had my arm in it up to here.

"Which means that chamber has less than eleven feet of water in it. And the sluice is shut, and has been shut for six years, and it is chained, and I have seen the chain and I have put my own hand on it. And the gate is upstream of that chamber.

"So the head at the Sallow Head is not in the chamber," said Tarin Rusk. "The head is behind the gate. And I cannot get a rod to a shut gate in that position without going into a chamber that I have been told has nine feet of silt in it, and there is no other way in and there is no instrument in this basin that can be put on the other side of that chain."

"None," said Sef Ordry. "There is none in this basin and I have looked, and there is one in the city of Ostre, four days from here, and it is a hundred and forty years old and it is in a private counting-house and it belongs to a man of eighty-one, and I have written to him about it once."

---

He would not put him in the chamber and he said why out loud in the shed, in daylight, with three other men there, and it took him about twenty seconds and he did not soften it.

"No."

"I have not asked yet."

"You are going to, and I am going to answer it now so that you do not have to make a fool of yourself on Saturday afternoon in front of a woman from the city. No."

"Why not."

"Because you have never set a gate bed in cold water in the dark," said Sef Ordry, "and I have not either, and I have got nine years and a man of thirty-three who has been out of a wheelbarrow in a bad shoulder for six weeks. That chamber is nineteen deep and the silt in it is nine and it is not silt you can stand on, and if it goes over a man it takes him down and holds him and the man next to him cannot get a hook in without going in after him.

"I have had two men in thirty-one years go into something I should not have put them into. I have one left who would go, and it is Jory, and Jory is nineteen.

"You will go in with me on the Friday, because you asked, and I will take you as far as the ladder and you will go down eleven feet and you will hold the bucket and you will not go down another foot, whatever the bucket is doing. And Margit will be on the ladder, because she is the fitter and the fitter goes first, and if there is brass in the bottom of that chamber then it is brass she put there and she will know it before I do."

"And if there is a branch in the wall," said Tarin Rusk, "I am not to see it."

"You are not to see it," said Sef Ordry, "and I will tell you why, and then I will stop talking about it. When a man is in a hole with a lamp and a bar, and a thing comes out of a wall that has no business coming out of a wall, the man's first act is to put his hands on it. And his second act is to open it. And that is not wickedness, that is what a hand is for, and I have done it myself in four countries.

"You have opened three things in your life and I have read two of the transcripts. Both times you had somebody standing next to you who had been asked. Both times there was a record. Both times it was written down and it is in four buildings and a man in a registry has to decide about it this year.

"Down there, in the dark, in nine feet of silt, on a Friday afternoon, with me and a boy of nineteen — you will put your hands on it and you will not stop and there will be nobody to ask. And I am not going to be the man who lets a man with a good head and no crew open a thing in the dark with a bucket in his other hand, and I have said that out loud in a shed with three witnesses and if anybody wants to say it was not said, they may say so in a court."

"I am not going to argue with that," said Tarin Rusk.

"No," said Sef Ordry. "I know. That is why I said it in daylight."

---

The card inside the shed door was on a nail and it was the size of a man's hand and it had been rained on about nine hundred times.

> **SALLOW HEAD. INTAKE CHAMBER. CLEARING AND RODDING.**
> *1st year — cleared, rodded to the sill, silt 2 ft 4.*
> *2nd — cleared, rodded to the sill, silt 2 ft 2.*
> *3rd — cleared, rodded to the sill, silt 2 ft 6.*
> *4th — cleared, rodded to the sill, silt 2 ft 5.*
> *5th — cleared, rodded to the sill, silt 2 ft 7.*
> *6th — cleared, rodded to the sill, silt 2 ft 4.*
> ***SEVENTH —***

There was nothing after the seventh, and the hand on the six entries was Marrow's, and it was eleven years old.

"It is a card," said Margit Saye. "It is a card in a shed door and it is not a record, and if it were burned in this fire no office in this basin could tell me it had existed."

"That is exactly right," said Sef Ordry.

"And the copy is in the volume."

"The copy is in the volume."

"And the volume at Vellum ends at the sixth year," said Margit Saye, "with a tidy line, *cleared, rodded to the sill, silt two feet four*, and a return for the seventh year that says *no works required*, and both of those are true, and between them they say that a chamber has been clear since the sixth year of the standard and nobody in that chamber has had a rod in it in eleven years."

"A card shows what was done," said Tarin Rusk. "A volume shows what was done. And neither of them shows what was not done, and the only place in this basin where the not-done is written down is a piece of card on a nail in a shed, and the shed is at the foot of an embankment, and a man has to go and stand in front of the door to read it."

"Yes," said Sef Ordry.

"And the master of the Guild wrote the sixth entry and has not written the seventh, and he is the master of the Guild, and the card is in the door, and everybody in this basin who could say anything about it works for him."

"Yes," said Sef Ordry, again, and did not say anything else, and the reason he did not say anything else was that he had worked for him for eleven years.

---

Margit Saye had the specification book open on the bench at the end of the afternoon and she was going through it because that is what she does at a bench and not because anybody asked, and she stopped with her finger on a line and read it out.

"Two hundred and eight. Sallow Head, embankment, under the road. Void, no plate fitted, no instrument required."

Margit Saye said: "It is in the back."

"It is in the back of the ninth year, which is this year," said Sef Ordry. "I have had this book nine years and I have read the whole of it four times and I have never once read the back, and the ninth year is the one I am in now."

"A specification book is a book of what a plate ought to be," said Margit Saye. "You go to the entry because you have got a plate out and you want to know what goes back in. Nobody in the history of this trade has ever turned to the back of a specification book in order to find out what a man decided not to put in."

She read it again, and then she read the line above it, which was the two hundred and seventh, and it was a plate.

"It is a good entry," said Sef Ordry. "It is exactly the sort of entry that is only written once and never looked at again."

"And it is a year after the return," said Tarin Rusk, "which says the culvert is solid, and both were written in the same office, and the good return came first and the entry repeated it, and neither of the two men who wrote them knew the other had written anything at all. And both of them were true. And between them they say that there is a stone thing under that embankment with no water in it and no plate and nothing required, and that is a sentence about a piece of road and not about water."

"A stone thing," said Jory Vant.

"A stone thing with nine feet of rod in it and no void and no through flow, and to remain so," said Tarin Rusk. "Which is the return. And the specification book says the same thing in one line and does not know it is saying it. And both of them have been in a drawer in Vellum for eight years and the drawer is on shelf C and the shelf is where the whole of the Sallow Reach is kept, and somebody has had to want a plate out of a drawer to find either of them."

He shut the book. He did not open it again and he did not ask to.

"You are not going to take that book down that road," said Margit Saye.

"No."

"You are not going to write anything down out of that book," she said. "Not a figure, not a bore, not a page."

"No," said Tarin Rusk. "And I would like it noticed that the plate list was part of a price and the price was not paid and the book is in a shed and I am standing next to it, and that is the third time in three months that a man has been in a room with a thing he wanted and has been asked not to have it, and that two of the three were not hostile."

"It is not a price," said Sef Ordry. "It is a book and it is a trade and it is in a shed, and the reason he is asking is that he does not have a copy and a man without a copy is a man who has to keep the original in his head, and I would not have him do that, and I am not going to lend it to him either, and both of those are true."
