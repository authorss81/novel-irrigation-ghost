# Chapter 0066 — Eight Of Nine Villages

The notice went up on the door of the shop at Brant on the Thursday morning of the eleventh day of this month, and it was one side of a sheet, and it was in three hands, and it was the fourth thing put up in a public place in this Reach in eleven days, and every one of the other three had cost somebody something to keep there.

It said what it was going to do, and the date, and the place, and the hour, and the reason, and it said who it was served on, and it said, in the middle of the third line, that the person serving it was *of no office, of no district, and in no standing in this Reach*, which Tarin Rusk had insisted on and which Perdie Ammid had made him put in three times because she said a notice that does not say who the man is has a man in it somewhere and the man in it is always the person who wrote the most.

> *Notice of intention to open the West Flume sluice at Sallow Head, an installation of the Open Channel Guild, on the fifteenth day of this month at eight in the morning, for a service window on the flume.*
> *The parties to this act are: the Open Channel Guild, by service on its fitter; and the nine villages named at the foot, each of which is asked to sign as a party or to refuse in writing.*
> *No village is asked for water. No village is asked to pay. A village that signs is a party and may afterwards be asked what it agreed to and by whom and on what day.*

And at the foot, in a column, the nine: Brant, Nether Sallow, Bell Sarg, Fenn's, Wyle, Cold Ashton, Dray, the two villages on the Kerrow lane, and Marl.

It went out on the Friday with the Bexhill cart, and Margit Saye put the Guild's copy in a Guild envelope and sealed it herself and did not ask for it back.

---

Eight of the nine came back inside four days, in writing, in their own hands, and every one of the eight refusals was correct, and not one of the eight was a refusal of the water.

That was the part that nobody had expected, including Tarin Rusk, and it was Nesta Sarg who put it in one sentence on the Friday evening in the front room of the shop with about twenty people in it.

"None of them said no to the window," she said. "All eight of them said no to the paper. And they are the same thing and they are not the same thing, and about four people in this room think they are the same thing and I would like it said out loud that they are not."

A village that signs the notice becomes a party. A party can be asked what it agreed to. And the thing it agreed to is that on the fifteenth day of this month, at eight in the morning, an installation belonging to the Open Channel Guild will be opened, and a channel will run, and whatever happens on the flume and in the long drain and in nine hundred acres of apple for four days happens to a document that nine villages are named in.

Marl's was two sentences and the hand was a clerk's. Cold Ashton's was one sentence and the hand was a man's of about seventy. The two villages on the Kerrow lane refused in the same words on the same paper with the same pen, which is not a copy, and which Armist Sarg signed and Nesta Sarg did not sign, and which is now in four buildings and in a chest at Brant on facing pages in the second book.

The two reasons nobody expected came from Fenn's and from Wyle.

Fenn's wrote to say that no person in that village had ever signed anything in a matter concerning the works, and that the reason was a matter of about 1848 in which a village signed a schedule and the schedule was used nine years later to shut a tap, and that the village had lost the tap and had not had water since and had not had a row either, because a village that has been wronged once and does not row is a village that other people trust, and they were not going to spend that on a window.

Wyle wrote to say that it would sign if it were asked what it was agreeing to, and that nobody in this basin had ever asked anybody that, and that if somebody asked it, in writing, with a date on it, then Wyle would sign on the second of the month after this one, and that in the meantime Wyle would not sign, and that Wyle would like its refusal to be read out.

And it was read out. On the Friday evening, by Perdie Ammid, in the front room of the shop, in full, and Wyle's was the third one read and it got the quietest attention in the room, and a man at the back said afterward that he would not have signed either, and Nesta Sarg said, "You would not have signed either because nobody asked you what you were agreeing to, and that is the fault of the notice and not of the man, and the notice was written by him," and looked at Tarin Rusk, and he agreed with her in about a second and a half.

---

The ninth signature was given on the Friday morning, in the room over the shop, in front of eleven witnesses, and it was given by a woman of fifty-four who had paid for the printing of her own notice and who had put four books on a table in that room on the twenty-seventh day of the month before and lost the only thing she had.

"It is signed," she said, "and I am going to read what signing it does, and then nobody is to thank me for it, because I have found out in the last four days that a person in this Reach who is thanked gets thanked and then stops, and I am fifty-four and I have got nine days of the ground before the fifteenth and I am not going to spend one of them being a woman who was brave.

"It makes this village a party. It means that after Monday, when somebody wants to know what happened on that flume and in that drain, this village is a document and cannot answer. It means that the eight villages that refused are right and that this one is not, and that in four years somebody will make that into a story about the village that was first, and I would like it said now in this room that there is a woman of sixty-three behind this counter who has taken money for the chairs for thirty-four years and has never once taken a side, and that she is not taking one now.

"And it means that from Monday I am not a person who keeps a book. I am a person who is named on a piece of paper, and those are not the same, and the difference is the whole of my life and I did not know that until Wednesday."

She signed it, and eleven people witnessed it, and Bess Ilk wrote *witnessed* and the eleven names, and then she asked for the sheet to be pinned above the cut card at the culvert and the boy of fifteen nailed it up himself, because he is on the roster and the roster is the only thing in this Reach that has been kept to.

---

The witness box is the fifth box on a notice that Perdie Ammid printed at Culm on the eighteenth day of the month before, and on the eighteenth it said *reach* and *works* and left *source*, *keeper* and *witness* empty, and on the twenty-seventh she said out loud in a room of forty-one people that the number that would go in the witness box is four years, and Aldis Kerr said, put it in the diary and do not ask me, and nobody asked her.

On the Friday, with the notice going out, Tarin Rusk put the five boxes on the table in the room over the shop and asked the room, in daylight, with the doors open, for a witness.

He said what a witness was for, and he said it correctly, because Nesta Sarg had said it in a cottage in this village eleven days ago and he had written it down: not somebody who watched, everybody watched; somebody who will still be drinking out of it when the argument is finished.

Forty-one people were in that room on the twenty-seventh. There were about thirty-four on the Friday.

Nobody stood up.

It took about forty seconds and then a woman at the back said the thing that everybody in the room was thinking and had not said, and it was Margit Saye, who had come down from the works for it and who stood at the back with her case under her arm.

"You have all been asked and I have been asked," she said, "and the reason nobody is going to stand up is not that it is dangerous and not that nobody qualifies. It is that in about four days a flume sluice is going to be opened and a great deal of water is going to come down a channel that has not carried any in six years, and if a person's name goes in that box then for the rest of their life there is a piece of paper in a village that says they were standing at that flume when it ran.

"And a thing like that is not refused out of modesty. It is refused because a person can see perfectly well what the next four years are going to be like, and I would refuse it in front of this many people and I would not be ashamed, and I have said no to a stranger's request in a shed in this basin in my life and I did not enjoy it."

And then Tarin Rusk said the rest of it, and Perdie Ammid said the rest of it, and the room did not argue with either of them because neither of them was a person who was asking for something.

"The oath," said Perdie Ammid.

Nobody in that room knew what she meant and about four of them had been on a bank at the third mile and one of those four was in this room.

"It is on two crossing stones at the third mile below the intake, and it is two hundred and fifty years old, and it has four lines on it, and I have known about it for eleven days, and so has the man who read it out on that bank with five people in front of him, and I have refused to have it read out in an assembly and I stand by that and I am going to say the rest of it in a room of thirty people on a Friday because I have run out of year.

"The third line is the only reason that stone matters. It says a man who takes water of the Sere is to be *named among the takers, so that a man who is thirsty can come to the door of the man who has the water*.

"That is a witness. That is the oldest witness clause in this basin and it is cut in stone and it is a hundred and thirty years older than my chest, and if the Reach had been keeping it, the fifth box on that notice would have been full in March, because the taking is going to be named on the day and not argued about in a room in November.

"We have not been keeping it. Aldis Kerr was eleven. I have never seen it. The Reach does not know it exists, and the man who read it out on that bank did not tell the assembly, because I asked him not to, and I was right to ask him, and I want it understood that I have been right about the stone and I have been wrong about the keeping of it, and both of those are mine, and I am not asking anybody to say anything about it, and I would rather somebody did say something about it in about four years than now."

Nobody said anything about it for about eleven seconds, which is a long time.

"The fourth line is worse," said Tarin Rusk. "And I am the one who has to say it, and I am not going to pretend I have not worked out what it means in nine days.

"*I will not take it in a year when the taking is easier and the giving is not.* That is Monday. I have known that since the Thursday before last and I have not said it in a room, and the reason the second half of it cannot be done is not a legal difficulty. It is that the giving is not ready, and the giving is not ready because eight of nine villages have just written in their own hands that they will not be a party to it, and a road with one village on it is not a road anybody can take water down on Monday.

"And the third line cannot be done either, and this is the whole of it, and I have been in this Reach ten days and I have not said it out loud until now. The third line says the takers are to be named. The takers are a man of thirty-three with no office, no district, eleven pence, two live charges, a name in four buildings, who is paid by the villages out of no water account, and who could not vote in any assembly in this Reach if he tried, and a man of thirty-eight with one hand, and a woman of forty-six from Bexhill who is here on four days' notice, and a boy of fifteen who is on a roster and is not named.

"That is a list of four people who can be sent for. That is a list of people whose door a thirsty man is to knock on, and a list of people whose door a thirsty man is to knock on is a list of debtors, and eight villages have written in their own hands this week that they will not go on a paper, and I have just asked thirty-four people in a room to put their names in a box, and there is not one of those two things that is going to happen today and I would like everybody to understand that I have not asked them to do anything wrong, and that I have asked them for the one thing in this basin that nobody has ever been able to give anybody."

And then Perdie Ammid said the sentence about the chest, and she said it flatly, and she had thought about it for two days.

"The other reason the third line cannot be done is in the chest in my front room, and you sleep twenty feet off it, and you have slept there ten nights."

Tarin Rusk did not say anything.

"You are not going to tell me that you did not look," said Perdie Ammid. "And you are not going to tell me that you did. Both of those are the same answer from a man who knows what is in a chest twenty feet from his head, and I have decided that I would rather have neither, and that I have not told anybody in this room what is in the chest, and that is correct, because on Sunday at four o'clock there is a count going on a card and one line of that card has a reason under it, and that is the only place in this basin where that sentence is going to be written this month, and the day after it is written, anybody who has been in a village in this Reach can go and read it."

That was the whole of the Friday, and the notice went out at two o'clock in the afternoon on the cart, with eight refusals and one signature on it, and the eight refusals went up the road in a bundle tied with string, and the one signature went with them loose, because Perdie Ammid said that if the two were tied together then in four years somebody would be able to say that the one village that signed did it because the eight that refused were tied in with it.
