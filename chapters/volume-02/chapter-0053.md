# Chapter 0053 — Two Days West

They were on the road at ten past four on the Friday morning and out of the second gatehouse by twenty past, and the gatehouse watchman walked the whole length of the yard with them because the regulation requires it and because he wanted to.

The gatehouse yard smelled of the gate and of a man who had been in it since two o'clock, and the ground outside the arch was wet for about four feet and then dry, and the dry began where somebody had once carried a hundred yards of broken flags from the top of the mill road to make a path and had stopped.

"Six miles on the mill road and then the cattle track," Wick Venn said. "The cattle track joins the Kerrow lane at the lime kiln, and the lane is nine miles and there is a toll bar at the top of it, and after the bar it is a service road and the service road is a Guild road and you pay for it whether you are in a wagon or on your legs."

They went up the mill road at a walk for the first two miles, because the greys were young and the wagon's rope-bound tire had a habit, and Tarin Rusk walked on the second cart with his back to the direction of travel because it was the only seat going that way and because he had discovered in about four hundred yards that the cart's rail was a rail and not a handhold.

He tried it twice. The first time nothing happened at all and he understood why nothing had happened, which is that his hand had closed on nothing and closed completely and correctly on a piece of wood his thumb was not against.

The second time he got two fingers round the rail and could not get the third.

He took his hand off and put it in his coat and did not say anything about it, and Margit Saye, who was sitting on the case of plates with her back to the front board, said:

"How long."

"Since the seventh of the month before last," said Tarin Rusk.

"That is eleven weeks."

"Eleven weeks exactly. Day seven to yesterday, and I have been counting it since the seventh of the month before last and it has not wanted a day."

"Then you are not going to be able to take a rope on this journey," said Margit Saye, "and I am not going to ask you to, and I would like it understood that I am not being kind about it. I have four men at Bexhill who cannot do the work of this journey either and I have learned to be grateful for the particular thing they can do, and you are the fourth man on a cart with a bad hand and a good head, and that is a person, and I would like the cart to get to the works."

---

Niko Venn had not come and had not been asked twice.

He had stood in the arch at twenty past four with a chalk in his hand and a line on the coping and the second line under it that said *not a reading of a stable tank*, and he had said, when Tarin told him he was not coming, "Then I will hold the count and you will not write to me about it, because I have got it."

He had four pebbles in a tin on a shelf in his aunt's old room and he did not know what they were for and he had stopped looking, and on the Friday morning he also had a job, which was to be the only person in that lane with a stick in his hand and a reason to use it, and which Perdie Ammid had not known about and which nobody had offered him and which he had taken.

---

The Kerrow lane climbed for four miles and then stopped climbing, and the climbing was the whole of the second day.

There were about forty acres of apple on the eastern side of the lane, on ground that had been in an orchard since before anybody living had a right to it, and the trees were in leaf and the grass between the rows was burnt to a pale straw colour, and there was a wind pump standing in the middle of the top block with its sails still and its tail vane hanging.

Jory Vant asked about the wind pump, because he was nineteen and had never seen one that was not turning.

"That is the whole of the reason you cannot use it," said Sef Ordry. "It is a wind pump. There has been no wind on this road for four days that I have stood in, and when there is wind it is not the sort of wind a wind pump wants, and there has not been a sort of wind a wind pump wants since the spring before last. That wheel will not turn again until somebody puts a new vane on it and somebody gets the ground under it into a state where the tail can pull, and neither of those is work a village can do in a fortnight."

"I have been told the wind in the high country is not what it was," said Jory Vant.

"You have been told that by somebody in a city," said Sef Ordry, "and they are right, and it is not the wind that has changed, it is what we have done to the ground the wind comes down on, and I have been a sluicewright for nine years and I can tell you that a catchment with a flume on it and no trees on the flume takes the rain in about a third of the time it did, and the mountain villages know it and the cities have never once asked them."

Nobody said anything else about it, and Sef Ordry went on loading the cart, and Tarin Rusk wrote it in the blue book at the top of a page, and the page heading was **Kerrow lane** and there was nothing under the heading but that.

There was a farm at the third mile where a woman of about thirty was walking the boundary with a dog and looking at a hedge, and the dog was not interested in anything, and she stopped and looked at the wagon and then at the four men on it and then at the passenger.

"You are the reader," she said. It was not a question.

"Somebody put up a notice and I answered it," said Tarin Rusk.

"You did not answer it. A woman in Brant did." She scratched the dog behind one ear and did not look at him again. "I have got four hundred trees on this ground and I have got no water for them since the second year and I am not going to be able to set fruit this season either, and if you write anything down about this ground, write the name on the top, because I have four hundred trees in a book in Vellum under one name and my husband's grandfather's brother called it something else, and I do not know which is right and neither does he."

"What is it in the book?"

"Dray Fenn," she said. "And it is Fenn, and it has been Fenn in every paper my husband's family has ever had, and it is not Fenn on the ground, because the ground is not Fenn, it is what the ground has always been called, and I would not have told you that for a farthing in a city."

"Then say it to somebody in a village," said Tarin Rusk.

"There is a lane two days west of here called Nether Sallow," she said, "and there is a Sarg woman in it with a brother on this road who has not spoken to her in four years, and it is over a paper as well, and you will get neither of them to say so on a highway, and I have tried.

---

They were at the toll bar at about two in the afternoon, and the bar was a boom across a lane with a hut on the low side and a dog that did not get up, and the man in the hut was about sixty and had a book and the book was not a toll book.

"Guild road," he said. "Farthing a wheel."

"Six wheels," said Sef Ordry.

"Six farthings." He took them in a box on the sill, and then he put his hand on the book, and then he took it off the book.

"Which wagon is this."

"The Guild's. Ammid's boy."

"Then it is free, and I am sorry I said six farthings, and I am not going to take it back off you." He shut the book. "You are going down to the works."

"We are."

"Then you will come back up this bar in a fortnight and ask me what I have seen, and I will tell you what I have seen, and I will not tell you what I have been told, and you will be able to tell the difference, because a thing I have been told comes back inside a month and a thing I have seen comes back when it comes back."

He wrote something in the book with a stub of pencil, and did not read it out, and would not be asked, and would have written down anything.

---

They came out of the valley between three and four in the afternoon of the second day, and the whole of the Sallow Reach went away in front of them at once, which is a thing that happens once in a life.

It is not dramatic. It is a very large, very flat, very green thing with a low line of hedges across it, and the river at the bottom, and the flumes and leats drawn on the hillside like string, and the villages small and pale and mostly not visible, and the whole of it in a heat that had nothing behind it.

"There," said Wick Venn. "That is the Reach. And that is the Sere, and that is the Lower Sere, and I have worked that river in nine years and there was water in it every year of them, including the year the Reach water failed, and I have never been able to make those two facts sit together and neither can anybody in this cart."

"They are not two facts," said Sef Ordry. "They are one fact and a half. A river is a river. What fails in a Reach is not the river, it is the taps off it, and the taps off it are on a schedule, and the schedule was amended six years ago, and I have read the amendment, because I read everything, and I can tell you exactly what it says and I can tell you it is lawful."

"And I can tell you what it is worth," said Wick Venn, "because I have three farms on that flat with a tap off a flume, and in the second year I got two days of water and in the third I got none, and in this year I have asked twice and been told the works are dry."

"The works are dry," said Sef Ordry.

"That is what I have been told."

"Then you have been told the truth," said Sef Ordry, "and I am the man who has been saying it for six years, and I would like you to notice that I have been saying it in a shed at Bexhill to about nine men and that nobody has ever written it down, and that a man on a road saying it is worth a good deal less than a man in a shed saying it, and that this is the entire reason the Reach has had no water and it is not a mystery."

---

They were on the lane above Nether Sallow at about a quarter past seven on the Saturday evening, and there were nineteen households in that lane, and the light was going off the top of the hill behind them, and the first thing anybody in the wagon said was not a question.

Somebody had put an extra lamp on a post at the head of the lane, about the height of a man's hat, and the lamp was a storm lantern with the glass clean, and about forty yards below it a woman was standing in the road with her hands behind her back.

"That is her," said Wick Venn.

Perdie Ammid had walked eleven miles to meet a wagon.

She was smaller than she had been in a yard in the city and she had a shawl over the four books, and she did not greet anybody, and she looked at the five men and the passenger for about four seconds.

"Right," she said. "That is four men and a reader. Sef, Margit, Jory, Wick, and you. The cottage is the one with the butt in the yard and the butt is full and I have not had a butt full since the spring before last, and I want you to notice that I have not had a butt full since the spring before last and that I have put it there for a man who might not have come."

Then she said the four words.

"We want the water counted."

And then she said, to nobody, to the lamp and the lane and the dark behind the hill:

"And I am not going to say any more about that on a road, and if anybody in this wagon has understood it as an order, they have understood it wrong, and it will take them a week to be sure about it and that is the correct length of time."

---

They walked down the lane for three hundred yards, because there was one thing to see and there was no wagon that could go further and no light either, and Tarin Rusk had a bar under his arm that he did not use and a chalk stub in his coat that he did use, and Margit Saye had a lamp.

There was a trough at the top of a drain at the bottom of the three hundred yards, and the drain ran east under a cart track, and there was water in the trough, and there was a plank across the drain a foot above the trough with a notch cut in it, and there was a pail under the notch, and the pail was not full and it was not empty.

And the water was coming *up* out of the notch in the plank, and going into the pail, and the pail was on a stone, and the stone was wet, and the stone was wet on the side that faced away from the drain.

Margit Saye said, "That is a rise main."

"That is a rise main," said Tarin Rusk, "and I am going to write down four things about it tomorrow and I am not going to tell any of you tonight what they are, and one of them is that there is a notice nailed to that post and it has been there since we came down the last hundred yards and I have not read it, and I am going to read it now."

The post was a stake, and the nail was a four-inch nail, and the notice was a printed card in a frame of tin, and the tin had gone the colour of a slate roof and the print had gone with the weather off it but not off the tin.

> **BY ORDER. This structure is CLOSED. No water. No entry. Under penalty.**

There was no office named. There was no signature. There was no date, except that the tin had been cut out of a much larger piece and the cut edge was oxidised to the same colour as the rest of it, and the whole card had been under a roof.

And on the back of it, which they turned round because the back is where the date of a notice always is, there was a date in ink, and the ink had gone the colour of the front, and the date was six years old.

"Read it out," said Margit Saye.

"Closed, no water, no entry, under penalty," said Tarin Rusk. "No office. No name. Six years old." He put his thumb on the tin and looked at the nail. "The nail is not six years old. The nail is rusted through at the head and the head has been hammered back up again once, by somebody, in a temper, and then it went down again and it has been down since. The card is not nailed to this post. It is nailed to a frame, and the frame is nailed to the post, and the frame is older than the card by about ninety years, and this is a notice about something that was closed in a frame that was put up for something else."

Sef Ordry turned the card over again and looked at the back.

"It names no gate," he said.

"It names no gate at all," said Tarin Rusk. "It says *this structure*. And I have been in this trade for eleven years and I have read a great many of these, and a notice that says *this structure* is a notice put up by a man who has come to a thing he does not understand and put up whatever card was in the frame, and that is a road notice, and it belongs to a road man, and the road man is the man at the bar with a book."

"So it is nothing," said Jory Vant.

"It is six years old and it is nailed over a rise main," said Tarin Rusk, "and in six years nobody in this lane has taken it down, and the pail is under the notch and the pail is not full and not empty, and those are two facts that have been sitting in the same place for six years and neither one has ever seen the other.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we are going to take the pail off and put it back with a watch on it, and we are going to do it twice, and then we are going to find out who wrote on the back of that card, because somebody has, and the ink is not six years old."

Nobody walked back up that lane in a hurry. A woman of about sixty was standing at a door eleven houses up, in the dark, with a shawl over her head and a stick in her hand, and she had been there for some time.
