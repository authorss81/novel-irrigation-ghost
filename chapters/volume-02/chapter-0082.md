# Chapter 0082 — The Day It Named

The clerk of about sixty at Wyle had the paper in a drawer and had had it in the drawer for twenty-three days, and on the second day of next month, which was a Friday, he took it out at about nine in the morning and put it on the table in front of the boy of fifteen and told him to sit down, which nobody at Wyle had ever told him to do.

Brant is a village of about two hundred people four miles down the Kerrow lane. Wyle is a village of about two hundred people four miles the other way. Between them there is a lane, a toll bar with a man of sixty-one in it, and a clerk of about sixty who writes refusals.

The paper is the notice. It is a single side of a sheet and it has been round nine villages since the tenth of the month before this one and it came back with eight refusals tied in a bundle and one signature loose, and the one was Brant's, and Brant's was signed by a woman of fifty-four and the sentence under it said that Brant is a document after Monday, which was a thing she said in a room and not a thing anybody asked her to say.

The boy of fifteen carried it up the lane at about eleven on the Friday morning of the second and put it on the table and did not go out.

"Eight villages refused in writing," said the clerk. "One signed. That is the arithmetic and it is correct and it is in a book in your village with the date on it and you may go back and tell her so, and I have not sent it up anywhere and I am not going to until this is signed, because a return of one signature and eight refusals is a different thing from a return of one signature and no refusals, and I do not know which of those two my clerk at the county would rather have and neither do you."

"Sign it," said the boy of fifteen.

"I did not say I was going to sign it for Wyle," said the clerk. "I said I was going to put it on the table. Sit down."

He put his pen down beside it and turned the paper about so that it faced him, and he read the whole of it, and he did it slowly, and it took him about two minutes, and the boy of fifteen said afterwards that he had never in his life seen a man read a paper he had already written.

"Read it out," he said, when he had finished.

"I have not read it out since the tenth day of the month and I have read it out in three rooms and to myself in this room more than twice and I have lost count, and a man who has lost count of how many times he has read a paper out has stopped being able to say what the paper is worth. It goes up signed and it does not go up read."

Then he said the thing he had said he would say, and the boy of fifteen wrote it down in a hand that is not the clerk's and carried it down the lane afterwards and had it entered in the wages column of the second book of a village with a date against it.

"Wyle said on the twelfth of the month before this one that it would sign on the second of next month, and Wyle has not been asked anything since the twenty-fourth, and I want that said about the twenty-fourth: nobody has asked Wyle anything since the twenty-fourth. A letter came down the lane on the twenty-fourth and it was four lines and it asked what Wyle was agreeing to, and Wyle answered it in a whole side, and the answer came back up the lane on the Friday, and there has been nothing since the twenty-fourth. Nine days. Nine days of nothing, and a village is a very easily moved thing when it is quiet, and Wyle was going to be easy to move until somebody wrote to it and gave it a date to hold on to.

"So today is the second and today is a Friday and the paper is signed on the day Wyle named and not a day earlier and not a day later. **A village that signs a paper on a Tuesday because somebody came on a Wednesday is a village that a man in an office can move. A village that signs a paper on the day it named, in front of the man who asked, with the twenty-fourth in between, is a village that cannot be moved for about eleven weeks, and eleven weeks is longer than the county clerk's patience and that is the whole of the trick and it has been the whole of the trick since before either of us was born.**"

He picked up the pen.

"I am going to write *Wyle* at the bottom of it and the date, and nothing else, and no name of mine, because you have told me twice on the road that the clerk's name is not on it and was not asked for and I have never in my life had a paper go up without my name on it and I am going to do without it today, and I would like it entered that I did it without it and did not argue about it, because the day a village's name is on a paper with a man's name on it is the day the man can be moved and the village cannot."

---

It took him about four minutes, and the pen went down twice and was lifted once, and at one point the boy of fifteen said that the clerk of about sixty had stopped writing and had put both hands flat on the table, and the clerk said that he had been about sixty for eleven years and had written nine hundred papers for that village and had never once known on the day of writing what a paper was going to do in four years, and that he knew now, and that it was going to sit in a drawer in a counting-house for a hundred and thirty years and be read by somebody who had not been born.

He did not say what it would do.

He signed it. He put the date on it, the second day of next month, and a Friday, and he turned it about and read the whole of it once more, silently, with his lips, and then he put it in an envelope and sealed it and put the seal across the flap with his thumb, and it was not a Guild wafer and it was not wax, it was the crease of an envelope and his own name on it, and that was correct.

"Now take it down the lane," he said, "and put it in the shop, and tell the woman I said the date and not the day, because she will want to write both and only one of them is a fact."

---

What the boy of fifteen said at the shop door on the Friday afternoon, which was the clerk's sentence and the boy's memory of it, took about nine seconds to say and it is in the second book of Brant on a line of its own, and it is the whole of what happened at Wyle on the second of next month.

"He said: you have signed a paper and you have not been asked to sign a water and you have not been asked for a man, and you are going to be a party to a paper, and a party can be asked what it agreed to and can answer and can refuse and can be sued and can be shut, and if a man in a counting-house in four years asks you what Wyle agreed to, you will be able to say a paper, and that is the whole of it and it is a very great deal more than there was on the twenty-fourth, and I have been thinking about it since the twenty-fourth and I have come to the conclusion that I do not mind it, and that is a thing I have thought about and not decided, and I would like it written down with the date on it that I have thought about it and not decided it."

Then he took the envelope and put it inside his coat and went down the lane, and it is four miles and he did not stop and he was at the shop at about half past twelve, and Perdie Ammid did not open it until Bess Ilk was behind the counter, and Bess Ilk read it out because she reads everything that goes across that counter, and there were about nine people in the shop, and it is the ninth document in about a month in that shop with a date on it and a name at the foot of it, and it is the first one anybody in that room has signed on the day it was asked for.

And nobody was told anything about the water, because there is no water in it.

---

On the Friday afternoon a man of thirty-three came down the lane from the mountain on foot and put a hand on the table in the shop at about four o'clock and said, "It is signed."

"It is signed," said Perdie Ammid. "And I did not send for it, and I did not ask for it, and I have had a day and a half of knowing that it was going to be signed today because a woman of sixty wrote a whole side about it on the twenty-fourth and put a date in it, and I have been sitting in this room for two days with that knowledge and I have not used it, and I would like the room to hear all three of those sentences in one breath and then I would like nobody to ask me about any of them.

"You are going to ask me why. I am going to tell you and then it is finished. **Because a party signs for itself.** You have spent a month building parties out of sentences. You built one out of a printed fifth line and it went in a stove. You built one out of three men's reasons and it went in about five hours and those men had a bad Saturday out of it. And on the twenty-fourth I put a request in this book with your name at the top of it and it is a request and it is not a record of a route, and I have said so in a book and a shed and I am not going to say it again.

"And today a village signed a paper about an act, on the day it named, in front of the man who asked, and it signed it for itself and it was not asked for a man and it was not asked for water, and there is a village in this basin that has got a party and it is not yours, and I would like you to notice that I have not congratulated you about it, because it is not a thing to be congratulated about. It is a thing that has happened to a village that cannot be moved, and it happened on time, and nobody in this room did it."

Tarin Rusk stood at the table for a while.

"What do I do with it," he said.

"That is the wrong question and you have known it was the wrong question since Wednesday," said Perdie Ammid, "and you asked it in front of nine people on purpose, because you wanted somebody else to answer it, and I am not going to, and Bess is not going to, and the boy is not going to.

"Go and find out whether the men at the end of it can stand at a gate. That is not my sentence. That is the only sentence I have got left in this month and it is a fitter's and it is not mine and I would have said it Wednesday if I had thought of it, which I did not."
