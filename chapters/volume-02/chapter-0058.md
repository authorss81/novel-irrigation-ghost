# Chapter 0058 — The Jobs Nobody Wants

The gate bed at Culm is nine inches wide and twenty-two feet long and the deposit on it was four inches deep and had set like mortar, and on the Saturday morning of the thirtieth day of this month six men spent eleven hours on it with scrapers and a bar and a bucket, and by four o'clock in the afternoon twenty-two feet of bed was clean stone. Six men, eleven hours, twenty-two feet: about four inches of length apiece in a minute of working time, and that is the whole of the arithmetic of the trade and I have it on a board in a shed.

The heat had been going on since the middle of the month before and had not let up for a day. In the shade at ten o'clock it was thirty-four degrees. On the flags in the sun it was not measurable with the glass they had.

Tarin Rusk did a foot and a bit in the first hour and got to about four feet an hour after that, which is the pace of a man who has never done it and is being careful, and which was the worst of the six by a long way, because the other five were at eight and ten by the middle of the morning and had been at it in their teens. Nobody said so. Everybody could see it. By the middle of the morning Sef Ordry had taken the bar off him and given him a shovel and a barrow and had said nothing whatever about the change, and that was the correct decision made in about a second and a half.

"I have scraped for nine years," said Margit Saye, who was not scraping and was on the flume gate with a book. "Two things. You hold the scraper wrong, and I will show you once and then never again. And you are trying to take four inches off in one pass, and nobody takes four inches off in one pass, and the reason nobody can is that the deposit has set in layers and you have to take the top layer and then the second layer and if you take both at once you put a corner through the stone, and a corner through the stone in a gate bed is a new bed."

"How do I know where the layers are."

"You cannot know," said Margit Saye, "and that is why there is a man in the Guild who does nothing but scrape gate beds and he is forty-four and he earns a pound a day and there are not six of him in this basin, and every one of them learned it in a year, and the year is spent taking a quarter of an inch at a time and being laughed at by a man of thirty-three who is holding it wrong."

The scraper is a flat bar about fourteen inches long with a handle turned at one end, and it is worked with the whole hand and not with the fingers, and Tarin Rusk could not hold it with two fingers on the handle and the third over the top because the two-finger press he has used since he was thirteen has not been there since the seventh of the month before last, and the hand simply closed on the handle in the only way it had, and it closed wrong.

He worked with his palm and the heel of his hand and the corner of the bar on its edge, and it took him until about two in the afternoon before he stopped doing it in four-inch strokes, and Jory Vant, who was behind him for two of those hours, said nothing until four o'clock, and then said:

"You've stopped."

"I have stopped."

"You've stopped because your hand went," said Jory Vant, not unkindly. "I've watched you do it four times today. It isn't going to come back in an hour and you could have said so at eleven o'clock and got a shovel an hour earlier."

"I have not been in a scrape in eleven years," said Tarin Rusk.

"Then you are in a bad trade and I am in a good one, and neither of us is going to be a keeper of anything on the strength of it," said Jory Vant, and went back to it, and that was the whole of the conversation.

---

The reason the crew began to trust him was given out loud once, on the flags at about half past four, in about twenty words, by Sef Ordry, and it was never said again.

"You counted the water in a lane at the top of your own hill when everybody in your city was telling you there was none," he said, "and you wrote the number down when the number was against you."

"I wrote it down because I had to," said Tarin Rusk.

"I know," said Sef Ordry. "That is why."

And Margit Saye, who was nailing the lid on the flume gate and did not turn round, said, "I did not know that and neither of you has said a word of it to me in six years, and I am not going to be a person about it now."

And Tarin Rusk said, "I would rather somebody did not know it, and I would rather somebody did not tell it, and I am not going to pretend that is a virtue."

"No," said Sef Ordry. "It is not. It is the only useful thing about you and it is not a virtue. Have the barrow out of the cut before the light goes."

---

The condition about the specification book came at six o'clock, on the flags, in the same voice, and it was Margit Saye and she had been carrying it since the second day and had chosen a place with a witness in it.

"It is Guild property. It has been Guild property since 1871 and before that it was the company's and before that it was a man's, and it has never been anybody's else. You have stood next to it in a shed for three days and you have not asked to copy it, and I have been waiting for you to ask, because everybody asks, and I have had a man put a page in his coat in this basin and I have had a man take a plate out of the shed and sell it in the city, and both of them asked me first, which is the thing that everybody thinks is the protection and is not.

"So here it is. I am going to have to write to my own master about a stranger who has spent three days in a shed with an open specification book and has not asked for anything out of it. That letter is going to be a page long and it is going to say that nothing has been taken, and I am going to have to put my own hand to it, and I am telling you I am going to have to do it so that you cannot be surprised by it in a week's time.

"Which is your business and not mine, and the reason I am saying it now rather than in a week is that I would rather say it on flags than in an office."

"I will pay for it," said Tarin Rusk. "I will pay for a copy of two hundred and thirty-nine entries and for the time of whoever writes them out, and I will not take the book out of the shed, and you can take the money and buy the book back if you like."

"No," said Margit Saye.

"That is not a very good answer."

"It is a very good answer and it is for two reasons and I am only going to give you the first. If you pay for a copy, then a copy of the Sallow Reach leaves the Guild, and I do not know what a copy of the Sallow Reach is worth, and neither do you, and I have been in this trade for twenty-four years and I would still not put a number on it, and a thing nobody can put a number on is not a thing to be sold.

"And the second reason is that if you do not pay for it, then in five years' time there is a man in a shed with a book he was not given, and somebody will ask, and the answer will be *no, he did not pay for it either*, and that answer is going to sound exactly like mine."

---

They went up to the tank on the Saturday evening because Margit Saye said the tank card was due and Tarin Rusk said he had never seen one and she said, "Then you are going to stand there while I do it, and you are going to hold the staff, and you are not going to help, because a man who helps is a man who reads the number before it is read."

The tank is twenty feet by fifteen feet and two feet eight inches to the coping, and the coping is nine inches thick, and it is built of coursed stone with a lime joint, and it is on the low side of the service road with the embankment behind it and the low lane below that, and it was built in 1843 and it has been patched eleven times, four of them badly, and it is the best-built thing anybody in the party had ever put a hand on.

Three hundred feet of floor. A foot of water is seven and a half gallons in this basin. Two feet eight inches is eight hundred feet of water and **six thousand gallons of room**, and Margit Saye did that sum out loud at the tank with a stick against the wall, and did it again, and made the boy of fifteen do it.

The card is on a board inside the lid of a japanned box and the board is ruled and the box is the same box it has been since the Guild took the tank over.

> *Tank card. Sallow Head tank. Read on the last Saturday in the month, by two men, with the staff and the watch, and the level entered to the quarter of an inch. The year to be given. A fair copy to the counting-house at Vellum once in the year, in the cart, in the month of October.*

There were six years of entries on the board. Somebody had ruled new lines across the old ones and nobody had torn anything out.

The level on every one of them was between two feet six and an eighth and two feet six and a quarter, which is an eighth of an inch, and one line in the middle of the run reads two feet six and a fifth, and the clerk who ruled the board has ruled it once and ruled it well.

"The coping is at two feet eight," said Margit Saye. "The bottom of the draw is at two feet four. The mark on the inside of the wall is at two feet six and a quarter and that is the mark a keeper uses, and it has been on that wall since about the time I came, and it is a line cut with a chisel and no date on it.

"We are at two feet six and an eighth this evening, which is the lowest figure in the book, and the variation for six years is an eighth of an inch, and two men have signed every line of it, and one of those two men is a keeper and the other one is whoever comes with him, and it has been the same keeper for six years because a keeper does not leave."

"And that is six thousand gallons sitting in a stone box at a mark," said Tarin Rusk, "and it has not gone down a quarter of an inch in six years."

"It has not. It is within a quarter of an inch of the mark in six years of Sundays, which is either the most honest card in this basin or the least useful, and I have not worked out which.", said Margit Saye.

"So the flume sluice is shut and the tank is full and the mark has not moved."

"It has not."

"Which means whatever is coming in is going out somewhere, all the time, every day, and it is not going out of the flume, because the flume sluice has been shut for six years and I have put my hand on the chain."

"Yes."

"And it is not going out of a leak, because a leak would take the level down and a man would have put a stone in it."

"Yes."

"So it is going out of the six-inch waste pipe in the embankment chamber, which was put in to empty the chamber, which is under the road, and which the Guild's own volume says has been solid at both ends for eight years."

"Yes," said Margit Saye. "And I have known that for three years, and I have written it down nowhere, because I did not have the throat and I did not have the number, and a sentence with a pipe in it and no number in it is a sentence about a man."

The boy of fifteen put the staff in and they read it twice and the second reading was the same, and Margit Saye wrote it on the board and signed for herself and Tarin Rusk signed as the second man, and then he did not go and wash his hand, which Wick Venn noticed and did not mention.

Then, on the way down the embankment in the dark, in the middle of a conversation about nothing, Sef Ordry asked him the question.

"You are going to name it."

It was not a question with a question mark in it.

"I am going to say what I can do and what I cannot do, in an assembly, with a day and a rod and a pail and a watch, and I am not going to put a name on it, because they refused me a name on Wednesday in front of forty-one people and I signed the refusal into her book and I watched what it cost her, and I am not going to do the thing I refused to let them do."

"That is what you are going to do," said Sef Ordry. "I heard you say it, and I am not asking again and I am not going to say it to a man in a shed with four witnesses. I said it once, out loud, on a hill, so that it was said, and that is all I came up here for."

"Why did you come up here for that?"

"Because on Friday you had your hand on a pipe with nine feet of head behind it and three of us on a ladder," said Sef Ordry, "and you did not open it, and you said why, and the why was a rule and not a reason. And a rule is a thing a man says when the reason has gone out of the room, and I have worked for a master for eleven years who has said *by the book* about nine hundred times and I have watched what *by the book* do in this basin.

"So I came up here to hear you say it once when nobody was going to check you, and I have heard it, and I am going to leave it alone, and if you ever open that pipe without a man at the ladder head you will not have a witness, and I will not be one, and I have said that now and we will not say it again."
