# Chapter 0098 — What Goes In The Empty Space

The count card was printed at Culm on the Wednesday morning of the twenty-first and it was printed in a room over a shop in a town of about four thousand people, and there were about nine of them in the room, and Perdie Ammid was one of them, and she had gone up the lane on the Tuesday afternoon with a boy of fifteen and a boy of nineteen, and had got there at about seven in the morning, and had done it in a cart that was going up with a printer's paper order, and it had taken all night and she is fifty-four years old and has said out loud that she would do it again.

It was cut on the third of this month and it went up the mountain service road on the Friday the ninth in a cart, and it came back on the Wednesday the twenty-first, and a man of thirty-eight with one hand has already priced the twelve days and the two Saturdays and the two Sundays in it, in a yard in a village, out loud, and entered them in a column, **and that column is in a cart and I am not going to do it again.**

The press was a stone on a frame with a screw on it and it was about a hundred and eighty years old, and the room smelled of ink and of hot metal and of the wet rag a printer keeps for thirty years and does not throw away. The forme was set and the stone had been inked twice. The man at the press was sixty-eight, and he had a boy with him and a woman who set type, and five other people were in the room because a shop in Culm has a floor in it and anybody may stand on a floor.

Perdie Ammid did not go to the stone. She stood at the end of the table and let the printer do it, because a printer pulls a sheet off a stone and turns it round and holds it up, and that is the trade and it is not hers.

He did it. He pulled two hundred and fifty sheets, which is what a card that size comes to at a shilling the hundred, and he has printed about eleven thousand cards in this town and he knows the size of a card the way a man knows the size of a loaf, and he pulled the first one and held it up.

"There," he said. "Have a look at it before it goes on the board."

About nine people in a printer's room in Culm looked at a count card from a village forty-eight miles down a lane.

The first rod read as it had been cut. The second read as it had been cut, with both trials under the figure. The third read as it had been cut, and the hole was in it, and the reason was printed under the hole, and **the reason is a padlock.**

Under the third rod, in the space that had not been on the card when the boy of fifteen cut it, there is a new line, and the line has a heading, and there is nothing under the heading.

> *The third rod can be filled from:* — nothing.

The fourth rod was at the bottom, under a line, in the space where there had been nothing, and it is in a boy's hand, and it is headed in a woman's hand:

> *Fourth rod. The Sallow Dyke, second bridge, main cut. Not a supply. A drain. Figure read and not measured.*

and under it, in the same boy's hand, the figure, and under that, a signature, and the signature is nineteen years old and belongs to a person in this room.

"Nobody has told me about the third rod," said the printer, "and nobody has told me about the fourth, and I have set both, and I am sixty-eight years old and I have printed a card in this town every month since I was thirty-one and I have never had a card with a padlock printed under a hole in it and I have never had one with a fourth rod that is a drain, and I have had both in one morning, and I am not complaining, I am telling you what is on the stone so that nobody in your village can be told afterwards that it was not printed."

He put the sheet down on the table, face up, and squared it with his hand.

"Now then," he said. "**What goes in it.**"

Nobody in that room said anything for about eleven seconds.

"I have been at this trade fifty years," he said, "and I have never yet set a line with a heading on it and nothing under the heading, and I have done it today because a woman in a village wrote the heading and did not write anything under it, and I want to say why I did it, and then I am going to say the other thing, and then I am going to finish my two hundred and fifty sheets.

"**A card with a hole in it is an honest card.** I have printed notices in this town for forty years that were full, and I have printed notices that were empty, and the ones that were full were nearly always full because a man wanted a thing in them, and the ones that were empty were nearly always empty because nobody could find anybody to ask. I know the difference on a stone. I have never been able to tell anybody the difference until this week.

"And if I had printed a date under that line — and I have got three dates in my head that I could have put there and I am not going to say what they are — then in four years there is a card on a post at a culvert with a date in it, and the date is the day somebody thought a flume could be measured, and a man in a counting-house reads a card and a date and believes both, and I would have printed the belief and not the fact, and **a printer is the last man in this business who should be allowed to print a figure he cannot swear to, and I have printed figures I could swear to for fifty years and I am not going to start on a hole.**"

He squared the sheet again.

"So the line stands empty and I have put my own line at the foot, which is the only thing on that card that is mine, and it is not a figure and it is not a date, and it goes on the board with the card and any person who can read can read it."

> *There is no figure for the line above and I will not print one. A card that carries a hole is honest. A card that carries a figure somebody wanted in the hole is not.*
>
> *— the printer, Culm.*

And then the boy of fifteen stood up off a bench at the back of a printer's room and said the thing he had told a village on the Friday that he had a thing to say about and that he was going to say in the right room and not in this one.

"**She is the only person who can say what goes in it,**" he said, "and she said in a room in this village in a fortnight that she is not going to tell him, and she has not told him, and he has asked her, in front of nine people, and she is not going to tell him, and I have walked that card forty-eight miles up this lane twice in three weeks to find out whether she was going to, and I have found out, and I am glad, and I am not going to say so in a way that makes it a thing she has to be thanked for, because she has told us in a room that she is not going to be thanked and I have believed her and so should all of you."

Nobody in that room thanked her.

Perdie Ammid stood at the end of the table and did not ask the printer to print a figure, and she did not ask him to print a date, and she did not ask him to leave the line out, and she did not ask him to take the line out, and about nine people in that room watched her not do four things, and the printer watched her do them.

"You will want to know what I would have put in it," he said, after a while.

"I am not going to ask you what you would have put in it."

"I would have put the date the plate could be fitted," he said, "and I would have had to go and find out what that date is, and it is a plate and a keeper and a district sixty-one miles east with no acting commissioner in it, and I have a cousin in that city who could tell me by Tuesday, **and I did not send to him, and I want it noticed that I did not send to him, because a man of sixty-eight who does not send to his cousin about a hole in a card has understood something about printers and almost nothing about water.**"

Nobody thanked him for that either, and he did not expect to be, and the two hundred and fifty sheets were printed by about half past ten in the morning.

---

Two things were done in that room before the sheets were tied, and neither of them was a figure and both of them are on the page.

The first was the strip of paper an inch and a half wide that has been gummed along the foot of that card since the eighth of this month, with a window on it in a woman's hand. The printer had set it separately and had not put it into the forme, and he said so before anybody asked, and Perdie Ammid said that was correct, and did not take it off, and the strip is still on the card and it will be on the card when the card goes back up the road.

The second was a copy for Marl, whose clerk had asked for a copy of the sheet of the fourteenth and had not been refused and had never been given one, and about nine people in that room had heard about the clerk asking for a copy and about four of them had thought at the time that it was the only sensible thing anybody in the Reach had done in a month.

"Marl is a clerk and he asked for a copy of a sheet he signed," said the printer, "and I am going to give him a copy of this card, and it is not the same thing and he will know it is not the same thing, because a card is a count and a sheet is a decision, and I am not a clerk and I do not pretend otherwise. **A copy of a count is a copy of a number. A copy of a decision is a man handing his reasons to the next man who comes.**"

And then he said the other thing, which was about his name.

The boy of nineteen had asked him for it, twice, in the ordinary way a boy of nineteen asks a printer, on the grounds that the signature at the foot of a card ought to be a person.

"I have printed at Culm for fifty years," he said, "and I have signed the foot of a card with this town since I was thirty-one, and I have been asked for my name in that time by about four people, and two of them were right to ask and I have given it to both of them, and I am not going to give it to a boy in a hurry.

"**Because the town is longer than I am and it will still be here when I am not, and a man who has been printing for fifty years has a very good idea how long fifty years is and a very poor one about anything else.** Write *the printer, Culm*, and if anybody in that village wants my name they can come up here and stand in this room and ask me, and it will take them two days and a night's bed, and that is what my name costs, and I would rather somebody paid it than that I gave it away on a boy's asking."

Perdie Ammid asked him for it herself, once, at about eleven o'clock that morning, and was refused in the same words, and she entered the refusal on the fifth sheet in her own hand with the date on it, and wrote under it, and she has read that sheet out to nobody since and she read it out to him:

> *The twenty-first day of this month. A printer of sixty-eight at Culm was asked for his name by a boy of nineteen and by me and refused both, in the same words, and gave a reason, and the reason is a better one than mine would have been. He is not named on the card and he is not named in this book and I am not going to be the person who names him in a drought. He printed a card with a hole in it and a line at the foot of it in his own hand and he is not thanked for it, and he did not ask, and I have said so in a room and I am not going to be thanked for saying so.*

The cards went down the lane on the Thursday morning in the cart that had brought three people up, with the sheets in the boy of nineteen's coat and the strips of paper still gummed to the foot of every one of them, and about nine people in that room watched two hundred and fifty copies of a card go out of it, and every one of them had a hole in the same place, and under the hole on every one of them there was a padlock printed and nothing else, and at the foot of every one of them there was a line in a printer's hand saying that he would not fill it, and about two hundred and forty people in a valley were going to walk up to a post at a culvert and look at it, and anybody might look at it, and the point of it was never anything else.
