# Chapter 0075 — The Reason, Said Twice

On the Thursday morning of the twenty-fifth day of this month the ground in the low fields of the Sallow Reach was like a bench, and about a dozen people who had been setting apple for ten days stopped, and it was not a decision anybody had to make.

The planting window is the first ten days from the fifteenth, and after the tenth the ground is like a bench and the seed rots in it, and that is not local opinion. It is in a book of customs a hundred and thirty years old in a chest in a village and it is on a card at the culvert with a strip of paper across the top of it, and on Thursday the twenty-fifth it stopped, and nobody wrote anything down about it, and it was the first day in about four weeks that nothing in this Reach happened before nine o'clock in the morning.

Tarin Rusk was at the flume at eight with a lath and a watch and a boy of fifteen, and they timed the stone throat twice out of habit, and neither of them wrote the figures down, and on the Friday morning Perdie Ammid asked him for them and he said that the two men who had measured it twice were the two men who had measured it on the twenty-ninth day of the month before and the first of the month after that, and that he would not have their figure off a Thursday in a drought with a boy holding the watch.

Nine hundred acres of apple, sown or not sown, and a hundred and twenty gallons an acre if you had a hundred and twenty gallons an acre, and nobody in the Reach has that and nobody in the Reach ever has.

---

Margit Saye went home on the Friday morning of the twenty-sixth day of this month, at about ten, on a cart that goes to a village four miles down the lane and then takes the mountain road, and she carried the specification book in its oilcloth case and a rule and a lamp and a bottle and a tin and a roll of string and an eleven-inch canvas book of her own, and she carried nothing else, and that is the whole of the trade as she has said it twice.

She said goodbye in about ninety words at the gate of the shop at Brant and she said all of it to Bess Ilk.

"Right," she said. "I have said the useful things. There is one left and it is not useful.

"I was right in a shed on the tenth day of this month that nobody had read that sheet and I was right on the eleventh in a room of thirty people about the hand, and I was right on Saturday about the three clerks, and I have been right about nearly everything I have looked at for twenty-four years and I have got nothing out of it and that is the trade and I would like it said in a shop in a village that a fitter being right is not the same as anybody doing anything about it.

"Twenty-four years of plates and every one of them somebody else's number, and a fitter's whole value is that she is right about a hole, and nobody has ever once sent for me about a hole. They send for me about a hand. And there is no such thing as a fitter who is sent for.

"That is all. Do not answer it. Bess, the cup is in the case and I want it back, and the boy is not to carry anything, he is to be told that, and if anybody puts him on a cart with a form on it I want to know where."

Then she got on the cart and Tarin Rusk, who had come down to the lane, said four words to her and got three of them out before she shut the door.

"I am sorry —"

"Do not," said Margit Saye. "You have not done a thing to be sorry for yet. That is the part I have been trying to tell you since Saturday and I have failed twice and I am going home. You have not done a thing yet. Ask me in a month, if I am still the only person who can read a hand, whether you would still be sorry."

---

The four of them did the flume on the Friday afternoon because the flume is the flume and it is a stone launder with a lath's worth of silt in it and nobody has an instrument for that, and because a man with one hand and a barrow is the only person in the Reach who can get anything anywhere, and because a boy of fifteen with nine hours and a quarter on a Saturday in thirty-six degrees is the only person who can walk two miles and four chains of it and not be asked anything about it.

Sef Ordry sent Tarin Rusk out of the shed at about one o'clock and did not let him back in until about three, and at three o'clock he was in the shed door with the card in his hand and Jory Vant on the bench and the boy outside with the barrow.

"Copy the four lines of that thing in your own hand," he said to Jory Vant. "On a clean sheet. Do not do the fourth one twice and do not leave the second one short, because a return is a thing somebody has to be able to read in about eleven years in a room with the lights on.

"And copy the four and not the fifth. The fifth is not about the water and it is not mine to copy and it is not yours to copy, and when he puts it in he will put it in himself, and that is the correct procedure, because a line that names people is not a line that a boy of nineteen should be the hand on."

"Right," said Jory Vant, and copied four lines in his own hand, and did it well, and it took him about nine minutes, and it is in a book in a village in the second book under the returns, and there is a signature at the foot of it that says *the four lines above are copied in the hand of J. Vant, apprentice, and the fifth line is not copied by me and I was not asked to copy it.*

Margit Saye would have said the rest of it. She was on the cart.

---

At about four o'clock on the Friday afternoon Sef Ordry put the card back on the nail and came out of the shed and crossed the yard to where a man of thirty-three was standing at the wrong end of a launder with a bad shoulder, and neither of them had said anything for three hours.

"Right," said Sef Ordry. "I said a thing once, on the flags, on the thirtieth day of the month before, with about twenty people in front of me, and you gave me a bad answer to it and I gave you the right one, and you wrote it down, and you have not asked me since and I have not said it since.

"I am going to say it a second time and it is going to be a different sentence, and I want you to understand before I start that I know it is a different sentence, and I want you to understand why.

"I am saying it to you, and not to a yard, and not to a boy, and not to a woman from Bexhill, because I have got a woman from Bexhill's opinion of it and she gave it to me at about eleven this morning on a cart and I am repeating it here because you were not in the lane and you should have been.

"She said: *that is a good rule and it is not the reason, and you know the difference, and you are giving me the version that can be written down.* And she is right and she is a fitter of twenty-four years and I am a sluicewright of nine, and I have spent the morning deciding that she is right and deciding to say the other one anyway, and I have decided because the other one is the one a man can carry into a room on a Saturday morning and use.

"The one for the yard was this: **he counted a water in a lane at the top of his own hill when everybody in his city was telling him there was none.** And you said *I wrote it down because I had to*, and I said *I know. That is why.* And that is the reason. That is the whole of it. I have never said anything better and I have never been able to improve on it and I am not going to try.

"This is the other one and it is for a man with a sheet of paper in his coat.

"**A man who is going to do a thing on a Saturday tells the men who have to be there on the Saturday.** Not asks. Tells. And he tells them before he does it, and he tells them while they can still say no, and a man who has told a man and been told no has to do a different thing, and a man who has done a thing and then told a man has done it alone and dressed it afterwards.

"That is the trade. That is not a moral. I have had four masters and three of them never told anybody anything and I have watched the three of them be surprised in a shed about nine times, and I have worked for the fourth for two years and he tells everybody everything including the parts that make him look like a man who has been wrong, and in two years that man has not been surprised in a shed once, and he is fifty-four and he is not clever and I would rather work for him for nothing.

"So I have said it once for a yard and once for a man, and the first one is the reason and this one is the instruction, and I have never said the instruction before and I am not going to say it twice."

"Go on," said Tarin Rusk.

"That is the whole of it," said Sef Ordry. "I have not got a third one and if I had it would be the same one again and I would have said it then."

---

They stood in a launder that has been dry for six years for about a minute, and then Tarin Rusk asked a question that was on the card and was not on the card.

"Tell me it on Saturday."

"No," said Sef Ordry.

"You have said it twice. You have never said it twice to the same man. Tell me it on Saturday morning and I will do whatever you tell me and I will do it in front of you."

"**No,**" said Sef Ordry. "And that is not a trick and it is not a test and I am going to tell you what it is in one sentence and then I am going to be at a gate bed on Tuesday and you are going to hold the other end of it, and there is no water in that part of the works at any hour of a drought, and we are not going to talk about this again in this Reach.

"It is a rule. A rule is a thing a man says when the reason has gone out of the room. And the reason has not gone out of the room. The reason is that I have been in a shed with a man for nine days and he has not once told me to do a thing he would not be doing himself at the same hour, and that is not a rule, it is a habit, and a habit is worth more than a rule and it is worth more to me than anything in a bundle of forms.

"And you cannot ask a man to say a habit out loud a second time, because the second time it is a rule. And a rule can be given in one line and I have just given it to you in one line and it took eleven years off me and I am not going to have it handed back to me in the morning in a shed in a different voice.

"Ask me a question tomorrow morning. I will answer anything you ask me. Ask me about the form. Ask me about the fifth line. Ask me whether I have read it. Ask me whether I have seen your coat. I will answer all of it and I will answer it first. **You have not asked me one question all day and I have been standing in a yard waiting for it since about eleven o'clock, and that is what I have been doing with my Friday, and you should have had it by eleven and I have wasted four hours and it is not your fault and it is not mine.**"

Then he went and scraped a gate bed, and it was the stop on the Cold Shoulder line on the north side of the works, and that gate is shut and has a plate in it and the nearest water to it is in a gravel bed nine feet away that is not going anywhere near it, and he had told a man in a launder that he would be working on the Tuesday and it had turned into a rule inside four hours, and a rule that is not kept inside three days is not a rule, and he kept it.

---

At about seven in the evening the boy of fifteen was paid out of the wages column in the second book for the second time, for the Thursday and the Friday, two days at five shillings, and the column had not had a sum in it since about 1841. The entry said his name is not to be written down and is not to be asked for, in the same words as on the thirteenth of this month, and Perdie Ammid wrote *as before* beside the second one and ruled a line under it, because a thing written twice in a column is a rule and a thing written once is an accident, and she said that out loud to a room of about nine people and it is the only thing she has said this month that she has not said before.

Wick Venn had been in a field since seven in the morning and had come in at dusk and had eaten about half a loaf and a cheese that Bess Ilk put on the wall of the flume field at eight without telling anybody, which was the third morning of that and which nobody had begun to talk about yet.

And the boy of fifteen said, at about eight, standing in the doorway with his coat on, the only thing he said in the whole day, and he said it to Tarin Rusk and not to anybody else.

"You have had a sheet in your coat for eight days now. I have carried a boy of thirty-three's coat twice over a stile and it is in the inside pocket and it is a sheet of paper and it is folded twice and you have not taken it out in front of anybody all week, and I am not asking what is on it and I am not to be asked, but I am telling you that I have noticed, because a thing I have noticed and a thing I have not noticed are two different things and I would rather you had the first one."
