# Chapter 0060 — Two Ends Of One Water

They did the throat twice.

Margit Saye would not take eight hundred and sixty gallons an hour into a shed on one trial on the strength of one lath, and on the Monday morning of the first day of next month she went down to the west side of the embankment with the same lath, the same two-inch rule, and a different watch borrowed from Wick Venn, and she put the lath in at the same height in the same slot, and she got twenty feet in **thirteen seconds and a half**.

"Eight hundred and thirty-three," she said, and scratched it out, and wrote the second one underneath.

"Eight hundred and sixty on the Friday, eight hundred and thirty-three on the Monday, and that is three per cent," said Tarin Rusk, "and I am going to say in a room on Wednesday that the figure is between those two and that it is not a number anybody may lean on, and I am going to say it in those words, and if somebody writes nine hundred and one on the card I will take the card down."

"Then write eight hundred and fifty," said Margit Saye, "which is the middle of the two to within a bucket's worth, and be dull about it."

"That is the first time anybody has offered me a middle number in my life," said Tarin Rusk, "and I do not think it is a fudge, and I am going to say why I think so, and the reason is that a man who has two live charges standing against him and a name in four buildings is not going to be able to defend an extreme figure in about three years, and the middle is the only figure that will still be true when somebody is looking for a reason to say I was being clever."

Jory Vant wrote it on the card at the culvert, in his own hand, over the printing, and it said:

> *This drain is fed from a stone throat at the west side of the road embankment and the water comes out of the pipe at the top of it and goes over the side of it and falls. Measured with a lath and a watch. Eight hundred and forty gallons an hour on the first day of next month. Eight hundred and sixty on the twenty-ninth day of the month before. The figure is about eight hundred and fifty and it is not a figure anybody should lean on.*

Underneath, in Jory Vant's hand again, because nobody else would write on a notice:

> *The drain is the Long Drain and it is not called the Sallow. It has never been called the Sallow. The word is on the wrong thing and we know whose word it is and we are not going to use it.*

---

The second number was the one everybody already had.

Six gallons in one minute and fifty-two seconds, and six gallons in one minute and fifty-three, and a hundred and ninety-three and a hundred and ninety-one gallons an hour, and Nesta Sarg would not let anybody write either figure without the two trials printed underneath it.

So the two numbers sat on the same card, three hundred and forty yards apart in the world and one sheet of tin apart on the card, and eight hundred and fifty against a hundred and ninety is four and a half times.

That is the whole of the mystery of the water in the Sallow Reach and it is not a mystery at all, and the man who explained it did it with a folding rule and a length of string on the flags outside the shop at Brant on the Monday afternoon, in about four minutes, in front of about thirty people.

"The drain is two feet two inches wide at the top," he said, "and there is an inch and a half of water in the middle of it at the plank and the plank is a foot above the trough, and the bed under this lane is gravel and has been gravel for four hundred years, and the water table in a dry summer is about a foot below the invert at Nether Sallow.

"So between the throat and the plank there are three hundred and forty yards of a drain that is not lined and is laid on gravel, and the difference between eight hundred and fifty and a hundred and ninety is six hundred and sixty gallons an hour, and it has gone into the ground.

"It has not been lost. I want that said again because it is the whole of the fourth day. It is in the ground under a lane and it is in a gravel bed and the water table is a foot below that and it is going down, and it has been going down for however long that crown joint has been open, and nobody knows where it comes out, and I am not going to pretend to know.

"And I am not going to write the phrase *no loss* anywhere, because a man with a rule and a string can put a bound on a difference and cannot put a place on a gallon, and a gallon in the ground under a lane is a gallon that somebody might want in an argument in eleven years."

Then a woman of about sixty at the back asked him what would happen to the weep hole itself, and Tarin Rusk looked at Margit Saye, and Margit Saye answered it, because it was her trade and her throat.

"I have seen one go," she said. "Four miles up the Kerrow valley, above the lime kiln, on the same main. Two inches of weep in a stone joint, and about eight hundred an hour through it, and it went on for four years and the joint went from two inches to four and a half in the fourth year, and then the stone above it went and it stopped, and nobody noticed for a season, and the field at the top of it went back to what it was in the year the sluice was shut.

"So there is one of these four miles up that hill, with a year on it, and nobody has been to it, and I am not going, because I have four men and two of them are needed here and one of them is nineteen.

"And I am saying it in this yard because if I say it in a shed in eleven weeks it will sound like a thing I decided not to do."

---

Mara Quill was forty-eight miles up the lane at Culm on the Tuesday morning of the second of next month, on a matter of the Memory Office's own, which was a parish register that had been kept in a press since 1811 and had been damp, and which had come up from Vellum with a warrant and a printed instruction and a second reader.

She had been at Culm since the Friday. Nobody had told her anything. She had been in the Reach for four days and had been given a table in the front room of the register's house and had been fed, and she had asked about a tithe map and had been told, correctly, that there was no tithe map and that there had been a hearth tax and that the hearth tax was in a chest at Brant and had not been looked at since a man had gone into it for a stick.

She and Tarin Rusk had not met for ten weeks and a day, which is from a dispensary step in the spring to a printer's back room in the west, and both of them knew the interval to the day and neither of them said it.

They met for about four minutes in a room over a shop in which nine people were standing, because the printer of Culm had asked her, in the ordinary way, to look at a card before it went on the press.

The card was Jory Vant's card. It was the one at the culvert, re-cut, and the printer had set it up because a printed card is a printed card and a card that has been chalked on is not a thing anybody can keep.

"There are three figures on it," said Mara Quill.

"Three."

"Eight hundred and forty. Eight hundred and sixty. Eight hundred and fifty. And then there is this." She put a finger on the bottom of the sheet. "Thirty-eight gallons an hour, the quantity taken, per household, per day. And it is the best one on the card and it is the only one anybody in this village is going to read first, and it is in a hand that a woman has put her name to as a figure she can be held to, and it is not a measurement."

"It is what they take," said Nesta Sarg.

"It is what you take. It is not what the drain does." Mara Quill did not raise her voice, and she was not being dramatic, and she was doing the thing she did, which was to say a thing in a room in such a way that four people who had not thought about it thought about it inside a minute. "If that card is printed and it is nailed to a post, then in four years there is a piece of paper in a village with a figure on it that says what this village can afford to take out of its own drain. And in four years somebody wants a window, and somebody else wants to say this lane has had a steady supply of eight hundred and fifty gallons an hour and has never wanted more, and thirty-eight is in his hand, in ink, on a printed card, and I do not have to be in the room. I have watched that do it four times and I have never once been in the room."

Nobody in the room had thought of it. Tarin Rusk had not thought of it, and he had wanted thirty-eight on the card, and he had wanted it on the card because it was the figure that made the village look like a village that needed something.

"Take it off," said Nesta Sarg.

That was the whole of it and she said it in nine words and she was agreeing with a woman she had met at a door that morning against her own village's interest, in a room, in front of nine people, and she did it because it was true and because she had been on that lane for four days and had watched the number being loved.

"It is in my book in my hand and it is what I do and it is not a number anybody may print," said Nesta Sarg. "Print the two and the middle. Leave mine off."

"You will want it in a year," said Mara Quill, "when somebody says the village never asked."

"I have got it in a book," said Nesta Sarg, "in my own hand, and the book is in a chest at Brant and my grandmother is in the same chest in a different way, and if the day comes that somebody has to be shown what I did with a pail and a cart and a horse, then they will come and ask me, and I will tell them, and that is the whole of what a number is for."

The printer looked at the card, and then at the whole room, and then at the two women.

"Right," he said. "And I will print what I can swear to, and I have printed things in this village that were wrong once, in the year the assize came up, and a woman of eighty lost eleven acres over a sheet of mine, and I have not printed a figure in this village since and I am not starting on the fourteenth of next month."

---

The disagreement was written down in the same afternoon, on the page of the second book that the duty roster was on, and it was written in two hands, and Perdie Ammid read it and initialled it and said that was the correct procedure.

The roster said: eight hours on and eight off, four in the yard and two at the plank, from the first of this month to the fourteenth day of this month. Nesta Sarg on the pail, because she has done it for thirty-two years and her mother did it before her. Jory Vant on the lath, because he is the only man in the party who has been shown how to hold a watch in both hands. Bess Ilk on the trough, because it is her yard. A boy of fifteen whose name is on the second page of the second book in a hand about a hundred and thirty years old and who does not want to be named, so the roster says a fifteen-year-old. Wick Venn on the barrow, because a barrow is not a trade and a barrow is the only thing in that lane that can take anything anywhere.

And in the fourth week of the six, on four days a week, on the hours of the day, a man of thirty-three who is paid five shillings a day and who is not a member of any assembly in the Reach and who could not vote in any of them if he tried.

Margit Saye is not on the roster and said so. "I am the Guild," she said. "The moment I am on that page the page is a Guild page and it is a page in a shed in eleven weeks' time. Put me on the lath and let me be a woman with a lath."

Sef Ordry is not on the roster either, for the same reason, and for one more, and he gave the one more out loud in a yard at the end and it is the only thing he said in the Reach in ten days that anybody wrote down.

"I am a man of Bexhill and if I am on a village roster then in four years' time somebody can say the Guild had a place in it. And I have spent my life being the man who keeps the Guild out of rooms, and I am not going to start on the twenty-ninth day of the month before because I have been up a hill with a nice story about a weeper."

Underneath the roster, in Tarin Rusk's hand and then in Mara Quill's, the fifth condition of an agreement made on a dispensary step in the spring, in a village sixty-one miles east, in a book that a refusal has made into a record:

> *We were shown the same sheet of paper at the same time on the second day of next month. He wanted a figure on it and she would not have it on it, and the reason she gave was correct, and the woman who wrote the figure agreed with her against her own village. He says nothing here about the reason. It is in the fourth book at the page before the notice, in his hand, at the top, where anybody may read it.*
> *Neither of us has withdrawn anything.*

And Wick Venn wrote a line underneath the whole of it, in his own hand, and showed it to nobody and did not read it out and did not put it in the book, and it said:

> *In the field 9 a.m. to 1 p.m. and down the lane 3 p.m. to dusk, there and back, and a man cannot get water and be somewhere else at the same time, and I have been trying to say that since June.*

---

They cut the notice off the post on the Tuesday evening of the second of next month and turned it over and cut the tin out of the frame, and the frame went back on the post because a frame is ninety years old and a card is six.

The back of the card had six years of a village's water in pencil in five hands, and the front had *CLOSED. No water. No entry. Under penalty.* on it, and no office and no name and no gate.

Six hands wrote on the front of the new card and Jory Vant did the printing because he was the only one of the six who could do it, and it said the two figures and the middle figure and the two trials, and it said that anybody might go and look at the throat and the plank and the tank and see for themselves whether the figures were right, and it said the date.

And across the top of it, in a hand that was not any of the six, on a strip of paper an inch and a half wide, because the card was already full:

> *A count will be taken at the plank and at the tank and at the flume sluice on the fourteenth day of this month at eight o'clock in the morning. The figures will be read out in the yard at the shop at Brant at four o'clock and put on this card. Any person may come and stand at the plank and bring a pail.*

And under the strip, in the hand of the boy of fifteen who does not want to be named, the date.

> *The fifteenth day of this month.*
