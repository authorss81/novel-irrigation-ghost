# Chapter 0072 — Three Men Alive

The Bexhill cart went up the mountain service road at ten on the Friday morning with boards, a load of lime, and two things out of the Sallow Reach that were not freight.

The first was a printed card in two colours with a hole in it, going sixty-one miles east to a place where a boy of twenty-two had been keeping a count by himself for nineteen days and had not been written to, in writing, since the second day of this month, in front of two people, at his own request.

The second was a letter in a Guild envelope with a wafer on it, sealed by a woman of forty-six, going to a master at Bexhill, which had been announced in a room at Brant on the thirtieth day of the month before and had not gone and had got heavier every day it had not gone.

Jory Vant carried both of them up to the bar and put them in the cart himself, because the driver was loading boards and did not ask and Jory Vant has never in his life waited to be asked to do the carrying.

"How much is a card with a hole in it," the driver said, when Jory told him what the third rod was.

"It is a padlock," said Jory Vant.

"That is not what I asked."

"It is a padlock and six years and a plate in three pieces in a barrow and a chain that is not renewed, and the reason is printed under the hole so anybody can see it, and the man who put it there did not have to."

The driver looked at the card for a while and then put it under a board so it would stay dry, and said that a card with a hole in it was the only honest thing he had carried this year and that he was not being funny about it, and Jory Vant got back down the road on the hinge of a cart that was going to Bexhill for four days and did not tell anybody at the bar what the driver had said, which took a great deal out of him and which he was entitled to.

---

Margit Saye wrote the letter on the Friday morning in the back room at the shop at Brant with the door shut, and it took her from about half past seven until about ten minutes to nine, and it is four sides of a sheet and it is not a paragraph and it is not anything anybody asked for.

It said that nothing had been taken out of the specification book, which was true, and that it had been open on a bench beside a shed for three days in a month where it was open, and that a stranger had stood next to it and copied nothing out of it and had not asked to, and that she had told him no twice and that the second no was about money and had been about a thing he would do in five years, and that both her reasons were correct and that he had accepted them without arguing, which was the part that had been going round in her head since the thirtieth day of the month before.

It said that the weep on the west shoulder of the road at the fourth mile is four and a half inches in the crown of a cross joint, with a second weep eighteen inches above it and two courses open above that, and ninety gallons an hour twice, and a rind on the lip, and a slate on a stake saying not to be opened and not to be cleared, and that she was not going and had said so in a yard.

It said that the only instrument in this basin that could put a number on a head at a shut gate in that position is a hundred and forty years old in a counting-house at Ostre and belongs to a man of eighty-one, and that three letters have gone to him this year and there have been three answers and the answers are the same.

And it said, in the last third, on the second side, that a fitter of twenty-four years standing in a company was asked on the eighteenth day of this month by a man with no office and no district and two charges against him to put her name to a return of water supplied from a work, and that he did it in a room with nine people in it and did not ask her, and that she was not going to write to her master about that as though it were an injury, because it was not an injury, and because in four years' time somebody will ask that man what he did on the eighteenth day of this month and the answer has to be in a hand and not in a mood, and that she is therefore writing it down while she can still remember it accurately, which is a fitter's reason and not a grievance's.

She sealed it herself and put the wafer on it and did not show it to anybody, and that was correct, and Tarin Rusk, who had asked to see it, was told no.

"I am going to say this once and you are going to hear it and then we are not going to have the conversation," she said. "I am forty-six and I am going home on the Friday after this, and a fitter of twenty-four years who is asked a question about a piece of paper by a man who has no standing answers the paper and not the man, and I have just spent three hours writing down what you did on Thursday, and you did not ask me to, and if I had been in the room on Thursday you would have asked me, and you did not ask me, and I have noticed that and I have written it down in a letter to a man at Bexhill rather than in a room, and that is the whole of what I think about it and it is not nothing."

---

What a fitter takes with her, when she goes, in this basin, is in a case, and it is worth writing down because the case is not hers and the contents are not hers and she has never had a moment in twenty-four years when either of those facts was in doubt.

The specification book goes back. It went out in an oilcloth case on the Monday of the second day of this month and it goes home on the Friday in the same case, and it has been in a shed, and a stranger stood beside it, and nothing came out of it.

The forms go back. There were about forty of the bundle left on Saturday and by the Monday there would be none, because the shed is a shed and a shed gets forms.

A rule, a lamp, a bottle, a tin of wax and water, a roll of string, and a small flat oilcloth book that is not a specification book and is not kept by the company and is hers and is a fitter's own: eleven inches by four, bound in canvas, and it is in her hand and it is a record of every plate she has set and every bore she has found, and she has never shown it to anybody, and it goes home with her because it is the only record in the world of two hundred and thirty-nine plates in this basin and it is not a Guild record and will never be entered in one.

And she is taking, in the ordinary way, twenty-four years of the ability to read a hand.

---

On Saturday afternoon she put three slips of paper on the table in the front room and nobody had asked her to and nobody was to be thanked for what followed.

"I am going to show you a thing and then I am going to go away, and I would like the two of those to be in that order," she said, "because if you see the thing first and I am here you will ask me to keep looking, and I am going home on the Friday."

The three slips were about the size of a call ticket and they were all the same hand, and the hand was the small upright hand with no loops that was in the flyleaf of the third book over the struck keeper's name.

"Those are the counting-house's own specimen slips," she said. "Three of them, out of the case at Bexhill, and they are the standard, and every fitter in this company is taught them in the first year, and I have had them in my case since I was twenty-two. They are the hand of the index desk. That is what the index desk writes like, and it is a hand and not a man, and it is on three slips because there have been three clerks at that desk since about 1880 and the counting-house has never withdrawn one, because a specimen slip is a hand and not a name, and because a man is never to be taken to a desk on a specimen."

"What are the three," said Jory Vant.

"I am not going to name three men in a room," said Margit Saye. "Look at them. They are the same hand. They were learned off the same copybook. There are three men alive in that building who write it, and one of them is the index clerk this month, and if I gave you a name out of those three, on this piece of paper, on a Thursday, in a drought, with a man in Bexhill who cannot answer a letter, then in four years you would have a story with a man's name in it, and the name would be a guess, and the guess would be in a book in a village, and a guess in a book in a village is the only thing in this basin that cannot be got out again."

Perdie Ammid said, "Then the amendment cannot be traced."

"The amendment can be traced to a desk," said Margit Saye. "That is a different sentence and it is a real one and it is worth more than a name and less than a name, and I would like it said in a room that the two things are being confused by everybody in this Reach including me until about ten minutes ago. A desk is not a man. A desk cannot be refused. A desk cannot be cross-examined and it cannot be sued and it does not have to answer anything, and the whole of the trouble in this basin over six years is that there has been an amendment on a flyleaf and a name nobody can put, and if the good practice of that office is the reason the name cannot be put, then the good practice is doing that and does not know it and will not be told by me.

"Because I have watched it happen. There is a rule in that company and the rule is right and the rule is that no fitter shall refuse a certificate or a setting on the ground of a hand, and that no man shall be named on a hand. Twenty-four years I have had that rule and I have agreed with it every time, and on Thursday morning a man of thirty-three read a hand to a room of nine people and neither of us could put a name to it, and the reason we could not is a rule I would defend in front of a bench of my own trade."

---

Here is the thing she did about it, and it took about four minutes, and it is why the chapter is called what it is called.

She turned the three slips face down.

"I am not taking those home," she said. "They are the company's and they are in the company's case and they go in the company's drawer and in about four years there will be a fourth one and the fourth one will be the man who is index clerk then. And I am not going to walk out of a counting-house with three slips of a hand that a matter in this Reach turns on, because I do not know what is in this Reach, and because the day a fitter carries a hand out of an office in her own bag is the day an office stops writing anything down at all, and this basin has already had one hand in one place for six years and does not need another one travelling.

"What I am going to do is write it out. From memory. In my own hand, on my own paper, on a sheet of nothing. And I am going to do it now and not look at the three, and I am going to give it to you, and you may keep it."

She wrote for about three minutes and it was very bad. It was a hand that was near enough for a person who can read hands to say *that is the index desk* and not one bit good enough to be evidence of anything, and at the top of it she wrote *written from memory and not traced, and not evidence of anything, and the only use of it is that a fitter of forty-six can look at a page in four years and know whether she is looking at her own trade or at another one.*

"Now look at that," she said, "and then look at the flyleaf in the chest, and tell me whether I have got it."

Jory Vant had it in five seconds. Perdie Ammid took a while and got it.

"That is the desk," said Perdie Ammid.

"That is my memory of the desk," said Margit Saye, "in my own hand, and it is a thing a village can hold and it is worth nothing to anybody except in about four years and to a fitter and to nobody else, and that is the best kind of paper there is, and there is a man at Bexhill who has spent eleven years with a much better one of the same kind and calls it his guilt, and I would like it noticed that he is right and I am going to tell him so on Tuesday."

---

On Sunday morning the four of them kept the roster, which is the only thing in this Reach that has been kept to, and the field behind the nine hundred acres of apple took a man of thirty-eight for the sixth morning running, and a boy of fifteen took his second wage out of a column in the second book of a village that has not had a sum in that column since about 1841, and the entry said his name is not to be written down and is not to be asked for, and it said it again, and Bess Ilk put a cup in front of him and would not be argued with about the cup.

At about two in the afternoon Margit Saye put her case down in the doorway of the shop and said one more thing to nobody in particular.

"Eight days ago I said I would not fill a witness box and I did not, and I said in a room of thirty-four people that a name in a box is a debt for the rest of a life, and I said it about a box with four boxes' worth of nothing in it, and I was right, and I have thought about it every day since and I have found out this week that I said it about the wrong thing.

"It is not a box. A box is nothing. It is a line on a printed form in a bundle that comes up from Bexhill twice a year, and it is a rule about the rule, and it is a hundred and thirty years old, and the oath on two kerb stones is two hundred and fifty years old, and I have no idea which of the two it is a copy of and neither has anybody else and I am not going to find out.

"And there is a line on that form and it is not in a box, and it is on the bottom, and it is the only line on that sheet that is not about the water, and I have watched a man look at it for a week without reading it, and I have read it, and I have read it twice, and I have not told him what it says, and that is the worst thing I have done this month and I have broken a Guild seal on a Monday.

"Ask him. Before anybody signs anything. Do not ask me."
