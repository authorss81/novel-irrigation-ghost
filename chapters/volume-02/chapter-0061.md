# Chapter 0061 — A Break And Not An Overflow

They went up the Kerrow valley at ten past five in the morning because at ten past five the west side of a road in the third week of a drought is the only side of it a man can stand on.

The party was Margit Saye's four men and Margit Saye, and Tarin Rusk, who was not one of the four men and had not been asked to be one of them and was going.

Sef Ordry, thirty-one, with a bar and a cold chisel in a roll. Jory Vant, nineteen, with a lath, a coil of twine and a watch in a tin. Wick Venn, thirty-eight, with the barrow, because a barrow was going four miles up a hill to a place where nothing was going to be brought down, and because Wick Venn brings a barrow the way other men bring a hat. And a boy of fifteen from Brant that Bess Ilk sent because she does that, on no roster, who was the only one of the five who had never been up this road and who said so once, at the first gate, and then not again.

At nine o'clock the thermometer in the box on the wall of the lime kiln read thirty-six degrees, and the boy read it twice, said the second reading was the same, and sat down in the one strip of shade the building had.

"Four miles," said Margit Saye. "And then we are going to spend the middle of the day on a bank in the sun because that is the only thing there is to do, and I would like it understood before we start that I said in a yard on Tuesday that I was not going, and I have come, and the reason I have come is that I could not stop thinking about the texture of that bank and it is a plate fitter's curse and there is no other word for it."

"You said you were not going," said Tarin Rusk.

"I said a great many things on Tuesday," said Margit Saye, "and one of them was true and most of them were a woman being careful in a yard."

---

The main comes down this valley on the west side of the road in a stone conduit under the shoulder, and it has been in the ground since 1843, and it is laid dry stone.

That was the whole of what Margit Saye said about it in the first mile, and then she said one more thing.

"A dry-stone conduit has joints. It does not have a pipe with a joint in it. Every stone in it is a joint, and a stone that has nothing behind it is a stone holding up the whole weight of the mountain off the other side by being in a wall with its neighbours. And it has been doing that for a hundred and eighty-one years with a stream in it, and a stream in a dry-stone conduit for a hundred and eighty-one years finds the one stone that was not laid by the same hand as the next four, and goes round it, and comes out under it. That is a weep."

"And a break is what," said Jory Vant.

"A break is a weep that has got past the stone."

---

They found the soft bank before they found the weep, and they found it by accident, because the boy of fifteen went for a short cut across the shoulder of the road to get in front of the barrow and put his right foot into ground that gave him about four inches more than it should have.

He came back and said, "There."

"What," said Sef Ordry.

"It is soft."

"That is soft," said Sef Ordry. "Is it soft the whole way."

They walked it. It was soft for about thirty yards, on the west side, from just above a fence corner to a slab of rock about nine feet square that is the only hard thing for a quarter of a mile. There had been no rain in the valley for twenty-two days, so the grass on it was burnt pale and standing up in the heat like wire, and the ground under it was the colour of a biscuit.

Sef Ordry put the bar in at the fence corner and it went in eighteen inches and came up with wet gravel on it, and he did it four times, at four places, in a line, and every one of them came up wet, and the third one came up with a piece of the old road metalling in it, which is a thing that does not lie four inches under a bank in a drought unless there is water under it.

"Right," he said. "Then that is not a bank that is soft. That is a bank with water going through it, and it has been going through it for a year, and it is going through about ninety gallons an hour at the moment, and there is nowhere in thirty yards for ninety gallons an hour to go except down."

Margit Saye was on her knees in the grass with her palm flat on the ground, and she said, without getting up:

"It is a break and it is not an overflow, and I want the two kept apart, because everybody in this basin is going to want to call it an overflow and an overflow is a thing with a design in it. This has no design in it. A conduit that is a hundred and eighty-one years old has a joint in it, and there is nine feet of stone over the joint, and there is a head on the joint of about what the tank at the bottom of this hill has behind a shut gate, and the joint has opened the way joints open. It is the most ordinary thing in this trade and I have seen it four times."

---

The weep was a hundred and forty yards above the soft bank, on the west shoulder, and it came out of the face of the conduit into a field.

It came out of a **cross joint in the crown** — a joint where a stone from the course above had been laid across the conduit, which is what a conduit under a road is for and what makes a road out of a channel.

Margit Saye got down to it on her hands and knees with a lamp and a scraper and about four minutes of swearing, and then she called for the boy.

"Get your eye on it. Not your hands. Your eye."

He knelt in the grass with his cheek nearly in the dirt and she held the twine across the opening and he said, "Four and a half."

"Measure it with the rule as well," said Tarin Rusk.

The boy had a rule in a sheath of oilcloth that belonged to the shop at Brant and had belonged to the shop's last fitter before him, and he measured the opening with the rule and then measured it again with a length of twine, and came up the same.

"Four and a half inches," he said. "It is a hole, though. It is not a number. It is a hole that happens to be four and a half inches across this morning."

"Twice is twice," said Margit Saye. "Write four and a half and write that it is a hole."

And then she went up the face eighteen inches with the lamp and found the second one, which was a weep out of a bed joint and about the width of a thumb, and it was running clear.

And above that, with the lamp held in her teeth, she put her thumb into the vertical joint between two courses and got it in to the first joint, which is four inches, and the clay in it was wet, and she took the clay out on her finger and looked at it and then looked at the field below the shoulder.

"Two courses open above a weep that is four and a half inches," she said. "That is the whole of it. That is the sentence I have been not saying to myself since the second day of this month, and I am saying it now, out loud, in a field, to five people and a boy, because I would like it said where it can be argued with."

---

They measured it on the Thursday morning, twice, with a pail and a watch, because on the Wednesday the boy of fifteen had done it with a hat and Wick Venn's watch and had written the result on the back of his own hand in pencil and had struck it out himself the same afternoon, and a figure struck out on a boy's hand is not a figure.

Two gallons in one minute and twenty seconds.

Two gallons in one minute and twenty-one seconds.

"Two gallons in eighty seconds is a gallon and a half a minute, and that is ninety gallons an hour," said Jory Vant.

"Two gallons in eighty-one seconds," said Margit Saye. "That is eighty-eight and a bit, and I am going to write eighty-nine, and the reason I am writing eighty-nine and not ninety is that a second on this drain at this rate is about a gallon an hour and I would rather the card be a shade under a thing that is going to grow than a shade over it."

"Ninety and eighty-nine," said Tarin Rusk. "Both printed. And the middle is ninety because the middle is what a man will read, and the middle is the one that will still be true when somebody is looking for a reason to say I was being clever, and I have had that argument twice in three days and I am not going to have it a third time."

Then they stood about eleven yards below it and looked at the ground.

"Where does it go," said Tarin Rusk.

"It goes into that field and the field drains at the top of the field," said Margit Saye, "and the field drain is not the long drain, and I want that said because the last time four men and a reader stood in a field in this Reach they spent a morning finding out that two things four miles apart are the same thing, and this one is not. The field drain goes off the west side and down to the Kerrow, and it has been dry since the flume was shut, and it has been dry for six years, and it is about two feet wide and it is in gravel, and ninety gallons an hour into two feet of gravel in a field is a wet corner and not a stream."

"And that is why the bank is soft," said Sef Ordry.

"That is why the bank is soft," said Margit Saye. "Not the joint. The joint is the small part. Ninety gallons an hour out of a hole for four years, going down the west shoulder of a road in gravel, and the gravel is under a road embankment, and the road embankment is the shoulder, and the west shoulder is the side the road is cut into. The water has been going through the road for four years and the road is still standing because stone in gravel will stand, and it is standing on the water, and the water is going down under it and not along it, and a road that is standing on moving gravel is a road with a date on it and nobody knows the date."

---

Sef Ordry sat down on the rock slab, in the only shade for nine feet, and did not say anything for about two minutes, and then he said the thing they had walked four miles for.

"The rind is holding it."

He was holding a piece of the weep in his hand. It was the size of a thumb, white-grey, hard as a shell, and it had come off the lip of the opening in one piece and had the shape of the water on the outside of it.

"That is a hundred and eighty-one years of water dropping out of a hole and putting the dissolved part of itself on the edge, faster than it has been dissolving for about four years. The hole is four and a half inches across and the opening you can see is about two, because two and a half inches of the hole is inside this.

"And it is the only thing in this basin standing between that joint and four hundred gallons an hour. Not the stone above. The stone above is two courses and a thumb. When that rind goes, and it will go and it will not ask anybody, the hole is four and a half inches of open cross joint under nine feet of stone and a road, and it will not be four hundred gallons an hour for long, because the whole west face has to go, and then the conduit is a channel, and the channel takes what the mountain gives it.

"And when that happens there is one thing in this basin that can take this hill's water out of this hill's main and put it somewhere a man can argue about, and it is the flume sluice at the bottom of the hill, and it has been shut and chained for six years, and I am not going to tell you who shut it or why, and I have said I would not and I am not going to."

Nobody asked him.

"Then say the rest of it," said Tarin Rusk, "because I can see you have got it and it is worse than that."

"The rest of it is a date," said Sef Ordry. "There is one date in this basin in which the flume sluice is going to be opened with the seal broken properly and two people standing at it, and you have all written it on a card, and it is the fifteenth of this month, and it is eleven days away.

"And that is a planting date. That is not a date anybody in this valley chose because of a hillside. That date is in a book in a village because a woman of fifty-four had a field and a window and a calendar, and she was right about the field, and she has never once in her life been asked about the hillside.

"And the hillside does not know what day it is."

---

They were on the road going down at about a quarter past three on the Thursday, and Tarin Rusk did not put his hand on it, and Jory Vant noticed, and said so, out loud, on the road, with the barrow going down in front of them.

"You did not touch it."

"No."

"Why not."

"Because I was told about myself on a hill in the dark five days ago by a man I have known twelve days and he was right, and I would like it written down that I had a hand on it on the twenty-ninth day of the month before and did not open it, and that I did not put a hand on this one either, and that the reason is the same reason and not a better one today."

Jory Vant wrote it on the back of his own hand in pencil and did not read it out.

---

They did two things on the Thursday and only two things, and both of them were on the list and neither of them was at the hole.

They cleared the weeds and the silt out of the field drain for about sixty yards below the weep, so that ninety gallons an hour had somewhere to go that was not the west shoulder of a road, and Sef Ordry insisted on it and was right, and it is a thing that takes an hour and a half with two men and a bar and it is not a repair and it will not stop a rind going.

And they put in the ground, on the fence line, a stake and a length of lath with a bit of white paint on the top of it, and on the lath a slate, and on the slate Sef Ordry wrote in his own hand:

> *Weep, west shoulder, fourth mile above the kiln. Cross joint in the crown of the conduit. 4½ in. Second weep 18 in. above. Two courses open above that. Ninety gallons an hour at the fourth day of this month, twice, and a rind on the lip. Not to be opened. Not to be cleared. Anyone finding this: the number is on the card at the culvert at the bottom of the hill and the card is the only place it is written.*

And beneath it, because he wrote it himself and because he had written a line under an empty seventh in a shed a week ago and had not enjoyed it either:

> *This is not an overflow and there is nobody to catch. The joint is a hundred and eighty-one years old and it has opened. It is going to open the rest of the way and the only thing that can take this water is the flume sluice, and the flume sluice is sealed until the fifteenth, and on the fifteenth the seal is broken with two people at it and a sheet of paper read out first, and if the rind goes before the fifteenth then the fifteenth is too late, and I am putting that on a slate on a fence in a field because a man who says a thing out loud in a field on a Wednesday is a man who has not put it in a book.*

---

At the toll bar at half past four on the Thursday afternoon the road man put his hand on the book and took it off the book, and Tarin Rusk asked him one question, and it was the first question he had put to a stranger in four days.

"What has gone up that road this month."

"Nothing," said the man, and he wrote it down, and he did not read it out.

That was the whole of the answer and it took about four seconds, and a wagon of lime had gone up on the Tuesday and a man on a horse had gone down on the Wednesday and nothing else had gone up the road in four days.

"You told me on the twenty-third day of the month before that I would come back up here in a fortnight and ask you what you had seen," said Tarin Rusk, "and it is not a fortnight."

"It is not," said the man, who was about sixty and had been on that road for about forty years. "It is twelve days, and I have written the day you came, and the day is on this side of the leaf where nobody looks, and I have been sitting here since six this morning and there has been nothing to tell you.

"You will be at my bar on the fifteenth, in the evening, after whatever it is you are going to do at eight o'clock in the morning, and I will tell you what I have seen since Friday, and it will not be much, and I am not going to read you the book."
