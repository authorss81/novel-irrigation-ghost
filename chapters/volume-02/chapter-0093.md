# Chapter 0093 — A Cart With Nothing Else In It

The cart came at nine on the Friday morning of the sixteenth and it was booked for nine and it came at nine, and the carter put the board across it himself and asked what was going under it, and was told four books, and said that four books was nothing, and then charged the same as he would have charged for four books and would have charged more.

Jory Vant carried the third book out himself. It is the Reach's own copy of the schedule of the works of the Reach, printed in this village in 1902, and it is a hundred and thirty pages, and it has been in a chest in a room over this shop since the middle of the month before this one, and it went under the board and then the Guild's volume went on top of it, which is a hundred and thirty years of a shelf in a counting-house at Vellum, shelf C, volume 44, carried sixty-one miles by a man who has never opened it and will not.

The four field books went in a satchel with a strap. Four hundred leaves. Twenty-six years. The first fifty pages of the second excepted, and the reason for the exception entered with them, and the reason is on a page of its own and is the fourth of the month before this one and is about a man who died.

Nobody in the shop said anything about it. Perdie Ammid stood at the kerb with her hands in her sleeves and watched it go, and about eleven people watched it go with her, and Bess Ilk came out from behind the counter for it and stood at the kerb too, which is not a thing Bess Ilk does.

Then Perdie Ammid went back inside and shut the door and sat down at the table and did not speak for about a minute and a half, and when she spoke it was in the voice she uses for entries.

"I am going to do the entry now while it is fresh and I am not going to do it at the end of the day, because at the end of the day I will be able to make it sound better and a better-sounding entry is a worse entry.

"It goes on a sheet and not in the column, because the column is in the satchel, and it is the fourth thing I have put on a sheet in thirty-one years and I am going to number them, and this is one.

> *Sheet one. Entered at ten past nine on the sixteenth of this month, at the kerb, and read again at four o'clock in the afternoon because I did it badly the first time.*
>
> *1. The record of the Sallow Reach left this village at nine o'clock this morning in a cart with nothing else in it, and there is no copy of it in this village and there is not going to be one.*
>
> *2. There is a line at the foot of the schedule of the works of the Reach, in a hand that has been in that book since before 1902, that says the water of the works is not delivered to any district and is not stated to be delivered to any district, and that no return be received in respect of that work from any district whatsoever until an assembly of the villages has said otherwise in writing on a day it has named. That line is mine. I entered it on the thirtieth of the month before this one in this room. **It is the only reason the four books have to go, and the only reason they could go.***
>
> *3. I am not going to write a paragraph about what I feel and I am not going to be thanked and I have said that four times this month and I am counting them.*
>
> *— P. Ammid, bookkeeper. Witnessed by nine.*

"Nine," said Sef Ordry.

"Eleven, at the kerb," said Perdie Ammid. "Nine at four o'clock. **The number of witnesses on an entry is not a measurement and it is the only number in this book that nobody has ever once been tempted to make do what it wanted.** Write that down, Mr Vant, because it is the only useful thing I have said in a fortnight."

---

The man of sixty-one came down the lane on the Friday afternoon.

He came on foot, which is four miles, and he is sixty-one, and it took him from about one o'clock to about five, and he came into the shop and did not sit down, and he had a book under his arm that is not a toll book and has not been a toll book since anybody can remember, and forty years of a road are in it, and six years of a road with no inspection on it are in it, and he has not read it out to anybody in six years.

"Perdie Ammid," he said. "I have been asked a question by a boy of fifteen standing in my doorway and I have thought about it for a day and a half and I have come down four miles to say what I am going to do about it and not what I think about it."

"That is a good way to begin anything," said Perdie Ammid.

"I will say it once," he said. "**In the room, to the nine.** Not on your sheet, which is yours, and not in a book, which is Vellum's, and not in a lane, which is where I keep things. I have kept a thing in a lane for forty years and it has not done anybody one bit of good and I am sixty-one and I am not going to be a story about a man at a toll bar in anybody's file.

"And I want three things and I am going to ask for them and I am not going to be refused, because I have walked four miles.

"One. I want to be asked the question in the room, out loud, by somebody, in front of the nine. I have answered four questions in this basin one at a time in a hut and I have not answered one in a room and I am sixty-one and I would like to have done it before I am sixty-five.

"Two. I want it written down in my hand and not in anybody else's, because I have watched three men in my life be right about a hand and be believed because of it, and I have watched two clerks be right about a hand and be believed because of it, and the difference between those two things is a thing a man can lose his job over in a counting-house and cannot lose in a lane, and I know which one I would rather be.

"Three. **I want the nine to be told that I am the sixty-first person in this room and that I have not been asked for anything since the spring before the year the flume was shut, and that a man who has not been asked for six years does not have a right to a large answer, and that I will give a small one and it will be correct.**"

Nobody in that room said anything for a while, and then Sef Ordry said: "That is the correct thing to have said, and it is going to be in the book in your words and not mine, and I am going to read it back to you on the morning of the meeting and you are going to tell me if I have got it wrong."

"Then read it back to me," said the man of sixty-one, "and I will tell you."

---

He went at about five and Perdie Ammid entered the whole of it, the three requests and the sixty-one, and then at about half past eight that night she did the thing she has been putting off since the thirteenth.

She took out a sheet of paper and wrote three lines on it and then put the pen down and sat looking at it, and Tarin Rusk was in the room and did not say anything, and after a while she read the three lines out loud and then read them again and then said no.

"No," she said. "That is a letter to a man who has just told a village in his own hand that he has been wrong in print for six years, and I am the only person in this basin who is going to have to write to him, and I have had a fortnight to find out what I am going to say and it is the fifteenth and I have three lines and they are all about me.

"**I have written to a man four times in thirty-one years and three of them were about a book and one of them was about a book that was not mine. I have never in my life written to a man to ask him for a thing for myself, and I do not know how to start, and I have got as far as the third line twice and both times the third line was *I am sorry that you have had to*, and I am not going to send a man of fifty-four a sentence with an apology in it that I have not checked, because he has just written four hundred words about being careful with a document.**"

"What will you do with it."

"I will keep it," said Perdie Ammid. "**A thing begun and not finished is the only kind of record in this basin that cannot be got out again**, and I have found that out the hard way this month, and I am going to put the three lines in this book under today's date and put the sheet itself in the chest, and the chest is a chest in a room over a shop and the room has a lock and the lock is a shop's lock and I am fifty-four and I have never once in my life pretended that was a good lock."

---

The count card was at Culm. It had been at a printer's in a town of about four thousand people since the ninth of this month and it was on a stone in a forme with a third rod empty and a reason printed under the hole and a fourth rod in a boy's hand that did not exist a month ago, and it was a printer of sixty-eight who had asked one question about it and had not been answered, and the boy of fifteen knew that because the carter had said so at the kerb, standing with his hand on the tailboard.

"He asked the man on the cart," said the boy of fifteen. "He asked him what was going in the empty space, and the man did not know, and the printer said that was all right and that he would go on and set the rest of it."

"And nobody has told him anything since," said Perdie Ammid.

"No," said the boy of fifteen. "And I have been thinking about it for two days and I have got a thing to say about it and I am not saying it tonight because I am tired and I have been up a lane twice in three days, and I am going to say it in the right room and not in this one, and I would like somebody to notice that I have decided which room."

Four people in that shop said nothing at all, and about nine said things to each other that were not about water, and it was the Friday the sixteenth, and the window was on the Thursday the twenty-second, and six days is not eleven, and the eleventh is in the margin of a book that was in a cart that morning and is four days up a road.
