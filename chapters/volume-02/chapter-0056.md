# Chapter 0056 — The Word Is The Document

The room over Bess Ilk's shop at Brant holds thirty-one people comfortably and forty badly, and on the Wednesday morning of the twenty-seventh day of this month forty-one of them were in it and it had cost Bess Ilk eleven shillings to have the chairs down from the loft, and she took the money before the meeting and would not take it afterwards.

"You will get it at the end," said Perdie Ammid.

"I will get it at the end and I will get it from you, and if I do not get it at the end I will get it next week, and either way I have got it and the room has got it, and that is how a room stays open." Bess Ilk was sixty-three and had kept the house for thirty-four years and had voted in this room eleven times and had voted as a publican and not as a farmer, which she said out loud at the start of every meeting and had never once been asked to say twice. "There is a pot of tea at the back and it is a shilling, and it is a shilling because I have eleven gallons of water in this village in a butt and a jar and I am not boiling the jar."

She was right about the water. There was no clean water in Brant on the twenty-seventh day of the month except what came in a butt from a farm four miles off, and nobody in the room said anything about it, and the tea was made with the butt and not with the jar, which cost Bess Ilk eleven shillings' worth of nothing and which she mentioned once.

Perdie Ammid had four books on the table and did not open them.

There were two things. She said so at the start, in about thirty words, and she said them in the order she wanted them taken, and nobody argued with the order.

"First, a count. One day. A rod, a pail, a watch, and a woman who writes figures better than a man. And the figure goes into a book, and a copy goes on the card at the culvert, and every person in this room is told what it is on the day it is taken, not on the day it suits anybody.

"Second, a name. Not for the count. For the water. A route name for the Sallow Head flume and the long drain, so that if that flume is ever opened there is a record to open it under, and so that if a field of anybody's is drowned by it, there is a thing to be answered for.

"Those are two motions and they are not the same motion, and I have put them separately, and I have put them in that order, and I would like somebody to say now whether the order is wrong."

Nobody said the order was wrong.

---

The name took forty minutes and it failed in the way a thing fails when it is not false, and the failure was quieter than anybody in that room expected.

Tarin Rusk put it in front of them the way he would have put a head in front of a board, which is to say he put the working first and the recommendation after it, and the working was four lines and they were about a route and not about a word.

"A name is a thing two parties can both use," he said. "That is the only claim I am going to make for it and I am going to make it in front of you and not to you. If the count is taken against a name, then anybody who disagrees with the count has to argue with the name as well as the number, and there is nobody in this basin who can be that person, and the number survives. If it is taken against no name, then anybody who disagrees with the number only has to come with a rod, and there are four men in this basin with a rod, and any one of them can be here on the day.

"I would rather have four men with rods than nobody. I am telling you that I have been the man with the bar in a lane for two months, and a name did not help me and a name cost me everything I have, and I have no argument against a name at all. My argument is that it is not worth what it costs to make one."

"What does it cost," said Domen Prouse, who was fifty-eight and grew beans and had been in this room nine times.

"It costs what it costs to agree," said Tarin Rusk. "And I cannot tell you what that is until somebody says the word out loud, and then everybody in the room is arguing about a word instead of a number."

"Then let us hear the word," said Domen Prouse.

"It is the Sallow," said Tarin Rusk.

---

And the room did the thing that rooms do, which is to become very quiet and then to have four people say four different things at once.

"It is the gate." That was a man at the back who kept a horse.
"It is the reach." A woman near the door.
"It is the tank and the branch and the flume and the gate." Sef Ordry, from the side.
"It is a word we have not said in this room in my life," said Aldis Kerr, who had not been invited and had come.

And Domen Prouse stood up, which he did not need to do, and said:

"No."

The room waited. He had said it in a tone that was not a shout and was not going to be improved on.

"I will tell you why, and then I will sit down, and then you will do whatever you like with it.

"Everything the Sallow takes that has ever gone wrong in this Reach has gone wrong to somebody who had a name for it. Nineteen fifty-nine, the flume sluice was shut at the wrong time and Brasse Fen went under four inches and lost a crop and the farmer went to a meeting about it for two years and was told that the sluice was on a schedule and that the schedule was not in the room.

"Now go and take the name off it.

"Every one of us in this lane has been on this side of a document for two hundred years and the only thing that has come of it is that we cannot be answered for and cannot answer anybody. The long drain has no name. There is no name on it. There has never been a name on it. And that means that when my father pulled three sluice planks out of a flume in the winter of the year the frost was in the ground and put eleven inches into Bell Sarg's ground and ruined a winter feed, there was nobody to sue, and there was nobody to sue Bell Sarg, and both of those facts were of use to him and to me and to the man who took the planks.

"And you are asking me to hand that over. You are asking me, on a Wednesday morning, in a room I have a key to and a beer on, to put the Sallow on the long drain in a book, and the moment that is done the flume sluice is on a schedule with a name on it, and the flume sluice belongs to the Guild, and the Guild has a volume, and there is an entry in the back of that volume from eight years ago that says the channel under the road is solid and nothing is required, and I do not know that and I would not have believed it, and it is in a shed I have never been in and it is on a card I have never read.

"So I am not refusing you because the number is wrong. I have not heard the number. I am refusing you because a name is a way of being able to sue somebody, and a village with no name has lost that way and got used to it, and I am not going to be talked out of the only piece of equipment this room owns by a man who has been in the basin four days and has not yet had a reason of his own that is bigger than a wage."

Nobody said anything for a while, and then Aldis Kerr said, from the wall:

"That is the second reason," she said. "There is a first one and you did not give it, and I am seventy-one and I have got about as long as the back of that chair.

"The first one is that the word belongs to a different thing.

"I was brought up in a lane that calls that drain *the Sallow*. Not the gate. Not the flume. The Sallow is the wet ground at the bottom of the valley and the water in it and the right to take fish off it after the sluice is shut. It is a wet common. It has been a wet common for as long as there has been a lane.

"Take the name off the gate and put it on the wet common and you have told every family in nine villages that what their grandmothers called the wet ground is a thing a man in a shed has a schedule for. And in sixty-eight years of the row over the name — the row about the word, not about a drop of water, there has not been one row about a drop of water in sixty-eight years — a man in a shed has taken that word and put it on a piece of water two hundred yards away that is not the same piece of water and never has been.

"I am not saying that is why we are poor. I am saying that when you take a word a people have used since before anybody's grandmother and you hang it on somebody else's thing, you have taken the thing that made them a people and made them a row."

Domen Prouse sat down.

---

Perdie Ammid then said the thing that was to cost her twenty-six years, and she said it in about ten words, and she said it to the table.

"Then I have to write it down."

"Write what down?" said Domen Prouse.

"His refusal," said Perdie Ammid. "In the fourth book. In his hand. In the place it belongs, which is on the page before the notice. Dated. With the date he said it and not the date I write it."

Nobody in that room understood what she had just done and about four of them understood it inside a minute.

Perdie Ammid opened the fourth book on the table, in a room with forty-one people in it, and she turned to a clean page, and she said:

"I have wanted these four books to be a record since I was twenty-eight years old, and I have spent twenty-six years refusing to let them be one, and I have said the reason out loud in this room in a cottage on Monday, and the reason is that a record is a thing somebody else keeps.

"And a book of water customs with a refusal in it is not a book of customs. It is a record. A refusal is a thing a person can be held to, and a thing somebody can be held to is held by somebody, and I have spent twenty-six years keeping this book in a chest at Brant because a chest at Brant is mine and a counting-house at Vellum is not.

"And I am going to do it anyway, today, in this room, because he has said no in front of forty-one people and there is nothing else I can do with it, and because a chest is not a thing that answers a question and this room is going to start asking me questions this afternoon."

"Then you will lose the chest," said Bess Ilk, from behind the pot.

"I will lose the chest," said Perdie Ammid. "Not today. Today it is on this table in a room over your shop and forty-one people have watched me open it, and after today it is not in a chest in a house in Brant, it is a record that forty-one people know about, and there is no putting that back in a chest, and I knew that when I said what I said, and I am fifty-four and I have thought about it for eleven days and I have decided that a book nobody can question is not worth the twenty-six years."

She wrote the date, and then she turned the book round and put it in front of Domen Prouse and put a pencil in his hand and did not say a word.

Domen Prouse wrote about two inches. It took him a while. Then he put the pencil down and read it out, because Perdie Ammid asked him to, and it said:

> *Refused, in this room, the twenty-seventh day of this month, by Domen Prouse of Brant, a name for the Sallow Head flume and for the long drain, for the reason that a name is a way of being able to be answered for and this village has not had that way since before anybody now living and I am not giving it up in a Wednesday morning in exchange for a count that any man with a rod can dispute in a year.*
> *I have not said the number is wrong. I have not seen the number.*

Perdie Ammid read it out once and then put a card over the page and tied the ribbon, and the room sat with it.

---

The count was agreed in eleven minutes and it was agreed without a name on it, and it is the only thing agreed in this room in two days that is finished.

One day. The **fourteenth of next month**, which is the day before the planting window opens and not the day of it, because a count on the day the window opens is a count made by a man in a hurry. A rod at the plank and a rod at the tank and a rod at the flume sluice. A pail and a watch. **Nesta Sarg on the pail, because she has done it for thirty-two years and her mother did it before her and the number on the card at the culvert is in her family's hand.** A woman with a good hand to write, which is not Nesta Sarg, because a person cannot measure a thing and write it and also be argued with, and Nesta Sarg said that herself in a cottage on Monday and would not give way on it.

The figure goes into a book. A copy goes on the card at the culvert, on the front, over the printing, where anybody going up the lane can read it. And a third copy goes into the bookshop at Brant, and the bookshop keeps it on a nail by the door and does not charge for reading it, and Bess Ilk said that part was hers and made him put it in the motion.

And the witness box is still empty, and Perdie Ammid said the word *four years* out loud in the room, and Aldis Kerr said, "Put it in the diary and do not ask me," and nobody asked her.

Tarin Rusk did not get a name and did not get a keeper and did not get a source.

What he got was a day, a rod, a pail, a watch, a woman who writes, a woman who measures, a refusal in a book that cost a woman of fifty-four twenty-six years, and a nail in a bookshop door.

At half past four on the Wednesday afternoon, after the tea and after the chairs had gone back up into the loft, a boy of nineteen came into the shop and asked whether there was any paper with an old thing on it, because Mr Prouse had told him there was a slip off the pin in the meeting room with a year at the top of it and a line struck through the middle of it, and that if you wanted to know how a name got to be a name you wanted to see that.

Jory Vant brought the slip out from behind the pin, and it was the length of a hand and it had a nail hole in the top of it, and he read it out in the shop while Bess Ilk listened and did not serve him anything.

> **THE WORKS OF THE REACH. Printed at the bookshop, Brant, in the year 1902.**
> *1. The Long Drain, from the wet common at Nether Sallow, 4 miles 2 chains, with the sluice at the head of it.*

And across the whole of that line, in ink of a later date and a different colour, from a left hand with a long stroke on the crossbars of the t's, the line was struck out, and above it in the same ink was written:

> *2. The Sallow Dyke, from the tank, 2 miles 4 chains, with the flume sluice.*

And there was a third line under the second, in the same type and not touched by the later ink, and it was the only one of the three that nobody in that shop had ever asked about.

> *3. The Sallow Head intake, with a plate, at a head of 7·31 metres.*

"Seven point three one," said Jory Vant, and stopped, and said it again, because he could not say it the way it was written. "It is one number. It is not two numbers with a mark between them."

"A head of water on a mountain," said Bess Ilk, who had not been asked. "Printed in this village since before I was born, and I have never once been asked where it comes from."

"And the plate at that intake is eleven feet above the floor of a chamber nineteen feet deep, and it reads dry," said Tarin Rusk. "So it is on the other side of a chain, it has been since 1902, and the nearest thing to an answer is four days away in Ostre and belongs to a man of eighty-one who has not answered me."

"Four miles two chains became two miles four chains," said Jory Vant, and he put his finger on the first line, "and a drain became a dyke, and a sluice became a flume sluice, and the wet common at Nether Sallow became the tank, and somebody moved a number about four hundred yards and changed every word on the line."

"Two miles four chains is a different length and not a correction," said Bess Ilk, without being asked. "Four miles two chains is the long drain. I know where the long drain ends because it goes round my brother-in-law's goat shed. Two miles four chains is the flume, and the flume has a sluice, and the sluice has a Guild plate in it, and the plate is a quarter of an inch, and I have seen it because I have walked past it four times a week for thirty-four years."

"So somebody did not correct it," said Tarin Rusk. "Somebody replaced it. And they replaced a thing that was four miles long with a thing that was two miles long and they kept the first number and gave the second one a new one, and in a book of works that is not a mistake, that is a decision, and there is a person in this village who knows who made it and is sixty-eight years old and does not remember."
