# Chapter 0051 — A Notice With Two Boxes Filled

The notice came up the hill on the Monday morning in a satchel that Niko Venn had carried from the counting-house at Vellum, and it had been pinned to the front of a sluice warden's weekly return, because that is where a thing goes in this city when the person who sent it could not afford a stamp and would not say so.

There is no stamp office below the terrace road. There is a warden's station, the return goes to four buildings every Tuesday, and anything pinned to the front of it is read by whoever opens it first, which on a Monday is a woman with a slate and a boy who cannot read.

Sluice Warden Pell read it standing up, in his own doorway, at about ten in the morning, and then he read it again, and then he said, out loud, to the lane:

"That is not for this district."

And then he unhooked it, and gave it to Niko, and said, "Take it up to him before he has made the tea, because he will read it with a cold cup and then again with a hot one and mean something different by the second reading."

---

The lane at the left of the arch was wet to about four feet from the arch stones and dry everywhere else, and the wet part was not water that anybody had put there. It was the ground remembering the fifteenth, three days ago, and giving it back, which it had been doing every morning since at about a quarter of five.

The yard cistern stood at its coping and had stood there since the afternoon of the twentieth of the month before last, and it had not gone down by anything a boy with a stick could see in three days, which was not the same as not going down. Niko's chalk on the coping read *4,700* with a second line under it, and under the second line he had written, in the small careful hand he used for things other than numbers: *not a reading of a stable tank.*

Tarin Rusk took the notice off Niko, folded it once, and read it standing up.

It was printed on a sheet of the kind that comes in pads of fifty from a chandler in a market town, and it had been printed, and not written, which meant somebody had paid for the impression and had wanted it to be the same notice all the way down the road.

> **READER-KEEPER WANTED. Sallow Reach, west of the Sere.**
> *Fourteen weeks from the first of next month. Five shillings the day, six days. A cottage and a yard at Brant with no rent.*
> *The work is to read, and to count, and to be answerable to an assembly of the villages and not to a firm.*
> *Five boxes are provided on the back and the finder is asked to complete them. Do not guess.*

And on the back, five ruled boxes in a printer's hand, and a line under them.

And two of the five were filled.

The first said *Sallow Reach*. The second said *Sallow Head works, Kerrow Water, intake, gate chamber, tank, and the West Flume from the tank to the Reach fields*.

The third said, in the printer's rule and nothing else, nothing.

The fourth said nothing.

The fifth said nothing.

And the line under the five boxes said: *Wage five shillings. Dates, the first to the fourteenth week. Cottage at Brant. Carriage from the foot of the mountain service road. The person is to be paid out of the villages and not out of any water account, and is to give no account to any firm.*

Tarin read it three times, and the third time he read it out loud, which he had never done with a document in his life and did not particularly enjoy.

"Wage five shillings," he said. "Six days. A cottage with no rent. Fourteen weeks."

He turned it over and looked at the three empty boxes the way a man looks at a form he has filled in a hundred times with two of the five.

"That is a form with two boxes filled and it is the same five-box form a warden fills in on a district road, and I want to say that out loud before I say anything else about it, because a man who has been reading forms for a month and a half gets the shape of a thing into him whether it is there or not." He put it down on the table by the door, face up, and squared it with two fingers. "It does not say who paid for the printing, and it does not say who is to be paid, and it does not say who is answerable if the cottage has no roof. And the wage is in the notice and the notice is not signed by anybody. And three of the five boxes are empty, and one of them is *source* and one of them is *keeper* and one of them is *witness*, and I am going to be careful about which of those three frightens me most."

---

Wick Venn came down the lane at about half past eleven with a barrow he was not pushing, which is how Tarin knew before he turned round that something had happened.

"Got the cart coming Friday," Wick said. "Tomas Ammid's boy with a pair of greys and a wagon that has a tire on it that ought to be three tires. Two days up. I said you would want to know."

"Yes. Thank you."

"Don't." Wick Venn put the barrow down against the wall, which he had never once done in that lane, and wiped his face with the back of his right wrist. "Don't thank me for that. I want to say a thing out loud in a yard, with people in it, and then I am going back to work and I am not going to stand about afterwards."

"Say it."

"Two months ago I carried a barrow up a switchback for four shillings a day and the surveyor gave me a shilling at the end of it as a bonus and I gave the shilling back in front of him." He held up the left hand, palm out, in the flat cold light of a lane with no sun in it, and did not make a fist, because he cannot. "This is what I am working with. I can tie a bowline with my knee on the line and the tail in my teeth. I cannot hold a bar, a pipe, a rail, a rope or a gate. That is the whole of what I have done for nineteen years and that is the whole of what I have left of it.

"And I have been to Brant. There is a room over a shop with thirty-one people in it and a woman of fifty-four called Ammid who has four books, and she wants a man who can read a head and a number and be answerable to thirty-one people, and she is offering five shillings and a cottage, and there is not one man west of the Sere who can do that work at any price because there is not one man west of the Sere who has ever been asked to.

"So I have told her your name. And I am telling you that I am recommending the man who got me three days of paid work at four shillings a day off the Assembly's witness fund in the summer, and that I would not have got it, and that I am not recommending you because of it, and I want that written down somewhere by somebody who is not me."

Tarin Rusk did not say anything for a moment, because the correct thing to say is *thank you* and he had been told not to.

"You should have told her your own name first," he said.

"I did. In the second letter. She wrote back and said no, and she was right, and she said why, and the why was that a man who cannot hold a gate cannot be a reader on a route with a gate in it, and that was a thing I had not thought of and it is the first time in a year that anybody has improved on my situation rather than apologising for it."

---

The lane filled up in the middle of the afternoon, the way it does, and Marda Ollan read the notice out in her own yard with a hundred and forty people in it or thereabouts, and Tarin stood at the back with his hat in his hand because Ondra had told him to on the Friday and had not explained why.

"Reach. Filled. Works. Filled," she said. "And those are the two that cost money to fill in and the three that are empty are the three that are free, and I want that noticed in a yard."

"Source," said Old Marden, from the front, on a stick.

"Keeper." Bel Crale said the last one and then said, "That is the one he will not put his name to, and he is right, and I have been saying for six weeks that the keeper box is the whole of what a warden may put in a box and I am not going to put the end of it in a box. And if the man west of the Sere has to fill three boxes, two of them are *source* and *witness*, then he is going to have to go and find a witness, and every person in this yard knows what happened the last time a man in this city went looking for a witness and found one by accident in a lane where he happened to be standing with a bar."

Nobody argued with Bel Crale and nobody thanked her, which is the ordinary condition of being right in that lane.

---

Perdie Ammid was in the yard.

That is the whole of it and it is not a small thing, because a notice pinned to a warden's return is read by whoever opens the return, and a woman who has learned that will do what she did, which is to stand in the yard herself and read it out loud before anybody can read it wrong.

She had come down on the Tuesday morning on the coach from the west with a cloth bundle under her arm and two cheeses in a basket, and she had walked out of the coach at the second gatehouse and up the mill road on purpose, because a man can be met at a coach stand and cannot be met at a gate, and she had a great deal to say and was not going to say it in a coach.

She put the four books on Bel Crale's bench under her cloth and did not open them, and nobody touched them, because she had not put her hand on them either.

"I wrote that notice," she said. "I paid for the printing. I am not the Guild and I am not the Office and I am not a firm and I have not been any of those things in my life, and I want the first thing said about me in this yard to be that and not something else.

"I have been keeping a record of the water of the Sallow Reach for thirty-one years. Four books. They are not a record. I have wanted them to be a record for twenty-six years and nobody has ever let them be one, and the reason nobody has let them is that a record is a thing somebody else keeps, and the moment it is somebody else's it is gone, and I have watched it happen twice to people I knew.

"So the notice says: pay him out of the villages and not out of any water account, and give no account to any firm, and there are forty-one villages in this Reach and the wage is thirty shillings a week, which is a pound and ten, and fourteen weeks of it is twenty-one pounds, and every one of those three numbers is on the front of the sheet in print. And the reason it says that is not modesty. It is because in this basin a thing that belongs to a water account can be closed, and a thing that belongs to a firm can be priced, and a thing that belongs to forty-one villages can be argued with, and I have wanted to argue with somebody about water for thirty-one years."

"And the five boxes," said Marda Ollan.

"And the five boxes. Two of them filled in a printer's hand, because I carried the pad to a printer at Culm and gave him my two lines and he set the whole sheet out of politeness. Reach, and works. The other three I left empty on purpose and I have been arguing about it for eleven days with two men who sell me things.

"The third box is *source*. Do not put a source in it. There is no source. I have asked and I have been told by a man with a rod in his hand that there is no source, and I have believed him, and if you put a source in that box you will have to take it out again in front of forty-one villages and I am not doing that twice in a year.

"The fourth is *keeper*. Do not put a keeper in it. I have gone round eleven of the forty-one villages this month with a slate and I have asked eleven assemblies whether they will accept a keeper appointed by a firm, and nine of the eleven said no, and one said it wanted to think about it, and I am the eleventh and I am thinking about it, and there are thirty villages west of here that I have not been to, and that is the honest state of it.

"The fifth is *witness*. Leave it. You will find a witness or you will not, and if you cannot find a witness then there is no window and there should be no window, and I would rather have a man who goes home in a fortnight than a man who writes down whatever he has been given."

"Then the notice asks for three boxes to be left empty," said Tarin Rusk.

"The notice asks for three boxes to be left empty," said Perdie Ammid, "and every man in this yard thinks it is a trick, and it is not a trick, and I will now say the thing that is actually the whole of it, and I would like the woman with the book to write it down, because I am not going to say it twice.

"A route needs a name, and a name needs a record, and a record needs somebody to keep it, and there is a man in the Reach who can do all four of those things for a fee of five shillings a day, and the fee is the easy part. The hard part is that the moment there is a keeper, the water has an owner of its record, and a record with an owner can be closed, and I have buried a husband who was the owner of four books and I have still got the books and I have not got him.

"I do not want a keeper. I want somebody to count."

---

She was in the yard for two hours and she was asked about four things and she answered three of them.

The fourth came from the woman from the top end with the slate, who said that a notice with three empty boxes and five shillings a day was a thing a person goes sixty-one miles for, and that a man who went for it would be doing it for the wage and not for the water, and that in this lane they had all learned that a man can want a thing and be honest about wanting it and still be wrong.

"I am asking him to be paid," said Perdie Ammid. "I have put the wage on the notice in print, which I have never done in my life, and I put it there because he is going to be asked to do work that nobody west of the Sere can do, and I am not going to have the man who does it be a man who cannot feed himself at the end of it. I would like somebody in this yard to notice that I have said that out loud instead of saying that I want the water."

The woman from the top end wrote it down, because she writes everything down, and Perdie Ammid looked at the slate and said, "That is the first time in four years that anybody has written down the right thing about me and I am going to be difficult about it for a week."

And then Tarin Rusk told her about the two charges, in the yard, in daylight, in front of a hundred and forty people, and he told her the thing on the notice second and the thing about himself first, which is the correct order and which she noticed and which Wick Venn also noticed and which neither of them said anything about.

Two charges, live, never joined, never resolved, one since the tenth of the month before last and one since the twelfth of this month, and a third that existed as a conversation and had not had a sheet of it in anybody's hand. A name in a Basin Assembly transcript four times, in four buildings, with a date on it. No office, no district, no money, and a room he had been given. And sixty-one miles away from here, on a ration cut by a fifth, a district of a hundred and twenty-six people with six thousand seven hundred gallons of the same crosscurrent water at the coping of a tank and no municipal supply of any kind, and a figure of sixty-eight gallons a day standing in a column that was empty, and none of it any use to him this morning.

In the middle of that, Perdie Ammid asked the only question anybody in the yard asked.

"Will you be able to come back?"

"There is a form for leaving the district and there is nobody in the city who can sign it," said Tarin Rusk, "and if there is, he will be looking at two live charges and a man going sixty-one miles to a village that has not asked this city for anything, and I do not know what he will do and I do not know that it will be right."

"Then I will not have you," said Perdie Ammid, "and I will not have Wick Venn either, and I will not have any of the four, and I will put the notice up again in March when the ground is hard and there is a four-month wait in it instead of a fourteen-week one, and that is what I have done twice."

"Then do not," said Tarin Rusk. "Because the reason there is no signature on the form is that there is no officer, and the reason there is no officer is a certificate and a chamber and a man who did not go down, and that is four miles away and it is a book, and a book is not a reason for a village to lose a season.

"I will go on Friday. I will go on the cart. And when I am sixty-one miles away with nobody able to stop me, you will want to know whether I am still counting. So we will do it this way: I do the work, I am paid the wage, and if anybody comes up that road to take me off it, I am to be allowed to say so on a Tuesday, in a yard, in daylight, in front of the assembly, and I will say so, and they may take me."

Perdie Ammid looked at him for a while and then said, "That is a notice condition and you have put it in a yard and not in a book, and I do not know whether that is clever or whether you have run out of book."

---

The amendment went in at the warden's station on the Tuesday afternoon, and it took eleven minutes, and it was the only thing Tarin Rusk did in that week that was entirely correct.

The notice as it stood named a wage and dates and a cottage and a man, and named no source and no keeper and no witness. A notice that names a man and no route is a notice that a man's name arrives before anybody has agreed what he is arriving to do.

So the notice was to be read as two sheets, the second in his own hand, setting out the two charges with the dates, fastened to the first in a way that could not be separated, and carrying a line stating that it was part of the notice.

Pell read both sheets standing up, in his own doorway, the same way he had read the first one, and then he said the thing that made him worth two counties.

"There is no staple in this office and no thread and no wax," he said. "And there is no box on this form for a second sheet, and I will not put a second sheet in a box that is not for it. I will write the two charges into the district book in a fair hand, on the Tuesday, and I will write that the notice is to be read with the entry, and I will write the entry out at the foot of the notice in my own hand, and I will put the mark of this office against it, and that mark is a circle with a cross in it, and anybody who has been in this lane knows it."

"Will a village be able to read your mark?"

"A village will be able to ask whether a circle with a cross in it is on it," said Sluice Warden Pell. "That is the same thing and it is better, and I have been doing this for nineteen years and I have watched about four hundred notices go down this road and I have never once seen anybody ask."

He read the entry out first, which is his rule, and wrote it afterwards, and then he read out what he had written.

> *Tuesday, the nineteenth day of this month. Entered at the warden's station, terrace road: a notice, printed, for a reader-keeper at Sallow Head, Sallow Reach, for fourteen weeks from the first of next month, at five shillings the day, six days, a cottage and yard at Brant at no rent.*
> *The notice names no source, no keeper and no witness. Three of its five boxes are empty and the notice says so.*
> *There are two charges standing against the person named, the second of them on the twelfth day of this month and the first of them on the tenth day of the month before last, both live, neither joined and neither resolved, and a third matter of which there is no sheet. The person named has asked that this be read with the notice, and this is entered at his asking and not at mine.*

"Read that back to me," said Tarin Rusk, "and then read it back again to Niko Venn, because he cannot read and he is the one who will be asked."

Pell read it back twice.

---

The leave application was the last thing and it is the reason the Tuesday was a wasted day after nine in the morning.

There is a form. It is one side of a sheet and it is the only short form in the district and it has been the same since before anybody alive was clerked. It asks for the name, the office, the date, and **the district of residence**, and the district of residence is a line that has to be filled in, and there is no box for *none*.

Tarin Rusk filled it in standing at the counter at a quarter past eleven on the Tuesday morning with a pen that did not want to write, and he wrote *none held*, and then he read the whole sentence twice.

There were four buildings in this city with a district in them and a keeper in them and a row in them, and there was a warden's return in a book with four copies of it that read, in a fair hand, in the fifth box of five, *keeper, none held*.

He had written the same two words in a box of his own.

He signed it, and dated it the nineteenth, and countersigned in the warden's presence because the form says so, and walked it to the second gatehouse, where the notice post is, and it was the nineteenth day of the month and the office at the far end of it has had no acting commissioner since the fifteenth of this month, two days before this one, at half past eleven on a night of rain, and the last thing in the registry book is a determination that a vacancy exists.

The clerk at the counter was a young man of about twenty-five who had been in the job nine weeks and who had a list on the desk of the things that were waiting for a signature, and there were thirty-one of them.

"This will sit," he said. "It has no officer to go to. I can send it to the commissioners, but the commissioners need a requisition, and a requisition is a chair's, and the chair is not the office." He put it on the list, which was the correct action, and then he said, without any malice in it at all: "You are the man off the terrace road, are you not. My uncle had the boiler out on your account."

"Yes."

"Then I will tell you what I would tell anybody." He squared the application on the pile. "A man on a stand-down is not to be out of the district without the written consent of the registry. And there is no written consent, because there is nobody to give it, and because a consent is a piece of paper from a man with a seal, and there is no seal in the office at the moment and has not been since the sixteenth."

"So a form has to go out four miles and come back with a signature, and there is nobody to sign it."

"Yes," said the clerk. "And when one of you two is appointed, it will go to him and he will look at it, and he will see that a man with two live charges wants to go sixty-one miles to a village that has not asked this city for anything, and he will do what is right, and I do not know what that is and neither do you."

Tarin Rusk thanked him, which was correct, and walked back down the gatehouse road with nothing in his hands.

It was a mile and a quarter back to the arch. On the way he worked out what he had, which was eleven pence, a bar, a folding rule, a chalk stub, a knuckle of string, a tin pot, a blue book with four and not one in it, and a bench book out of a shed at Bexhill that a man of fifty-four had pushed four inches across a table at eight in the evening for nothing, and which had in it, on the Tuesday mornings of nine years, the notation of about eleven thousand gate settings made by about four hundred different hands.

And the notice was going to the Sallow Reach on Saturday on a wagon with a tire that ought to be three tires, and Wick Venn was going with it, and there was no form in the district, or in the Office, or in the Assembly, that said a man with two live charges might go and read a number for a wage of five shillings.

There were sixty-one miles of that between here and a plank in a drain, and five boxes on a printed sheet, and three of them empty, and a woman who has just told him to leave them empty and has taken twenty-six years telling people why.
