# Chapter 0094 — Three Of Four

He asked three of them on the Saturday and the three were asked properly, one at a time, in daylight, each one told what the job was before they were told they were wanted, and each one told who else was on it, and each one given the right to say no until the following Wednesday.

Sef Ordry was the first and he was in the shed at the foot of the embankment and it took about four minutes and neither of them wasted one.

"The gate. Eleven o'clock, and six minutes past three, and the chain comes off and goes on and I do the chain. Nobody else touches the chain, including you, and that is not a rank, it is that I have opened and shut this gate twice and the second time was the fifteenth of the month before this one and I put the chain on at ten past ten and I would like to do it once more properly."

"Properly," said Tarin Rusk.

"With a bar and a grease and somebody to hold the lamp," said Sef Ordry, "and the inspection cover's padlock is going to come off as well, because it is on the cover and not on the gate and there is no sense in a man saying a chain is off while a padlock is on the cover of the thing he is talking about."

He said yes before Tarin Rusk had finished, and then he asked one question, which was whether anybody had told the boy of fifteen, and was told that the boy of fifteen was going to be asked at one o'clock, and said, "Then ask him before you ask anybody else, and do not ask him in front of anybody, and do not tell him what it is worth."

Wick Venn was second and it was in the yard at about half past eleven, standing at a gate he could not get through without lifting his right knee, which is how he gets through it.

"The outfall," said Tarin Rusk. "Two miles four chains up the launder and then the last stone, and there is a drop of about four feet onto the long drain, and you are the only man in this party who can be trusted with a watch, and I have said that out loud in a room before and I am saying it to your face now and it is not a favour, it is the reason you are being asked and it is the reason you are the only one I am putting at that end."

"You are putting me two miles from anybody," said Wick Venn.

"I am."

"Then I want to say the thing I am going to say and I have not said it to anybody and I am saying it to you because you are going to be at the top and I am going to be at the bottom. **I cannot hold a bar, a pipe, a rail, a lamp-post or a cistern lid, and if that launder puts a stone into that field at two o'clock in the morning then the four of us are four men standing about, and the only thing in this party that can do anything about it is me, and I cannot do it.**"

"I know," said Tarin Rusk.

"You have known since the sixth of the month before this one."

"I have known since then," said Tarin Rusk, "and I have put you at the end of a two-mile launder on Thursday night, and I am not going to pretend to you that those two facts are not both true, and I have not got a third thing to offer you. What I have got is that you will hear it before I do, and that is a job and not a comfort, and if it is not enough then say so now and I will take you off it and put the boy on the board and I will not think less of you than I did at half past eleven this morning."

Wick Venn looked at the gate for a while.

"Say yes," he said.

The boy of fifteen was third and he was asked at one o'clock at the kerb outside the shop, alone, in daylight, with nothing said to him beforehand, and the whole of it took about two minutes.

"You are at the tank with the level staff and a watch and a slate, and the slate is yours and nobody else's and you write what you see and not what you are told you are seeing. The water at the tank is going to fall about an eighth of an inch over four hours and a bit, and it has not moved an eighth of an inch in six years, and if it falls an eighth of an inch in four hours that is the first fact about this works in six years that anybody in this basin has been able to see, and I want it in your hand and not in anybody's.

"And if the water does not move at all, you write that it did not move, and you come and find me, and we stop."

The boy of fifteen said yes, and then he asked one question.

"Am I the fourth?"

"You are one of four," said Tarin Rusk.

"You asked a man of thirty-one in a shed and a man of thirty-eight at a gate and me at a kerb," said the boy of fifteen, "and there are four of us, and that is three, and I have been on this Reach's roster since the first of this month and that is seventeen days and I have been asked nothing in any of them."

"There are four of us," said Tarin Rusk. "Yes."

"Who is the other one."

And Tarin Rusk said, "You will know in the morning," and went up the mountain, and it was the worst sentence of the week and he said it in the ordinary voice and it was not a lie and it was not a kindness, and it was the decision of a man who has been the second one since he was thirty-one and had been told in a room on Saturday by a woman of forty-one that this was the whole of his trouble.

---

Jory Vant found out at about four o'clock on the Saturday afternoon, in the front room of the shop, with about nine people in it, from the mouth of the boy of fifteen, who told him because he had been asked not to and could not keep it and was fifteen.

Jory Vant is nineteen years old and he is an apprentice of Bexhill and of no district, and for a month he has been the person in this valley who was asked. He read four lines of five off a form and signed a statement saying he was not asked to copy the fifth. He took a count on the third of this month and wrote the figure down before anybody told him what it was. He wrote the fourth rod on a card and ruled it himself and headed it in a woman's hand and signed under it, and nobody in that room asked him to, and he has said since that it was the best four minutes of his life.

He stood in the middle of the shop floor and did not say anything for about eleven seconds.

"Is my name on the sheet," he said.

Perdie Ammid put her hand on the page and turned it round, and it was, and it was in the fourth space, and the space had his name in it in a hand that was not his and not anybody's, and it had been there since the Wednesday, and he had entered it himself as a copyist on the Thursday at four o'clock and had not thought about the four spaces.

"Yes," said Perdie Ammid.

"You entered my name in a space."

"I entered the name of the person who copied a sheet for me, in the space headed for it," said Perdie Ammid, "on the Thursday afternoon, at four o'clock, in front of eleven people, and you were in the room and you read the sheet out to me and I did not ask you. That is my hand and not yours and I will not have anybody say it is yours."

"I am not saying it is mine," said Jory Vant. "I am saying that I came up this lane with a fitter in the middle of the month before this one and I have been in this valley a month, and **every name in it that is worth a halfpenny is a name somebody asked me for, and I have one that nobody asked me for, and it is under a rod on a card at a culvert, and I signed it.**"

"Then you have two," said Tarin Rusk.

The room did not do anything at all.

X "I counted you as the man who already knew, and you did know, because you were in this room on Thursday night when I said it in front of nine people and you have been copying things for this shop since the second day. **That was my decision and it was wrong and I am not going to take it back**, because taking it back would make it a mistake of the head and it is not a mistake of the head, it is the thing I have been doing since I was thirty-one, and I told this room in the spring that the second one has cost more people than the first one ever would have, and I have proved it again with nineteen words and a boy of fifteen at a kerb."

"You are asking me to be in it," said Jory Vant, "and you are telling me you did not ask me, and I have to do both of those at once, and I have got until Wednesday to say no and I have not got a reason."

"You have not got a reason," said Tarin Rusk. "That is mine and not yours. If you had one I would use it and I have told you I would not."

"I know what you would do," said Jory Vant, "because you have done it four times in a month and been right three times, and I am the fourth, and I am not a hole in a pipe."

Nobody rescued him. That is on the page and it is the whole of what the room did between four o'clock and half past five on the Saturday afternoon, and it is that nobody in it said one word to make it easier, and the woman of fifty-four who is the only person in this basin who can say what a record costs did not say what it costs, and the man of thirty-one who stated his own bill in front of twenty-six people four days ago said nothing at all, and at about half past five the boy of fifteen went out to the yard and came back in and said that the level staff was four feet and that the tank was two feet six and an eighth and that the staff wanted a board under the foot of it, and after that the room did the ordinary thing and talked about a board and a slate.

At about six, Wick Venn, who had gone up the lane to look at a gate he could not get through, came back and asked whether the crew had been told, and Tarin Rusk told him.

"Then you have not told the last one," said Wick Venn, "and you have not told me, and I am going to say the thing and then I am going to go home.

"**A man who is going to do a thing on a Saturday tells the men who have to be there on the Saturday.** Not asks. Tells. And he tells them before he does it, while they can still say no. That is my sentence, I said it on Sunday in a lane to a boy of fifteen and I said in a room on Tuesday that I would say it twice and never a third time, and I have said it three times and I have breached it twice in a shed and once in a room, and I breached it the second time on purpose and the third time by accident, and the third time was on this afternoon and it was you and not me.

"And I am not going to stand in this shop and pretend I am the injured one," said Wick Venn, "because I got a job out of it and I asked you for it, and I have known all afternoon that somebody in this room was going to be the one who had not been asked and I worked out who about an hour ago and I did not say anything, because it is not my standing to say it and I have none. **Somebody cost him a day on Saturday and I knew it by two o'clock and I went and looked at a gate.**"

Jory Vant said, "You did not cost me anything," and Wick Venn said, "No," and went home, and the room did what rooms do, and Perdie Ammid wrote the whole of it out on a sheet of paper on the Saturday evening in one block, the three that were asked and the one that was not, with the date on it, and at the top of the sheet, in her own hand, she wrote the one word **copy**, and it is the first time in thirty-one years that she has written that word at the top of a page of her own hand, and she said it out loud before she wrote anything else on it.

"Because the book went to Vellum on Friday morning," she said, "and there is no book in this shop, and this is the second sheet I have had to begin since Friday, and the first one was the four books going to Vellum, and I have read both of them out twice to about nine people, and **a copy that has been read out is a copy, and a copy that has not been read out is a rumour with a coat on, and there is no third thing.**"

And then she wrote it a second time on the same sheet, under a line, in a hand that got smaller, because Wick Venn had been in the shop on the Thursday night and had said out loud that it goes in a column or it does not go in anything, **and the column is in a cart.**

The two of these are on a sheet and they are not a line of that column and they are not going to become one, because the column is in the cart and a man does not get two things into a thing that is a thing. They went to Vellum on the Friday cart in the same satchel as the books, in a hand that got smaller and is the same hand. There is still nothing at the bottom of the column, and a number is due there in about a year, and a man of thirty-eight with one hand has said that he wants to be alive to see it and does not think he is going to be the only one who is.
