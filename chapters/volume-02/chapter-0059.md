# Chapter 0059 — A Window Is A Date And Not A Wish

The planting window at the Sallow Reach opens on the fifteenth day of next month and it is a Monday, and it is a date and not a wish, and Perdie Ammid had it written on the back of the 1902 slip without knowing that she was doing it.

"First ten days," she said. "After the tenth the ground is like a bench and the seed will sit in it and rot, and a man can either have a field or have a story about a field. Nine hundred acres of apple in this Reach alone, and apple is set in the last ten days of the window or it is not set at all, and I have lost two orchards in my life and I am not going to talk about the second one."

The reach is seventeen hundred acres of which nine hundred are apple, and about two hundred and ninety households, and about nine hundred people, and none of those figures is in a schedule. They are in Perdie Ammid's second book, and the second book is the oldest of the four, and it is not in Perdie Ammid's hand in the first fifty pages.

"You cannot cost it in the way the Guild costs it," said Tarin Rusk. "I have been trying since Friday and I cannot, and the reason is that the Reach counts in gallons a household a day and Cresset counts in gallons a head a day, and there is no instrument in this basin that converts one into the other without a census, and there has not been a census of the Sallow Reach since the assize, which is a very long time ago and was done by men counting chimneys."

"Who counts in what," said Domen Prouse, who had come in with his coat on.

"Four gallons a household a day. That is the number in the fourth book and it is not the number in the assize, and I had the two of them run together in my head for two days, and the assize is the one for the land and the four gallons is the one for the house, and they differ by about seventy-nine times and I would not have believed that in a city.

"The four is in every one of the four books in a different hand, and it is a fifth of what a house of four people in this valley drinks in a dry summer, and that is the arithmetic of it and it is right and it has been right since before anybody's grandmother, and I have checked it three ways and I cannot improve on it."

"And what do they count in the other place?"

"Something not worth arguing about," said Tarin Rusk, "and I am not going to say what, because it is not mine to say, and because the whole difficulty of the business is that there are two figures in this basin for a household's water and I am the man who has spent nine years learning what happens when a number goes in a book with a name on it and I have got two live charges for it."

Which was true, and which was not the reason, and everybody in the room understood that it was not the reason and let it stand, and it was the first of three things in this basin that Tarin Rusk decided to keep because holding it was easier than giving it away.

---

The number that moved the room was not his and it was two hundred and fifty years old and it was in a book in a house at Culm, and a man of sixty-eight had let him have it out of a locked press on the strength of nothing.

The assize of the settling of the Reach sets out what a house is worth in water, and it is done in a hand that is a copy of a hand two hundred years older than itself, and the copy is good.

> *The water of a house in the lower field: in the season, ninety-four thousand five hundred gallons, delivered in four days at four several times, and the four several times to be appointed in writing by the assembly of the villages and not by any officer.*
> *The water of a house in the upper field: the whole of the water of the house in the lower field, and no more.*
> *A house that does not take its water at the four several times shall forfeit the price of it, and the price of it is the water and nothing else.*

"A fifth of ninety-four thousand five hundred," said Tarin Rusk, "is eighteen thousand nine hundred."

"It is," said the man of sixty-eight.

"Over four days, that is four thousand seven hundred and twenty-five gallons a household a day, for four days, four times."

"It is."

"For two hundred and ninety households, one fifth, for the season: five million four hundred and eighty-one thousand gallons. And four thousand seven hundred and twenty-five gallons a household a day, for four days, and I have had that wrong in my head for two days and I want to say so in a room rather than have somebody else find it.

"That is the water of the land a house holds," said Tarin Rusk, "and it is not the water a house drinks, and the difference between the two numbers is about seventy-nine times. The woman two days up this lane puts sixty gallons a day into a cart and calls that a good day, and her grandmother's generation called that a wet week, and neither of them has anything to do with four thousand seven hundred and twenty-five."

"Then what is it," said Domen Prouse.

"It is the water of the fields. It has to be, or the assize is a mad document, and the assize is not a mad document, because it has been copied by a hand two centuries older than itself and the copy is good."

Nobody in that room said anything for about eleven seconds, which is a long time.

"Now," said Tarin Rusk, "on the other side of that. A stone throat at the embankment, measured on Friday evening by a plate fitter with a length of lath and a watch, at eight hundred and sixty gallons an hour. That is twenty thousand six hundred and forty gallons a day. It is seven million five hundred and thirty-three thousand six hundred gallons in a year.

"And the tank it comes out of has not moved a quarter of an inch in six years, and there is a card with two men's signatures on every line of it, and the card goes to the counting-house at Vellum once a year in a cart, and nobody has ever read one.

"So there is more water coming out of that works than the Reach has been getting in six years by a factor I cannot make my mouth say, and the reason the Reach has had nothing is not that there is nothing. The reason is a shut sluice, a chain, a six-inch waste pipe, and two men in a shed who have read the same volume for eight years and have never read the back of the ninth."

Domen Prouse put his hand flat on the table.

"Then what has been taken," he said.

"Nothing has been taken. That is the point and it took me four days and a lath to say it. Nothing has been taken from anybody. There is a tank on a hill that is full to a mark it has not moved off in six years, and a sluice that was shut six years ago, and a pipe that was put in to empty a chamber and has been weeping out of its own crown joint for six years, and the water that was going down a flume is going over a waste pipe instead, and it is coming out of a stone throat on the west side of a road two feet eight inches below the top of the road, and it is falling into a drain.

"And the drain is the long drain, and the long drain has a village at the bottom of it."

"Four days," said Nesta Sarg, from the wall. "You have known it four days."

"I have known the throat for four hours."

"Four days of it in books and four hours of it with a lath," said Nesta Sarg, "and on Friday night you wrote on the back of a card in a shed that you would not open it, and this morning you are in a room telling a floorful of farmers there is more water in this Reach than they have ever had, and I would like to know which of those two men you are."

"I am the second one," said Tarin Rusk, "and I have been the second one since I was thirty-one, and I have never been the first one, and Doctor Sen in the lower fold will tell you that the second one has cost more people than the first one ever would have."

---

The other thing happened after the meeting, on the road down from Brant, and it is the reason that meeting was not the end of anything.

The old crossing stones are at the third mile below the intake on the lower Sere, on the lane that runs down to the Reach, and they are two hundred and fifty years old, and they are kerb stones out of a bridge, and somebody took them out of the bridge and set them up on the bank with a face carved on the one that is still standing.

> *The oath of the Sere.*
> *1. I will not take the water of the Sere without the whole of the Sere knowing.*
> *2. I will pay for what I take, at the price of the thing, and not at the price of the asking.*
> *3. I will be named among the takers, so that a man who is thirsty can come to the door of the man who has the water.*
> *4. I will not take it in a year when the taking is easier and the giving is not.*

He read it twice and sat down on the bank for about ten minutes and then told the three of them standing there the whole of it, word for word, in the exact words, and there were five people on that bank and four of them could not read and had never seen it.

Niko's not here. Wick Venn is. Nesta Sarg is. Domen Prouse is. And Perdie Ammid.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Sixty-one miles east," said Perdie Ammid, "in a city with a bookshop and a printing office and a law about anything you please. And here it is on a bank, two hundred and fifty years old, four lines, in stone, in a lane of nineteen houses, and Aldis Kerr is seventy-one and has been alive seventy-one of the two hundred and fifty years of it, and the other hundred and seventy-nine years of that stone have not had anybody at all standing in front of it."

"Some of us can read," said Nesta Sarg.

"You have just read it aloud to me for the first time," said Perdie Ammid, "and you said at the start of the walk that nobody in this lane had ever told you it was there."

"Since I was nine."

"Then it has been on that bank for a hundred and forty years in the lifetime of the oldest woman in this Reach and it has not once done a day's work, and I want you to understand that I am not being sentimental when I tell you that I am not going to have it read out in an assembly on Wednesday."

"Why not," said Tarin Rusk.

"Because it will do what you have just done to me," said Perdie Ammid. "It will make a thing of this that everybody has to have an opinion about, and an opinion is a thing you have to be able to give in a room, and there are about eleven people in this Reach who can give one and none of them are the people the oath is written for.

"And there is the other thing, and I am going to say it because we are on a bank and nobody is taking minutes. A stone with four lines on it is not a right. It is a piece of evidence. And the moment this Reach has a piece of evidence about the Sere, somebody will start a case with it, and the case will be fought over the Sere and not over the tank, and the tank will be shut for the duration by a man who has a schedule and a chain and a card, and in four years' time there will be a room in this basin with lawyers in it, and I have been to that room. I sat in it for two days in the year the assize book came up from Vellum and I watched a woman of eighty lose eleven acres over a foot of a bank.

"I am not giving this Reach a stone. I am giving it a rod and a pail and a day."

And Tarin Rusk said, "You are right, and I should have thought of it before the bank and not after it, and I have been thinking about it since the bank."

"Yes," said Perdie Ammid. "I have watched you have that three times this month and I have watched you have it about four other times in two years that I was not here for. It is a very expensive habit and you are the only person in the basin who cannot afford it."

---

He wrote to Mara Quill on the Sunday night at the top of the embankment, by a lamp, in a hand that was not his best, and it was four lines and it was the first thing he had written to her in two months that was not about evidence.

> *The Reach has a window on the fifteenth of next month and a day to count on, the fourteenth, and a village that has refused me a name in front of forty-one people and written the refusal into a book it has kept in a chest for twenty-six years, and the refusal cost the woman who keeps the book more than anything I have ever done to anybody.*
> *Eighteen thousand nine hundred gallons, per household, is the figure a room will believe. It is two hundred and fifty years old and it is in a locked press at Culm and it came out of a copy of a copy.*
> *I have stood next to two hundred and thirty-nine lines of a Guild specification book for three days in a shed and I have not copied one of them and I have not asked to, and I am telling you that before anybody asks me whether I have, and I have not got a reason why I am telling you rather than waiting to be asked, and I have thought about it for an hour.*
> *The witness box is still empty and a woman of seventy-one has said she will not fill it.*

And then, on the Monday morning, in a yard at Brant, six people wrote a duty roster on a page that had been ruled for something else, which was a page of the second book, and the second book is the oldest of the four.

Eight hours on and eight off, four in the yard and two at the plank, and somebody's mother, and somebody's boy, and a man of thirty-three who is paid five shillings a day and is on the roster in the fourth week of the six.
