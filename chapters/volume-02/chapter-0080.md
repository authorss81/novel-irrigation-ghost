# Chapter 0080 — The Last Saturday In The Month

The tank card is read on the last Saturday in the month. That is printed on the card itself, in a line of type at the foot of it, and it has been printed there for as long as anybody can remember, and the reason for it is in the tank: a level wants a body of water behind it that does not change on a week, and a week is a body of water that does not change.

The thirty-first day of this month is a Wednesday.

The last Saturday in this month was the twenty-seventh day of this month, and it was the twenty-seventh because the first of this month was a Monday, and everybody in that room could have told you that with their hands, and it took Perdie Ammid about nine minutes to say it out loud in the front room of the shop at Brant on the Wednesday morning, sitting down, with a pencil and the back of a broadsheet, because she is a woman of fifty-four who has kept a calendar in her head for thirty-one years and has never once had to use it out loud and did not enjoy it.

"The twenty-seventh was the last Saturday in the month," she said. "And there were four men in this room at a quarter past eleven on the twenty-seventh and one of them read three names out loud, and Jory Vant was in this room, and the boy of fifteen was in this room at about half past twelve and was sent out to the smithy, and there was no count taken at the tank, at the plank or at the flume sluice on the twenty-seventh.

"So the count is not read this month. It has not been read on the last Saturday in the month, and the reason is not a padlock, and the reason is not a drought and it is not a field. The reason is a Saturday, and a Saturday went into a room in this village and did not come out of it, and the card at the culvert on that post says under the third rod that the figure there is not zero and is not the same as zero and is on a padlock, and that is true, and it is now not the whole of it, and I am not going to take the padlock off that card, because the padlock is the reason and a Saturday is what happened in a room, and a card is a card and not an account of a week."

---

"So when," said Bess Ilk, from behind the counter.

"Not this month," said Perdie Ammid. "The card is read on the last Saturday, and the last Saturday has been, so the next one is the last Saturday of next month, and that is a fortnight off, and it is in a different month from the window, and there is not one date in this month left that anybody in this valley can use.

"Unless we do what we are allowed to do, which is read it on a Saturday. And there is a Saturday coming. It is the third day of next month. The first of next month is a Thursday and the second is a Friday and the third is a Saturday, and I have got that off the same pencil and I have checked it twice, and I would like somebody else to check it, and nobody ever checks my calendar, and it is a thing I have complained about for thirty-one years and I am not complaining about it today because today it is the only piece of arithmetic in this month that nobody can be got to change."

Nineteen people were in that room on the Wednesday morning and about nine of them said the same thing at the same time, and Bess Ilk said it loudest.

"That is the first day in about six weeks this Reach has been able to name that is not in this month," she said. "The count was on the fourteenth and the window was on the fifteenth and everything anybody has said in this room for a month has had a number in it that ends in this month, and you have just given us a number that does not."

"The sowing shut on the twenty-fourth and the count is on the third of next month, which is ten days after it," said Perdie Ammid. "So on the day we take the next count the ground will have been like a bench for ten days, and there is no window on that day, and the next sowing is not a thing anybody in this basin has a date for, and I have looked, and there is no date, and I am not going to invent one, and that is a thing I intend to be asked about in a room before the end of next week and I intend to say that I do not know.

"And the card has to be printed. It has three rods on it and it is a two-colour card and there is no press in this village and there is no press in this valley, and the last one that went to Culm was a card cut in this room and carried up the road on a Friday, and the press at Culm is a press of stone and type and a man of sixty-eight who opens a locked drawer for nothing, and the next Friday is the ninth of next month, and a card that goes up on the ninth comes back on the twenty-first.

"**So the count is on the third of next month and the card is printed on the twenty-first of next month, and between those two dates there is a hole on a post at a culvert that anybody may look at, and on the twenty-first there will be a new card on that post with a third rod empty and a fourth rod that did not exist a month ago, and nobody will be able to do anything about the third one and somebody will be able to do something about the fourth.**

"And I am not going to say one more word about that and anybody who wants to ask me about it is going to have to ask the man in Culm, and he is sixty-eight and he has not had a card in his press since the year the assize came up, and he will want to know what goes in the empty space, and I am not going to tell him."

---

The seventh line of the card in the shed door was written on the Wednesday morning at about eleven, in the shed at the foot of the embankment, by a man of thirty-one, and it is the first entry on that card in eleven years and it is not a clearing.

The card is the size of a man's hand and it is nailed to a shed door and it has six entries on it and the six of them are clearings, and the last of them was eight years ago and is in a different hand from the ones before it, and there is a seventh line and the seventh line has been empty since about the year a Guild specification book says a chamber at Sallow Head is void and no plate is fitted and no instrument is required.

Sef Ordry read the six of them and then wrote on the seventh, and then read it out twice, and then said:

"That is not a clearing and I have written it in the line where a clearing goes, and in eleven years a man standing in this door will read six clearings and a paragraph and he will have to decide what it means and he will decide it wrongly, and I have thought about that for about an hour this morning and I have decided I do not care, because a card with an empty line in it reads like a clean chamber, and I have been the man who left it empty, and a man who leaves a line empty for eleven years has not got the right to be the man who notices it is empty.

"*The seventh line of this card is not a clearing. There has been no clearing at this work in eleven years and there is not going to be one, and the flume sluice has been shut and chained for six of them, and the chain is not renewed and there is no keeper and there has not been one. A plate was requisitioned for the stone throat on the thirtieth day of this month and it will not be fitted until a keeper is named, and a keeper is named by an assembly of a district, and there is no district. The count is read on a Saturday and the last Saturday in this month was the twenty-seventh of this month and it was not taken, and the reason is a room in a village, and the reason for that is on a page of a book in a village and is not mine.*"

"That is a long line for a small card," said Jory Vant.

"It is eleven years long," said Sef Ordry. "You would write it shorter if you had done six of the entries and one of these."

---

And then the thing that is on the card in the end, which is not a date and is not a figure and is the last thing anybody in the Sallow Reach put down on the thirtieth day of this month, was put down by a man of thirty-eight with one hand who has been doing it since June in a lane four hundred miles from here and has never once been thanked for it in either county.

Wick Venn did it at about four in the afternoon in the yard at Brant, with a pencil, on the back of a broadsheet, and he said it out loud so that a woman of fifty-four could write it down, and it took him about four minutes, and it is in the second book in her hand on a page of its own.

"The counting of the fourteenth of this month was four and a half hours. Two rods read and one rod with a padlock on it, and a barrow and two pails and a lath and a watch, and about six people, and that is what a count costs, and it has been the same since the second of this month and it will be the same on the third of next month.

"The twenty-seventh was five and a half hours of my barrow and a lath and a boy of fifteen, and I was not counting anything, and the man whose paper it was was in a room with fourteen people in it, and I do not say that to make anybody feel bad about it and I would like it noticed that I have not said it to make anybody feel bad about it.

"Five and a half hours and four and a half hours is ten hours, and ten hours is a day and an hour of the kind of day anybody in this basin counts, and a day is what I said in a yard on the fourteenth day of this month in front of about four hundred people and nobody thanked me and I did not want to be. I said: **a count is a day and a window is a morning, and every village in this yard can give a morning and not one of them can give a day.**

"And on Saturday a room in this village gave a morning and took a day, and it did it with paper. That is the whole of it. I have been in this Reach six weeks and I have watched a hill, a channel, a chain, a book, a chest, a slab of wax and a barrow cost about nineteen days between the four of us, and a piece of paper with three names in it cost a day, and the day was a Saturday in the last Saturday in a month, and the thing that was lost is not fixable by anybody in this basin, because what was lost was a Saturday, and a Saturday does not come back and it is not even a day that anybody wanted, it was the day you were going to read a mark in a wall that has not moved an eighth of an inch in six years.

"I am not going to be a moral about it. I have been the man who says the useful thing in a room twice this month and I have said both of them and I am not going to say a third, and I am not going to be a man who has been let down by paper, because I have been let down by a window on the fifteenth day of this month by a woman standing in a drain and I was not let down by her either, and the only thing I have been let down by in six weeks is a ground that went like a bench on Wednesday, and no form in this basin has anything to do with that."

---

"What I want," he said, "is for the two sets of hours to go in a book. Not in a speech. In the second book, in a column, with the date on it, and the column is to be headed something and it is to be a column and not a sentence, because a sentence is a thing a man says once and a column is a thing you can add a line to in November.

"And when somebody in about four years wants to know what the water of this Reach cost in a month, they can add up two columns. One of them is wages, and that one has not had a sum in it since about 1841, and it has had eleven days in it this month and eleven days is not nothing. And the other one is going to be days, and it is going to be four and a half and five and a half, and it is going to be whatever it is on the third of next month, and in about a year there is going to be a number at the bottom of that column, and it is going to be the only number anybody in this basin has ever had about what it costs to be looked after."

Perdie Ammid ruled a column in the second book on the Wednesday evening, on a page of its own, and headed it, and wrote two lines in it, and the heading is:

> *Days lost, and by whom, and to what. To be added to and not taken back.*

And the first two lines are the fourteenth of this month, four and a half hours, six people and a barrow; and the twenty-seventh of this month, five and a half hours, a barrow and a lath and a boy of fifteen.

And the third line is not ruled and is not filled and there is nothing at the bottom of it yet, and the column is going to be a page in a book in a village in about a month and it is going to be the first page in this basin of anything anybody has ever costed in days.

It was a Wednesday. The card was on the nail in the bookshop door with a third rod empty and a reason under it, and there was a fourth rod that existed on the back of a boy's hand, and the count was on the third of next month, and the card would be printed on the twenty-first of next month, and nobody in the Sallow Reach had asked the printer of Culm for anything at all, and the man in Culm was sixty-eight and had opened a locked drawer for nothing in the middle of a drought.
