# Chapter 0092 — The Space Under The Foot

It came back on the Thursday afternoon of the fifteenth, and it came back in four pieces, and three of them were signatures and one of them was not.

The boy of fifteen had gone down the lane at half past two on the Wednesday with the boy of nineteen and a sheet, and they had got as far as the toll bar by four o'clock and as far as Wyle by about six, and the man of sixty-one had read the whole of it standing in his own doorway and had not asked them a single question about water, and they had slept at Wyle in a hay barn and come back up the lane on the Thursday morning with the sheet, and it had three signatures on it and a fourth thing.

The fourth thing was written across the second space.

> *I will not sign this.*
>
> *Not because of what the flume is. I do not know what the flume is and I am one of the nine men who is being asked to say, and that is a proper thing to be asked and I have no complaint about it.*
>
> *Because the water comes off the last stone onto the Reach side of the long drain and there is eleven acres of beans on that side and it is mine and there is nobody to lend me a man, and what comes off the last stone runs in a trench across the top of it before it finds the drain, and there is no way of taking it off my ground again before the sixth of the month after next, and the ground went like a bench on the twenty-four of the month before this one, and the assize is older than every man in this valley and it is a plan for ever and not for this year, and a plan for ever is the thing that has turned eleven acres of beans into a field that is not sown.*
>
> *So put it in the book in my own hand and with the date on it, and I am not angry at anybody, and I have not been angry since about eleven o'clock this morning.*
>
> *And I will be at this village on the morning of the twentieth of this month, at eight o'clock, if anybody wants me there, and I will be there before the nine, and that is the second sentence of this fourth paragraph and it is not a signature and it is not a consent and it is a man saying he will be in a room.*
>
> *— A. Sarg, one of the nine.*

Perdie Ammid read it out in the shop on the Thursday afternoon with about eleven people in it, and read it twice, and then she entered it in the second book on a page of its own, in her own hand, with the date, and she did not read it a third time and she did not soften one word of it.

"Witnessed by eleven," she said, when she had done it. "Entered at four o'clock on the fifteenth. And I want the room to hear the arithmetic, because it is the whole of what that sheet has come back with.

"**Three signatures out of nine, in a day and a half.** That is a rate of about two a day off a lane of four miles. It took the sheet in the middle of the month before this one thirteen days to come back, and eight of nine refused and one signed, and the eight refusals are the sheet that made the nine of us today. And now three have signed inside a day and a half, and the three are Wyle, which signed on the second because it named the second, and Cold Ashton, which signed on the Wednesday morning in one sentence in a hand of about seventy, and Marl, which signed in a clerk's hand and **asked for a copy of the sheet for its own file.**

"And a clerk who wants a copy of a thing is a man who wants to be able to say what he agreed to in four years. **Marl is the only one of the nine that has ever asked for anything in writing in the history of this Reach**, and it asked for it in the same breath as it signed, and I have read that sentence about four times today and it has not stopped being the most professional thing anybody in this valley has done in a month."

"What about the four that would be offended," said Sef Ordry.

"They have sent nothing back," said Perdie Ammid. "And a man who has read a sheet and sent nothing back has not refused, and there is a difference between those two things that costs this Reach a great deal of money about twice a year, and I am not going to teach it to nine men at a table. **The four that would be offended will come. They will come because the sheet asked them to name a day, and a man who has been asked to name a day and has not named one has to either name one or explain himself, and he will pick the one that costs him less.**"

"And the man who has written across the space," said Jory Vant, from the wall, "has not named a day either."

"He has," said Perdie Ammid. "**He has refused to sign and named a day in the same hand.** He has named the twentieth of this month in the second sentence of the fourth paragraph, where nobody will look, and it is a Tuesday, and it is five days from now, and it is the only part of that sheet that lets anybody else meet. Write it at the top of the page, because in about four years there will be a clerk who will read a refusal and not read the date."

---

Then the boy of fifteen said the thing he had gone up the lane to find out and had waited a day to say, which was that a man of sixty-one at a toll bar had read the sheet standing in his own doorway and had asked him one question at the back of it, and the question was not about water.

"He asked me what the sheet wanted," said the boy of fifteen. "Not what the flume was. He asked me what the sheet wanted. And I said it wanted nine men in a room to say what a flume is. And he said: that is not a want, that is a question, and a man who puts a question on a sheet and signs his own name under it has asked two people to come, and one of them is the man who is asking."

"What did you say to that."

"I said nothing," said the boy of fifteen, "because I did not have anything, and I have thought about it since the toll bar and I have still not got anything, and I would like somebody in this room to notice that I have been carrying a sheet for a day and a half and the best thing anybody said to me on the whole road was said to a man who is not in this room."

---

Nesta Sarg came in at about half past four. She had been at the plank since seven in the morning and had done four planks, and she had a stub of chalk in her hand, and she asked for the refusal to be read to her because she had not been in the shop when it was read and she does not ask anybody to read anything twice if she can get in front of the third reading.

Perdie Ammid read it to her. It took about forty seconds.

When it was finished Nesta Sarg did not say anything for a while, and the room did what rooms do, and then she said one sentence, and it was to the room and not to anybody in it.

"**He has signed for himself and not for me, and he will go on signing for himself and not for me, and that is correct, and I am not in the assembly of the villages and there is no form in this basin that would put me in it, and a paper I have not been asked about is not mine to sign, and I would not sign it if I were asked.**"

Nobody in that room said anything about the four years. About four people wanted to and one of them was Perdie Ammid, and Perdie Ammid did not, and the reason she did not is on a page of the second book dated the sixth of the month before this one and it is that a woman of fifty-four learned in that room that the fitter's lesson is the fitter's and not hers, and she has not repeated it since.

"You will want it in the book," said Nesta Sarg.

"I will want it in the book," said Nesta Sarg, "and it will go in the book, and this is the thirteenth time I have said a sentence and not been thanked, and I have been counting them since the Saturday before last and I am counting this one, and I have been in a room of about eleven people twice this month and both times I was asked a question I could have answered in April. I am going back to a drain that is a foot and a half deep to put a mark on a shutter, and if anybody in this room wants to carry a pail they can come to Nether Sallow on Sunday, and there is no speaking about it."

Nobody came. Nobody said they would not either, and about four people in that room wanted to, and one of them was a boy of fifteen who has said since that he would have gone and did not, and gave no reason, and has not been asked.

---

On the Thursday evening, at about eight, the man who had come down the mountain on foot stood in the shop, and he said the thing that had been said to him three times this month and that he had not got an answer to.

"I am not going to be able to do this on the twenty-second."

"You have not said that it is impossible," said Sef Ordry. "You have said a thing with a different shape, and I want it in its own words before the room hears the other shape."

"It is not impossible," said Tarin Rusk. "It is four hours and six minutes of a six-inch stone launder with nine inches of fall in it and a crew of four at two ends and a padlock on an inspection cover that is not the gate. It is the most ordinary piece of work in this trade and any man in that shed could do it with a bar and a chain and a boy with a watch.

"**What I cannot do is be the one who opens it.** Not because of the water. Because of the third rod. The third rod on that card is empty and the reason under it is a padlock, and if a man in this valley puts a name at the bottom of that card on the twenty-second then in four years the card will read that the flume was run by him, and he will not be able to say no, because he will have been there, and in about eleven years there will be a room in this basin with a card in it and a name at the foot of it, and the name will be the one that is checked.

"I have spent a month learning that a name in a book in a village is the only thing in this basin that cannot be got out again. I did not learn it from a fitter of forty-six and I am not going to pretend I did. I learned it from a printed line on a form of a hundred and thirty years old with three men's names in it that none of them asked to be there."

"Who is going to open it," said Sef Ordry.

"Four men, and each of them asked, and one of them telling the other three what is happening before it happens and not after," said Tarin Rusk. "And I will be at the tank with a rule and a watch and a level staff and I will do nothing else at all, and that is the whole of what I have for it, and it is enough, because four hours and six minutes is not a thing one man does and the arithmetic of that has been on the table in this shop since the eighth of this month."

"You have not asked the four."

"I have asked nobody," said Tarin Rusk, "because it is Thursday night and the four of them are at four different ends of four different things and I will ask them tomorrow morning, one at a time, on a Saturday, in daylight, and if one of them says no then I will do a different thing, and there is one other Saturday in this month before the twenty-second, and the twenty-second is a Thursday. **And I have just heard myself say that. I have run out of Saturdays and I am going to ask four men tomorrow.**"

Perdie Ammid entered both of those sentences in the second book, the first one and the second one, and the second one is on a page of its own, and it is the third entry this month in the column headed *what it cost*, and the first two of them are not about water at all.

And on the Friday morning the sheet went up the lane again with a space at the foot of it, and on the Friday the four field books, the third book and the Guild's volume went to Vellum in a cart with a board across it and nothing else in it, and on the Friday the flume sluice was still shut and chained on a chain that had been on it for six years, and a man of thirty-one had bought a new chain on the Friday morning with his own money and had it in his coat and had not told anybody, because the chain that was on that gate was stiff and a chain that is stiff is a chain that gets a padlock put on an inspection cover instead of a bar into a staple and told the boy of fifteen on the Saturday because the boy of fifteen asked him a question about it and a man who has bought a thing with his own money does not want to be carrying it in his coat asking a boy about it.
