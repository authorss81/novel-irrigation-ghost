# Chapter 0099 — Eleven O'Clock

They went up the mountain on the Thursday evening of the twenty-second in about an hour and a half of daylight. About nine of them were going to do something and about nine more had walked up to watch, and the nine who were watching were sent home at eight o'clock, because there is no room on the coping of a tank that is not somebody's back.

The weather was the same as it had been for a month. The boy of fifteen had set out the level staff at about half past eight on a board across the coping, with the foot of it on the stone and not on the silt, because a level staff on silt reads whatever the silt feels like doing, and he had thought of that on the Sunday and had told nobody and had cut a board.

At nine o'clock the water was against the mark at two feet six and an eighth, and the boy of fifteen wrote it on his slate, and the date, and the time, and the word *first*, and read it out, because a slate that is not read out is a slate.

Sef Ordry was at the flume sluice, four hundred yards below the tank and round the corner of the embankment where you could not see the tank from it. He had a bar, a can of grease, a lamp, and the new chain, and the padlock was in his coat, and he had taken it off and put it on again once that afternoon and would take it off at half past ten, because a man who is going to open a gate at eleven o'clock does everything else before eleven o'clock.

Jory Vant was at the first pier with a rule, a lath cut to a point and a piece of chalk, and Wick Venn was at the last stone, two miles and four chains away, with a lamp and a barrow and no bar, because a bar is no use to him and he had not brought one and nobody had offered him one.

Nesta Sarg was at the plank, which is her plank, and had been at the plank since seven, and the man of thirty-eight with one hand was with her because the plank is where the figures are taken and somebody has to hold the watch.

Armist Sarg was in the low field with about eleven people and four lanterns, and he had put the seed into those eleven acres on the twenty-second of the month before this one, in three days, by hand, and the ground went like a bench on the twenty-fourth, and he was going to put the water in on them.

And Tarin Rusk stood on the coping of the tank with a folding rule in his coat and nothing in his hands, because a man of thirty-one had told him in a shed on the seventh of this month what a plate was for and he had not listened to any of it, and because there was nothing on the coping of that tank to do except read a staff and write a slate and be the only man in this basin who could be there.

---

At eleven o'clock exactly, four hundred yards away and out of sight round the corner of the embankment, a man of thirty-one put a bar into the slot of a gate that had been shut for six years and lifted it, and a chain came off, and a padlock came off an inspection cover, and the water went.

What it did was not dramatic. It went down a stone launder six inches wide that had had six years of a field growing into it, past three piers of which two had been cleared on Sunday, at about three-quarters of an inch deep, and it made the noise water makes when it has not made a noise for six years, which is neither a roar nor a whisper.

At the first pier a boy of nineteen put a rule in and got three-quarters of an inch, and chalked it, and put it in again a foot further down and got three-quarters of an inch, and wrote both on the back of his hand and said them out loud to nobody, because he had been told to say figures out loud and it did not matter what day it was.

At the plank at Nether Sallow, which is two miles above the flume's outfall and which the flume's water never comes back to, a man of thirty-eight with one hand held a watch at eleven o'clock and again at six minutes past three, and a woman of forty-one filled a pail and they did it twice each time, and the two trials came out at one minute fifty-two and one minute fifty-three in the first hour and one minute fifty-three and one minute fifty-four in the fourth, and the two figures she wrote on the shutter in chalk with a stub were a hundred and ninety-three and a hundred and ninety-one.


"That is the number," she said, out loud, on the coping, to about two people. "**The flume is two miles below this and it does not come up to me, and I have been standing here twice tonight instead of at eight in the morning, and the only thing that has changed on this plank since Saturday is me.**"

At the last stone the water came off the end of it at about twelve o'clock and went down four feet into the long drain with a noise that a man of thirty-eight heard from two miles above and who was not there, and it went into the drain and it went along the drain and it did not stop at the plank and it never comes near the nineteen houses, because the flume's outfall is two miles below the plank and the nineteen houses are four miles two chains from the head of the drain, and that has been on the page since the fifteenth of the month before this one.

And in the low field the water came along a trench about two feet wide and a foot and a half deep that a man of fifty-eight had complained of in writing, and the trench is about a hundred yards long and runs across the top of his eleven acres from the outfall side to the dyke, and it was dug in March with a spade and his own back, and the water went down it at about the speed of a man walking, and it did not spread, because a trench two feet wide in a blue clay does not spread, it goes along.

And at about eleven o'clock the boy of fifteen put his eye to the level staff on the coping of the tank and read it and said it out loud, and it was two feet six and an eighth, and it was not down at all.

Nobody said anything. He wrote it down with the time on it, and Tarin Rusk read it after him with a rule and it was the same, and the boy wrote the word *none* under it, which is a thing he had done before, on a card, and had been told off for, and had not stopped doing.

---

Then Tarin Rusk put his hand on the inside of the tank wall, which is coursed stone with a lime joint and a chisel mark cut in it by somebody in about 1843, and the wall was wet to about the height of the water, and something happened to him that he has not been able to describe to anybody in any way that satisfied him, and what he has said since, in a shed and in a yard and in three rooms, is this.

"I could feel where it was," he said. "Not see it. **All of it.** Two miles and four chains of a stone launder with a man in it at one end and a man in it at the other and a boy with a rule in the middle, and I could feel where the water was in every foot of it, and where the low places were, and where the two piers that had been cleared were, and where the one that had not been.

"And there is no name for that and I am not going to give it one, and I want to be careful here, because about nine people in this valley have spent a month telling me that a name is a thing that gets negotiated and not a thing that is given, and they are right, and I am not going to say a name tonight.

"**What I am going to say is that I did not do anything.** That is the whole of it. I stood on a coping with my hand on a wall and I did not push, and I did not pull, and I did not think about anything, and the water went where it was going because a man four hundred yards away lifted a bar and nine men in a room said a thing in their own words on Tuesday, and if either of those two had not happened the water would not have moved an inch.

"I have been in this valley sixty-one days trying to be the man who does the thing. I have not been that man tonight and I could not have been. **Four hours and six minutes is not a thing one man does, and that has been on the table in that shop since the eighth of this month, and tonight it was the only true sentence in this basin.**"

And then the man who had not slept more than about three hours in three nights, and who was on his sixty-first day in a valley, said the arithmetic out loud on the coping of a tank at half past eleven at night while the water went down a launder, because he had promised in a room that a price that is not told is not a price, it is a surprise, and he had been the cause of one of those this month.

"**The assize of this Reach at a fifth is five million four hundred and eighty-one thousand gallons.** That is what these villages say they want for a season, and it is two hundred and fifty years old and it is in a locked press in Culm in a hand that is a copy of a hand two centuries older.

"And the window is three thousand four hundred and eighty-five gallons. That is about one part in fifteen hundred of it. That is about three quarters of one household's day. **The window prevents one field failing and it does not prevent this Reach's season, and any man in this valley who tells you otherwise is selling something.**

"And the other number, which is worse, and which is on this coping and has not been said out loud at night. **Eight hundred and fifty gallons an hour for ten days over seventeen hundred acres is a hundred and twenty gallons an acre, and a physician stood in a yard in this valley in the middle of the month before this one and said out loud, in front of about nine people, that a hundred and twenty gallons an acre is not a crop.** She is right, and she is sixty-one miles east, and nobody in this valley has written to her since the fourth of the month before this one, and she is not thanked.

"And the last number, and it is the one that is going to be in a board in a city in four days. **The Sallow Dyke takes seventeen thousand six hundred and forty gallons a day out of the lowest field of this Reach, west, into the lower Sere, and so into Cresset, and that is about three hundred and ten days of the Reach's own season a year, and it has been doing it for six years.** That is a fifth of a day's worth going into a trench tonight. The dyke is below the flume's own outfall and water cannot be lifted. It is the lowest field's own drainage and the field is not drained without it. And it cannot be stopped, and it cannot be used, and it is not going to stop on the sixth of next month, and there is nothing anybody in this valley can do about it tonight or this month or this year.

"So that is the price. **I cannot measure what the other side of it costs nine hundred and forty properties sixty-one miles east, and I have been asked to say so in public and I have, and I am saying it again at midnight on a tank because there is nobody else up here.**"

---

Nothing happened between eleven o'clock and six minutes past three that anybody in this valley would have put in a book, and that is the best thing anybody has ever said about four hours and six minutes of this trade.

The boy of fifteen read the level staff at eleven o'clock, at midnight, at one, at two, and at six minutes past three, and the readings were two feet six and an eighth, two feet six inches, two feet five and three-quarters, two feet five and a half, and two feet five and an eighth. So the mark fell an inch in four hours and six minutes, and it had not finished falling, and it came back the same inch in about a week. **The whole of six years' variation had been an eighth of an inch, and the whole of one night was an inch, and there is no instrument in this basin that can say which of those two is the interesting one.** Every reading is on a slate in a boy's own hand with the time on it.

Wick Venn sat on a stone at the last stone with a lamp under his coat from midnight until the light came, which was about half past six, and at about two o'clock he had to say the thing he had said in a yard on the Saturday, which is that if that launder had put a stone into that field at two in the morning the only man in the party who could have done anything about it is him, and he cannot. He said it to himself, twice, and the second time out loud, and there was nobody within half a mile of him, and he has said since that saying it out loud was not a confession to anybody and was the only trade he had left. Nesta Sarg did the plank at eleven o'clock and again at six minutes past three and wrote both figures on her own shutter, a hundred and ninety-three and a hundred and ninety-one, and the two trials on each, and she did not divide them by anything.

And at six minutes past three in the morning on the Friday a man of thirty-one put a bar into the slot of a gate and brought it down, and the water did not stop.

It ran on for about an hour at the top and about three hours at the bottom, and there is no instrument in this basin that would tell anybody which of those two numbers is the right one, because a shut gate on a stone launder two miles and four chains long does not stop water. It stops putting more in at the head of it. What is already in the channel has a long way to go downhill before it has finished being water. And for three hours after a man of thirty-one had his hand on that gate, the whole of that launder was still running, with nobody at the middle of it and nobody at the bottom of it except one man with a lamp who could not do anything at all, and the man who knew where every foot of it was four hundred yards away at the top with his hand on the inside of a tank wall.

At about four o'clock the man at the last stone walked up the dry part of it to the third pier to see whether the man at the first pier was still there. He was. Neither of them said anything, and they walked back down.

And then a man of fifty-eight in the low field, who had walked the length of the trench in a foot of water and had put his hand in it twice and had said nothing, said one thing to about eleven people and then went home.

"It has stopped at the end of the trench," he said. "It has not gone over into the next field. It is standing about nine inches in a hundred yards of a trench two feet wide, and it is going nowhere, and it will be there in the morning, and the beans are in it, **and that is the whole of what a window is.**"

---

And then the man of thirty-one did the thing that ended the standing of four men, and he did it in about four minutes, and nobody stopped him and nobody argued and nobody thanked anybody.

He took the old chain off and put the new one on. The old chain is in the shed at the foot of the embankment and it took him about four minutes to get the staple, and he put the bolt through and dropped it and turned the key, and the key went in his own pocket, and the inspection cover's padlock went on after it.

"Before anybody says anything," he said, "I am going to say why, in about ninety words, and then I am going to say the other thing, and then this is over.

"A sluicewright leaves a gate shut and chained and padlocked on the cover. I found this one exactly so, and I have been the party having the care of that work for nine years and the company has not been up this road in six, and that is the only thing I know how to do.

"And I have put a new chain on it, and I want both of those facts in the record. The chain that was on that gate had been on it six years and it is stiff, and **a chain that is stiff on a gate is a chain that gets a padlock put on an inspection cover instead of a bar into a staple, and that padlock is printed under the third rod of a card at a culvert, and the third rod has been empty for six years because of it.** I bought the chain on Friday morning with my own money at a smith's four miles up the lane and I am not asking the company for a chain and I am not putting it in a book. It is a chain."

"A sheet of paper signed by eight men on Tuesday says one window, for one night, and that it does not survive the night. **The plate is not fitted and cannot be fitted until a keeper is named, and there is no keeper and there has not been one for six years, and the works is not on the company's schedule and the slot is a waste-pipe slot, and the declaration said in terms that no person is appointed by it to anything.** So there is nothing to appoint me to and I am not asking.

"And the last thing, and then I will stop. **Three thousand four hundred and eighty-five gallons went down that drain tonight, in a drought, with nineteen households at the bottom of it, and it did not reach them, and I am the man who put the chain back on and in about four years there is going to be a card on a post with a date on it and a chain on a gate with a date on it, and a clerk is going to lay the two out side by side, and he will be right to.** And I have said the third line of this about four times this month and I have never once said it for myself, and I have got to bed now."

Nobody said anything to him. That is the whole of what that room and that yard and that bank did between six minutes past three and half past three in the morning, and about nine people were on the mountain at that hour and none of them said one word to the man who had just put a chain on a gate.

And then the man of thirty-eight with one hand said the thing that nobody else was going to say for eleven years, out loud, on a bank, in the dark, to a man of thirty-one and to nobody.

"**Those two things are in a row and nobody is going to separate them.** Two hours and a quarter of holding a disc of red wax in an open hand on the fifteenth day of the month before this one, and a name in a printed line on the twenty-seventh of the month before this one, and a day given away out of eleven acres of beans on the tenth of this month, and a chain put on at about twenty-five past three in the morning tonight. That is four things and they are in a row and the row is not separated, and I have been writing a column for two months so that somebody in about four years could lay them out, and I did not write one word of that column for you, and I am not going to."

And Sef Ordry said: "Write the fifth one on Saturday," and Wick Venn said, "No," and Sef Ordry said, "Write it," and Wick Venn said, "No, and here is why, and it is the last thing I am going to say about it: **a day a man gives on purpose is a day he still owns. You are not taking it off me. You are writing down that I gave it, which is different, and you have been doing the second thing for two months and calling it the first, and the first thing is not available.**"

And nobody thanked him either.

---

At about half past four in the morning it was light enough to see the tank by, and the boy of fifteen took his slate to the coping and read the staff once more, and it was two feet five and an eighth, and he wrote it down with the time on it, and then he took the level staff off its board and stood it against the wall and looked at it for a while, because it was his and it had done the only thing anybody in this valley has wanted a thing to do for six years.

Tarin Rusk put his hand back on the inside of the wall.

He could still find it. That was the thing. The launder had been running for three hours after the gate came down and most of it was dry by now, and he put his hand on a coursed stone wall with a lime joint and a chisel mark cut in it by somebody in about 1843, and the wall was wet to about the height of the water, and he could find two miles and four chains of channel the way you find your own front door in the dark, and there was nothing in it, and there was no way of giving it back.

"Mr Rusk," said the boy of fifteen. "Are you all right."

"I am exactly all right," said Tarin Rusk. "That is the trouble. **Write that down and do not put it in a book for about a year.**"
