# Chapter 0052 — Five Shillings And A Cottage

The notice had come up the hill on the Monday in Niko Venn's satchel, and on the Wednesday the Bexhill mail came up behind it, and the Bexhill mail is not pinned to anything because the mail goes where the mail goes, and there is a difference between the two and it is the difference between a thing a man chose to send and a thing he had to.

It was a single side, headed in a different and older type, and it had been printed in Bexhill and it was a **Guild day rate** and every man in the blue steps had had one in his life.

> **Open Channel Guild. Day rates in force, second year.**
> *Labourer, four shillings.*
> *Sluicewright, five.*
> *Keeper, six.*
> *Inspector of high gates, six and eight.*
> *Foreman, seven and six.*
> *Master: by contract, and not on this sheet.*

Wick Venn read it standing in Marda Ollan's yard with about sixty people in it and he read it out twice, because the second time he read it with his thumb under the figures.

"Labourer, four," he said. "That is a man with a barrow and a hod and no skill that anybody has to pay for. And I have carried a barrow up a switchback at four shillings a day in the summer of this year, and I said at the time and I am saying it now that a shilling of it was a bonus I did not want."

"Nine years at the Reach was a shilling and six a day for six days," said Tarin Rusk, from the back.

"Nine years at the Reach was a shilling and six a day for six days." Wick Venn did not turn round. "And in the winter a man could get a quid, and a quid is thirty shillings, and a shilling and six a day for six days is nine shillings, and if a man worked seven days in the winter instead of six that is ten and six, and a man got a quid in the winter and I never worked a seventh day in my life because there was not a seventh day."

"So a winter week's worth of your own trade in the Reach was two days and a quarter of a Guild labourer's rate in this city," said Bel Crale. "Which is the sort of thing that is either an outrage or an explanation, and I have never been able to work out which, and Wick, I have listened to you say that number four times now and every time you say it like a man reading a price off a wall."

"Yes," said Wick Venn. "That is because it is a price off a wall."

And Marda Ollan wrote it in the district book, in her own hand, with the date, because she had been told since June that a thing said once in a yard is a thing somebody can deny on Thursday.

---

Five shillings a day, six days, is thirty shillings a week, which is a pound and ten, and fourteen weeks of a pound and ten is twenty-one pounds. Wick Venn did that sum in the yard and then did it again, because it is the kind of number that gets done wrong, and the second time he did it out loud twice in front of four people and got twenty-one both times, and then Bel Crale did it on her fingers and got twenty-one and said there, and the first person to have it wrong was Marda Ollan in her own yard that evening, who got thirty pounds, and who wrote *thirty pounds* in the district book and had to strike it on the following morning and write *twenty-one pounds* underneath it and initial the correction, which is the fourth correction in that book in two months and all four are in Marda Ollan's own hand.

"Thirty pounds," he said. "A cottage and a yard at no rent. And I have been in a shed at four o'clock this morning doing that same sum with a piece of chalk on a board, and I want to say the other half of it, because a yard is half a wage.

"A cottage and a yard at no rent is worth about four shillings a week in this city, and in the Reach it is worth nothing at all, because a man can live in a barn there for nothing, and so every cottage on that notice is a cottage that somebody is letting, and the somebody has an eye to the work, and the eye to the work is worth more than the rent."

"Which somebody is Perdie Ammid," said Tarin Rusk.

"Which somebody is Perdie Ammid," said Wick Venn, "and I am not saying a word against her and I will not have anybody in this yard say a word against her, and I have said that she wrote back and said no to me, and she said why, and the why was better than anything I have got."

---

A boy of nineteen in the coat of the second gatehouse came up the hill on the Wednesday morning with Tarin Rusk's leave application in his hand, and gave it back.

"It is not refused," the boy said, and then corrected himself, because he had been in the job a fortnight. "It is not refused, because there is nobody to refuse it. It went onto the list at a quarter past eleven on Saturday and this morning a man in a good coat took it off the list and read it and put it back and wrote on it in the corner, and he would not sign his name, and I am not to say what it says, so I am going to read it to you because you are the person it is about and you have the right to hear your own document."

> *Entered on the list, twentieth day of this month, and there being no officer of this office holding a seal, the same is not to be transmitted. It is to be held.*

"That is a very good note," said Tarin Rusk.

"I do not know if it is a good note. I have carried it up a hill."

"Then it is a good note, and so is the twenty-five-year-old who wrote it, and both of those things are a waste of a good clerk."

---

The Bexhill mail came up on the Wednesday afternoon with Wick Venn, which is to say it came up with a man who was going up to Bexhill anyway to see his brother and who had been given a letter to post, and it was a sealed letter of a kind this city does not produce: the flap had a Guild wafer on it, and the wafer had the master of the Guild's own device pressed into hot wax, and the address was not *Rusk* but *Tarin Rusk, in the lane called the blue steps, terrace road, city of Cresset*, which is the whole of the address and no number, because a post rider in that country asks at the arch.

Inside there were two things, and Marrow had put them in the right order.

The first was a **Guild recommendation**, on a printed form with a serial number, filled in in a hand that wrote the way a sluicewright writes: hard down, no loops, and very slightly too fast.

> *Recommended: TARIN RUSK, of no office.*
> *Trade as known to me: flow, heads, plates, surveys. He has opened a narrow branch three times on a route I did not build, with a witness each time and a record each time, and I have read the transcripts of two of them and I would rather this basin had more men like him and fewer like the men who sign the schedules.*
> *Reasons for recommending, from the articles: (1) his competence in flow, which I have seen; (2) his competence in records, which I have read; (3) his character, which I have tested in one particular way, namely that he refused me for four weeks on a ground that was better than the ground I had offered; (4) he is not a Guild man and will not pretend to be one.*
> *This recommendation is made by me and by me alone, at my own charge, under article nine, and it is not a favour and it does not carry the price of the other thing, and I will not make that sentence twice.*
> *Signed. IVEN MARROW, master.*

Underneath, in a different ink, because it had been added afterwards and not sealed:

> *This does not answer the application for leave. I have no power over any officer of Cresset and would not use one. What it does do is put my name on a piece of paper that says a man is competent, in case a man in a registry in a city four miles away is ever asked to make a decision and would like a second opinion from somebody who does not work for him.*

And the second thing was a sheet torn off the back of a working book, folded twice, and it had four things on it and no heading, and the first three were reasons and the fourth was not, and Marrow had numbered them, and the number four said *this one is not for you.*

> *1. Everything the notice asks for is in the counting-house at Vellum, and the counting-house is a day and a night of road, and a man who has never read the eleven thousand has to start at the first shelf and it takes him most of a winter. I would be selling you a route by not telling you that.*
> *2. A man who reads a plate and a head and a certificate and calls it a route has a very short career in this basin and it ends with him being asked by a room why his number does not agree with theirs, and the room is never wrong about a shelf mark.*
> *3. Five shillings a day is what a man of thirty-three is worth west of the Sere and it is what I would have offered him in a shed in my own yard, and if he does the work of four men he will be at six in a year and I will be the one who signs it, and I have four hundred works in this basin and no money.*
> *4. This one is not for you, and I am putting it on this paper because it is about the thing I have not been able to say to you in a room in the last four months, which is that I have been the master of this Guild for eleven years and I have never had the Sallow Head in front of the Guild, and there is a reason for that and the reason is that the reach has two records and a shelf mark and if a master of this Guild puts the reach in front of a guild meeting then a committee is formed, and a committee reads one volume, and the other one stays at Vellum in a drawer.*
>
> *That is not a rule. I have looked for it twice. It is a habit and it goes back about as far as the Guild itself.*
>
> *I have had a plate out of the Sallow Head and a new plate in it and a man of mine under the channel at the far end with a rod, and there is nothing coming, and I have held the reading card for the whole of the basin for six years, and I cannot put a number on the Sallow Head. Not an estimate. Not a guess. I have a hundred and forty men and I have sent eleven of them at that works in six years and I cannot put a number on it.*
>
> *If you go, you will be the man who puts a number on it, and you will do it in about a week, and I would like it on this paper that I sent you and that I did not know the answer and that I have not been to that works since the twenty-second day of the month before and was in a store there with a lamp and did not go and look at the intake.*
>
> *I am not asking you to tell anybody. I am asking you to know that there is a thing on this paper that is mine and not yours, and that when it comes up, it will be mine.*

Tarin Rusk read it twice, standing in the doorway of the room over the back stair, and folded it and put it into the bench book out of the shed at Bexhill, which is the only place in the world he had in which to put a piece of paper.

---

The carriage arrived on the Friday morning at four o'clock, and it was not a carriage, it was two flat carts and a wagon, and it was Tomas Ammid's boy with a pair of greys, and the wagon's near tire was a rope bound round a rim.

There were five other men on that road going west and three of them were the crew of the works.

The first was **Sef Ordry**, thirty-one, a sluicewright of nine years standing and the only man in the party who was not carrying anything, having put his own box on the second cart and having then, when a boy of nineteen had tried to put it on the wagon, taken it off again and put it back on the cart.

The second was **Margit Saye**, forty-six, who had a case of brass plates wrapped in oilcloth under her arm and who carried it the whole way herself.

The third was **Jory Vant**, nineteen, who came last and who was the only one of the five who had not been outside the foot of the mountain service road in his life, and who said so, once, at the toll bar, in a voice that could not be heard over the horses, and then did not say it again.

The fourth was Wick Venn, who was going home to a village he had not lived in for nine years and who had, in the end, decided to go on to the works, because there was a bed at the shed and a bed in the cottage and it made no sense to change beds twice in three days.

And the fifth was the passenger, who was a man of thirty-three with eleven pence, a bar, a folding rule, a chalk stub, a knuckle of string, a tin pot, a blue book, and a bench book, and who had no office and two live charges and a district of residence that was *none held*, and who slept in a cart that night between the second gatehouse and the arch, with his coat over his face and a bad shoulder on the uphill side.

Wick Venn rode the first cart, and Margit Saye rode the second, and the arrangement of the five of them across two carts and a wagon had been made in about ninety seconds at six o'clock that morning by a man of thirty-three who was not a Guild man and had been given the authority of the arrangement for the length of one journey, and who gave it back on the second evening without being asked to.
